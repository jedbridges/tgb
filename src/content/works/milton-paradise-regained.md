## Overview

About seventy pages, John Milton's brief epic in four books, published in 1671, the sequel to Paradise Lost, on the temptation of Christ in the wilderness. After his baptism, Jesus is led into the desert, where Satan, uncertain who he is, tempts him over forty days.

The temptations move through bread, wealth, the kingdoms of the world, the glory of Rome and the learning of Athens. Jesus refuses each in turn with quiet argument. Set on the pinnacle of the temple, he stands while Satan falls, and angels carry him back to his mother's house.

## What happens

**Jesus led into the wilderness.** Following his baptism, Jesus is led into the desert, where he will fast for forty days, entering the extended trial that occupies the poem's entire four-book structure and dramatic action.

**Satan's uncertainty about Jesus's identity.** Satan, genuinely uncertain who Jesus actually is, undertakes the temptations partly as an extended process of interrogation, testing different possible answers to this central question through each offer he makes.

**The temptation of bread.** Satan first tempts the fasting Jesus with bread, an offer to satisfy immediate physical hunger that Jesus refuses, establishing the pattern of quiet argumentative refusal that will structure every temptation to follow.

**Wealth, the kingdoms of the world, and Rome's glory.** Satan escalates through wealth, the kingdoms of the world, and finally the specific glory of imperial Rome, each temptation offering greater worldly power than the last for Jesus to refuse.

**The learning of Athens.** Satan tempts Jesus with the learning of Athens, classical philosophy and culture, which Jesus refuses alongside the more straightforwardly worldly temptations, staging a considered rejection of classical learning itself.

**The pinnacle of the temple.** Set on the pinnacle of the temple, Jesus stands firm against Satan's final test while Satan falls, and angels carry Jesus back to his mother's house in the poem's quiet, understated conclusion.

## How to read it

Read Milton's choice of the brief epic form, rather than extending Paradise Lost's cosmic scope, as itself an argument about heroism; quiet spiritual victory achieved through refusal in a wilderness matters more here than any external battle or grand narrative action.

Pay close attention to how Satan's genuine uncertainty about who Jesus is structures the entire poem; each temptation tests a different possible answer to this central question, turning the confrontation into extended interrogation rather than simple contest.

Notice how Jesus refuses Athens's learning alongside worldly power and wealth; this considered rejection of classical culture as insufficient beside revealed truth has generated centuries of debate about Milton's own complicated relationship to the classical learning his earlier poetry drew on extensively.

Consider the poem's quiet, argumentative mode, verbal refusal rather than physical combat, as offering a genuinely different model of heroic action than epic tradition typically celebrates, locating true victory in patient restraint rather than conquest.

Read this poem alongside Milton's own earlier Paradise Lost to see how its considerably more compressed, four-book structure and quieter dramatic mode contrast with that poem's cosmic scope, while both share Milton's central concern with obedience, temptation and the recovery of paradise.

## Questions it raises

- Does Milton's choice of the compact brief epic form for Christ's temptation, rather than an expansive narrative matching Paradise Lost's scope, successfully convey heroic significance through quiet refusal, or does the poem's restraint risk feeling anticlimactic after its predecessor?
- How does Satan's genuine uncertainty about who Jesus actually is shape your understanding of the temptations themselves? Does this uncertainty make Satan's escalating offers feel more like sincere interrogation or calculated strategy testing different approaches?
- What does Jesus's refusal of Athens's learning alongside worldly power and wealth suggest about Milton's own relationship to classical culture? Does this rejection sit comfortably alongside Milton's own extensive use of classical forms and allusions throughout his poetry?
- How persuasive is the poem's central claim that quiet verbal refusal constitutes genuine heroic action? Does this quieter mode of heroism offer a compelling alternative to epic tradition's usual celebration of physical combat and conquest?
- Given that this poem serves as sequel to Paradise Lost, how does its considerably more compressed structure and quieter tone relate to the earlier poem's cosmic scope? Does this formal contrast serve the different theological point each poem makes?
- What does the poem's understated conclusion, angels simply carrying Jesus back to his mother's house, suggest about Milton's vision of spiritual victory? Does this quiet ending appropriately match the temptations' own quiet, argumentative resolution throughout?
