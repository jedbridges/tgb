## Overview

About two hundred and ninety pages, a trilogy of plays by Eugene O'Neill, Homecoming, The Hunted and The Haunted, published in 1931, that transposes Aeschylus's Oresteia to New England at the end of the American Civil War. Brigadier General Ezra Mannon returns to his wife Christine, who has taken his cousin Adam Brant as a lover, and she poisons him.

Their daughter Lavinia, who loves her father and hates her mother, drives her brother Orin to kill Brant; Christine shoots herself; Orin, consumed by guilt, kills himself; and Lavinia shuts herself up alone in the Mannon house with the dead. The whole runs some five hours in performance.

## What happens

**Ezra Mannon's return.** Brigadier General Ezra Mannon returns home from the Civil War to his wife Christine, who has taken his own cousin Adam Brant as a lover during his prolonged absence, setting the trilogy's central betrayal in motion.

**Christine poisons Ezra.** Christine poisons her husband Ezra, adapting the opening murder of Aeschylus's Oresteia into O'Neill's New England setting and beginning the cascade of violence that will consume the entire Mannon family.

**Lavinia's hatred of her mother.** Lavinia, who loves her father and hates her mother with an intensity the play frames in explicitly psychological terms, begins working to expose Christine's guilt and avenge her father's murder.

**Lavinia drives Orin to kill Brant.** Lavinia manipulates her brother Orin into killing Adam Brant, adapting Electra's ancient role into specifically Freudian terms where family violence emerges from repressed desire rather than divine compulsion.

**Christine's suicide.** Christine, having lost both her lover and now facing her son's knowledge of her guilt, shoots herself, the second major death in the trilogy's escalating cycle of violence and its psychological consequences.

**Orin's suicide and Lavinia's isolation.** Orin, consumed by guilt over killing Brant, kills himself, and Lavinia, the trilogy's last survivor, shuts herself up alone in the Mannon house with the dead rather than face any continued public life.

## How to read it

Read O'Neill's transposition of the Oresteia to New England as stripping away the Greek original's divine machinery and eventual institutional resolution, replacing external gods and courts with purely psychological compulsion driving the Mannon family toward destruction from entirely within themselves.

Pay close attention to how Lavinia's characterization adapts Electra's ancient role into specifically Freudian terms; family violence here gets explained through repressed desire and displaced aggression rather than external divine curse or compulsion.

Notice how the trilogy's three-part structure mirrors the Oresteia's own three-play architecture while replacing its movement toward transformation and the founding of trial by jury with Lavinia's final self-imposed isolation, refusing comparable communal resolution.

Consider the trilogy's considerable scale, running some five hours in performance, which allows O'Neill to trace psychological deterioration across three full generations with a patience closer to the nineteenth-century novel than to conventional dramatic compression.

Read this trilogy alongside Aeschylus's own Oresteia to see directly how O'Neill's psychological, secular adaptation departs from and illuminates its classical source, particularly in how each work resolves, or refuses to resolve, its cycle of family violence.
