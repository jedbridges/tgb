## Overview

About three hundred pages, lectures Jacques Maritain gave at Santander in 1934, published in 1936 and translated in 1938 as True Humanism. Delivered while watching fascism, communism and liberal democracy compete for Europe's actual political future, Maritain identifies something these apparently opposed systems genuinely share, an anthropocentric humanism inherited from the Renaissance and Enlightenment.

He argues that the humanism of the Renaissance and Enlightenment, in placing man at the centre, ended in the anthropocentric collapse of liberalism, communism and fascism. Against them he proposes an integral or theocentric humanism and sketches a pluralist, personalist democracy inspired by Christian principles but not confessional, a new Christendom for the modern age.

## What happens

**Diagnosing anthropocentric humanism.** Maritain opens by identifying anthropocentric humanism, inherited from the Renaissance and Enlightenment and placing man rather than God at the center of meaning and value, as the shared foundation underlying three apparently opposed contemporary political systems.

**The collapse of liberalism, communism and fascism.** Maritain argues that this shared anthropocentric foundation produced the specific catastrophic failures of liberalism, communism and fascism despite their considerable surface differences, treating these three systems as variations on a single underlying error rather than genuinely distinct alternatives.

**Proposing integral or theocentric humanism.** Against this diagnosed shared failure, Maritain proposes integral or theocentric humanism as an alternative, refusing the false choice between secular modernity's genuine achievements and premodern religious authority's simple return.

**Preserving modernity's genuine achievements.** Maritain insists his proposed theocentric humanism could actually preserve modernity's real political gains, personal freedom, pluralism and political participation, while correcting the specifically anthropocentric error he holds responsible for contemporary political catastrophe.

**Sketching pluralist, personalist democracy.** Maritain sketches a pluralist, personalist democracy inspired by Christian principles but deliberately not confessional, proposing a genuinely novel political form distinct from both straightforward theocracy and purely secular liberal democracy.

**A new Christendom for the modern age.** Maritain calls his proposed political vision a new Christendom, explicitly distinguishing this modern proposal from any straightforward restoration of medieval Christendom's actual political and religious unity, a considered alternative rather than nostalgic return to premodern arrangement.

## How to read it

Read Maritain's diagnosis of shared anthropocentric failure across liberalism, communism and fascism as the lectures' genuine analytical achievement; identifying what these apparently opposed 1930s political systems actually share, rather than simply cataloguing their differences, gives the argument real diagnostic power.

Pay close attention to how carefully the proposed theocentric humanism avoids simply rejecting modernity's genuine achievements; Maritain insists personal freedom, pluralism and political participation can be preserved while correcting what he identifies as their specifically anthropocentric foundational error.

Notice how the sketched pluralist, personalist democracy deliberately avoids confessional establishment even while drawing on Christian principles; this specific combination, Christian inspiration without confessional political structure, is central to what makes Maritain's proposal genuinely novel rather than simply theocratic.

Consider the lectures' historical timing, delivered in 1934 before the Second World War had fully confirmed Maritain's diagnosis of where competing 1930s political ideologies were actually heading, as giving the work genuine prescient weight beyond its purely philosophical argument.

Read these lectures alongside Maritain's own Rights of Man and Natural Law to see how the broader personalist and theocentric framework developed here extends into the more specifically practical and legal domain of human rights theory in that later, more focused wartime essay.

## Questions it raises

- Does identifying a shared anthropocentric foundation across liberalism, communism and fascism successfully explain these systems' actual historical failures, or does this diagnosis risk flattening genuinely significant differences between them into a single overly broad philosophical category?
- How persuasive is Maritain's claim that theocentric humanism could preserve modernity's genuine achievements, personal freedom and pluralism, while correcting its anthropocentric error? Does this proposal offer a coherent political vision, or does it remain more aspirational sketch than fully worked-out program?
- What does the deliberate distinction between Christian inspiration and confessional political structure accomplish in Maritain's proposed pluralist democracy? Does this distinction successfully avoid the problems of both secular liberalism and straightforward theocracy, or does it create its own distinct difficulties?
- How does the lectures' historical timing, delivered in 1934 before the Second World War's full unfolding, shape how readers should evaluate Maritain's diagnosis? Does subsequent history validate his specific analysis, or did events develop in ways his framework did not fully anticipate?
- Given that Maritain proposes a new Christendom explicitly distinct from medieval Christendom's actual historical arrangement, what does this careful distinction suggest about his own understanding of how Christian political thought should adapt to genuinely modern political and social conditions?
- What does this work's continued relevance to contemporary debates about religion and secular political life suggest about the durability of Maritain's specific framework? Does his 1930s diagnosis and proposed alternative still offer useful resources for understanding similar tensions in more recent political contexts?
