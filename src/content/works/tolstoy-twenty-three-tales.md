## Overview

About three hundred pages, the Maudes' collection, assembled in 1906, of Tolstoy's short tales, written between 1872 and 1906 and most of them for peasant readers in the 1880s. It gathers the stories for children from his primer, the folk-style parables such as What Men Live By, Where Love Is God Is, and How Much Land Does a Man Need, the Ivan the Fool tale, and later pieces including Alyosha the Pot.

The tales are plain in language and turn on charity, humility, the vanity of wealth and the presence of God in ordinary life.

## What happens

**Stories for children.** The collection opens with the short pieces from Tolstoy's primer for peasant schools, God Sees the Truth but Waits among them, in which a merchant wrongly convicted of murder forgives the real killer in Siberia after twenty-six years.

**What Men Live By.** The shoemaker Simon takes in a naked stranger who is an angel sent to earth to learn three truths, and who learns over years in the workshop that men live not by care for themselves but by love.

**Where Love Is, God Is.** The cobbler Martin, promised in a dream that Christ will visit him, spends the day helping a street-sweeper, a poor mother and an apple-thief, and learns at evening that each was the visit.

**How Much Land Does a Man Need.** The peasant Pahom, who thinks he would not fear the devil himself if he had enough land, is offered all the ground he can walk round in a day among the Bashkirs, walks too far, and dies at the finish, needing six feet.

**Ivan the Fool.** The old devil sends three young devils to ruin three brothers, a soldier, a merchant and the fool Ivan, and fails with Ivan, who cannot be tempted because he only values work done with his hands, and whose kingdom has no use for money or armies.

**The later tales.** Alyosha the Pot, a servant boy who does everything asked of him and dies without complaint, and the other late pieces close the collection in the same plain key.

## How to read it

Read the plainness of the language as the point rather than a limitation, the style Tolstoy came to think the only honest one after renouncing the books that made him famous.

Pay close attention to how the lessons are enacted rather than stated, the angel learning in the workshop and the cobbler discovering his visitors, so that the tales carry a religion without preaching it.

Notice the shape of How Much Land Does a Man Need, the greed measured in footsteps and paid for in six feet, and ask why a story so simple has outlasted the creed it served.

Consider that the same writer who built War and Peace decided a story a child could follow was the higher achievement, and judge whether the tales bear him out.

Read these tales alongside the Gospel of Luke to see the parables Tolstoy was imitating, and to compare the shoemaker's angel with the good Samaritan.

## Questions it raises

- Are the tales the fulfilment of Tolstoy's art, as he thought, or a narrowing of it, and can a reader who loves the novels honestly prefer them?
- How much of their power comes from their plainness, and would the same lessons work in the language of Anna Karenina?
- Is Ivan the Fool's kingdom, without money or armies, a serious picture of how people might live, or a fable that only works because no one in it is clever?
- Why does How Much Land Does a Man Need strike readers who share none of Tolstoy's religion, and what is its lesson without the faith?
- What does it mean that the tales were written to be read aloud in cottages, and do they change when read silently by people who have opened many novels?
- Is Alyosha the Pot, who does everything asked of him and dies without complaint, a saint or a warning, and does Tolstoy know?
