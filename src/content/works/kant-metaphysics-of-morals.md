## Overview

About two hundred and eighty pages, Immanuel Kant's late systematic work, published in 1797, applying the abstract moral law worked out years earlier in the Groundwork and the second Critique to the concrete, practical business of actual law and personal ethical conduct. The work splits into two genuinely distinct doctrines, treating external and internal duty as fundamentally different in kind rather than merely degree.

The Doctrine of Right covers external freedom, property, contract, marriage, the state, punishment and international relations, arguing that a rightful condition requires an actual civil state and that the death penalty is just. The Doctrine of Virtue covers duties one has to oneself, such as not to lie or to serve, and to others, such as beneficence, gratitude and respect. Great Books volumes often print only the general introduction and the Doctrine of Right under the title The Science of Right.

## What happens

**External freedom and its foundation.** Kant grounds the Doctrine of Right in external freedom, the capacity to act without being constrained by another's arbitrary will, establishing this specific conception of freedom as the foundation for everything the doctrine subsequently develops regarding property, contract and political authority.

**Property and contract.** Kant addresses property and contract as mechanisms through which external freedom becomes concretely secured among individuals, working out the conditions under which a person can rightfully claim exclusive use of external objects and bind others through voluntary agreement.

**The necessity of the civil state.** Kant argues that a rightful condition among people requires an actual civil state possessing genuine coercive authority, since without such authority individuals remain in a condition where their external freedom cannot be adequately secured against others' arbitrary interference.

**Punishment and capital punishment.** Kant addresses punishment as a necessary function of the civil state, and defends the death penalty specifically as a strict requirement of retributive justice rather than merely a permissible policy choice, one of the work's most startling and frequently discussed conclusions.

**International relations.** The Doctrine of Right extends its framework to international relations, addressing how the same underlying logic of external freedom and rightful coercion might apply, or fail to apply cleanly, to relations between states rather than only between individuals within a single civil society.

**Duties to oneself and others.** The Doctrine of Virtue turns to duties no external authority can properly coerce, obligations to oneself, including not to lie or to demean oneself through servility, and to others, including beneficence, gratitude and respect, treating these as binding precisely because conscience alone can enforce them.

## How to read it

Read the sharp distinction between the Doctrine of Right and the Doctrine of Virtue as the work's central organizing structure; Kant treats external, coercively enforceable duty and internal, conscience-based duty as operating by genuinely different logics rather than as two degrees of the same underlying moral requirement.

Pay close attention to how Kant's defense of the death penalty as strict retributive justice, rather than merely permissible policy, follows from his broader framework in the Doctrine of Right; this conclusion has struck many subsequent readers as surprisingly severe given Kant's equally strong emphasis on human dignity elsewhere in his moral philosophy.

Notice how the Doctrine of Virtue's treatment of duties to oneself, particularly against lying and servility, connects human dignity directly to self-regarding obligation, extending Kant's moral framework into territory conventional ethical theory sometimes treats as merely personal rather than genuinely binding duty.

Consider how this work applies and extends the more abstract moral philosophy Kant developed earlier in the Groundwork and the second Critique; reading this later systematic application alongside those foundational texts reveals how Kant's abstract moral law translates into specific claims about property, punishment and virtue.

Read this work alongside Kant's own Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals and Critique of Practical Reason to trace how the same fundamental moral framework develops from its initial abstract statement through this later, considerably more concrete and systematic practical application.

## Questions it raises

- Does Kant's sharp distinction between the coercively enforceable Doctrine of Right and the internally binding Doctrine of Virtue hold up as a genuine philosophical distinction, or does treating external and internal duty as different in kind rather than degree create difficulties when these two dimensions of morality actually overlap in practice?
- How persuasive is Kant's defense of capital punishment as strict retributive justice rather than merely permissible policy? Does this conclusion follow necessarily from his broader moral framework, or does it reflect a specific historical and cultural assumption Kant did not adequately examine?
- What does Kant's argument that a rightful condition requires an actual civil state suggest about his view of individuals living outside such political structures? Does this argument adequately address how external freedom might be secured through means other than coercive state authority?
- How does the Doctrine of Virtue's treatment of duties to oneself, particularly against lying and servility, compare to more conventional ethical frameworks that focus primarily on duties toward others? Does grounding self-regarding obligation in human dignity provide a persuasive account of why these duties genuinely bind?
- Given that this work applies Kant's earlier, more abstract moral philosophy to concrete legal and ethical questions, how well does this application actually succeed? Do the specific conclusions, particularly regarding punishment, feel like natural extensions of Kant's foundational moral law or more contestable additions?
- What does the work's division into two distinct doctrines suggest about the proper relationship between law and personal morality more broadly? Does Kant's framework offer a persuasive account of where legitimate legal coercion should end and purely personal ethical obligation should begin?
