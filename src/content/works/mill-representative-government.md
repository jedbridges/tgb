## Overview

About two hundred and twenty pages, John Stuart Mill's treatise, published in 1861. Mill asks what form of government is best and answers that it is representative government, because it both protects people's interests and improves their character by giving them something to do.

He then works through the machinery: the proper business of an elected assembly, which is to watch and criticise rather than to administer; the case for extending the suffrage, including to women; his arguments for plural voting weighted by education and against the secret ballot; Hare's scheme of proportional representation; the relation of a second chamber and a permanent civil service to the elected house; federalism; and the government of dependencies.

## What happens

**The dual defense of representative government.** Mill opens by arguing that representative government is the best form of government, grounding this claim in a dual justification, that it both protects citizens' interests more reliably than alternative systems and improves their character by giving them meaningful political participation.

**The elected assembly's proper business.** Mill argues that an elected assembly's proper business is to watch and criticise government rather than to actually administer it, drawing a considered distinction between legislative oversight and executive competence.

**Extending the suffrage.** Mill makes the case for extending the suffrage considerably beyond its contemporary limits, including extending it specifically to women, connecting his broader theory of representative government directly to questions of who should actually participate in it.

**Plural voting and the secret ballot.** Mill argues for plural voting weighted by education, giving more educated citizens additional votes, and argues against the secret ballot, positions that reveal him working through genuine tensions within his broader liberal commitments to democratic equality.

**Hare's scheme of proportional representation.** Mill engages with Hare's scheme of proportional representation, considering how electoral mechanisms might better translate the actual distribution of political opinion into representative outcomes within an elected assembly.

**Second chamber, civil service, federalism and dependencies.** Mill extends his treatment to the relation of a second chamber and a permanent civil service to the elected house, and addresses federalism and the government of dependencies, working out representative government's practical institutional machinery with considerable specificity.

## How to read it

Read Mill's dual justification for representative government, protecting interests and improving character, as the treatise's genuine foundational argument; this combination treats political participation as valuable not merely instrumentally but as a form of moral and intellectual education in its own right.

Pay close attention to how the distinction between an assembly's proper oversight function and actual administration shaped subsequent thinking about representative institutions; this separation between legislative and executive competence remains influential well beyond Mill's own specific historical context.

Notice how Mill's specific positions on plural voting and the secret ballot sit in real tension with his broader liberal commitment to democratic equality; these are not simply consistent applications of a single principle but genuine attempts to balance competing values he took seriously.

Consider Mill's extended engagement with practical institutional machinery, proportional representation, federalism, colonial governance, as evidence of applied rather than purely abstract political philosophy; this specificity distinguishes the treatise from more purely theoretical accounts of representative government's underlying justification.

Read this treatise alongside Mill's own Subjection of Women to see how his argument here for extending suffrage to women connects to and anticipates the more extended, specifically focused argument for women's equality he would develop in that later, separate work.

## Questions it raises

- Does Mill's dual justification for representative government, protecting interests and improving character, successfully combine into a coherent single argument, or do these two justifications sometimes pull in different directions when considering specific institutional questions the treatise addresses?
- How persuasive is Mill's argument for plural voting weighted by education? Does this position represent a defensible attempt to balance democratic equality against concerns about governance quality, or does it risk undermining the very democratic principles his broader defense of representative government otherwise champions?
- What does Mill's opposition to the secret ballot suggest about his broader theory of political participation and civic virtue? Does requiring public voting genuinely serve his stated goals of character improvement, or does this position create tension with protecting voters from potential intimidation or social pressure?
- How does Mill's distinction between an assembly's proper oversight function and actual administration hold up against how representative governments have actually developed since his writing? Does this theoretical distinction match how legislative and executive functions genuinely operate in practice?
- Given that Mill argues for extending suffrage to women within this broader treatise on representative government generally, how does this specific argument relate to and anticipate his considerably more extended treatment of women's equality in his later Subjection of Women?
- What does Mill's extended engagement with practical institutional machinery, proportional representation schemes, federalism, colonial governance, suggest about his own understanding of political philosophy's proper scope? Does this attention to practical detail strengthen or potentially dilute his more purely theoretical arguments about representative government's underlying justification?
