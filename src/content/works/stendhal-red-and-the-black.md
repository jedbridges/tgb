## Overview

About five hundred pages, Stendhal's novel, published in 1830, a study of hypocrisy and class in 1830. Julien Sorel, a carpenter's son in a provincial town under the Restoration, admires Napoleon in secret and takes holy orders as the only route open to ambition. As tutor in the house of the mayor, Monsieur de Rênal, he seduces the mayor's wife.

Sent to a seminary and then to Paris as secretary to the Marquis de la Mole, he wins the marquis's proud daughter Mathilde. A letter from Madame de Rênal wrecks the marriage, and Julien shoots her in church, is tried and goes to the guillotine.

## What happens

**Julien's secret ambition.** Julien Sorel, a carpenter's son under the Restoration, admires Napoleon in secret while taking holy orders as the only route open to his considerable but socially constrained ambition.

**Tutoring in the Rênal household.** Julien becomes tutor in the house of the mayor, Monsieur de Rênal, a position bringing him into close proximity with the mayor's wife, whom he will soon seduce.

**The seduction of Madame de Rênal.** Julien seduces Madame de Rênal, calculated social climbing and genuine romantic feeling intertwining within his own psychology in ways moral judgment struggles to cleanly separate.

**The seminary and Paris.** Julien is sent to a seminary and then to Paris as secretary to the Marquis de la Mole, his path taking him from provincial obscurity toward the capital's highest social circles.

**Winning Mathilde de la Mole.** Julien wins the marquis's proud daughter Mathilde, demonstrating how thoroughly performed social confidence can succeed at the highest levels regardless of his own humble origins.

**The letter, the shooting and execution.** A letter from Madame de Rênal wrecks Julien's prospective marriage to Mathilde; he shoots her in church, is tried, and goes to the guillotine, ambition's collapse before love's uncalculated persistence.

## How to read it

Read Julien taking holy orders as the only route open to ambition, despite admiring Napoleon rather than genuinely holding religious vocation, as exposing the Restoration's specific structural corruption, legitimate paths to advancement so thoroughly closed.

Pay close attention to how his seduction of Madame de Rênal stages calculated social climbing and genuine romantic feeling as genuinely inseparable within his own psychology, ambition and authentic desire intertwining rather than remaining cleanly separable.

Notice how his winning of Mathilde de la Mole's proud affection in Paris demonstrates how thoroughly performed social confidence can succeed at the highest social levels regardless of his own actual humble carpenter's-son origins.

Consider how Julien shooting Madame de Rênal after her letter wrecks his prospective marriage stages ambition's catastrophic collapse when confronted with love's own irrational persistence, calculated climbing finally overwhelmed by authentic passion.

Stendhal's On Love, written just eight years earlier, supplies the very vocabulary of crystallisation this novel dramatizes across two distinct relationships, Julien idealizing first Madame de Rênal and then Mathilde in ways the essay had already analyzed.

## Questions it raises

- Does Julien's choice to take holy orders despite his actual irreligious Napoleon-admiring temperament successfully indict the Restoration's closed social structure, or does this calculated hypocrisy also implicate Julien himself in a genuine moral failure beyond mere social critique?
- How should a reader weigh Julien's seduction of Madame de Rênal as calculated social climbing against its apparent genuine romantic feeling? Does the novel successfully show these motives as inseparable, or does one ultimately dominate the other?
- What does Julien's success in winning Mathilde de la Mole despite his humble origins suggest about class boundaries' actual permeability during this period? Does his success reveal genuine social mobility, or does it depend on exceptional individual talent unavailable to most in his position?
- How persuasive is the novel's climactic violence, Julien shooting Madame de Rênal, as consequence of love overwhelming calculated ambition? Does this act feel like a plausible psychological outcome, or does it function more as symbolic dramatic necessity?
- Given the novel's explicit framing as a study of hypocrisy and class in 1830, how does Julien's specific individual story illuminate broader social and political conditions during the Restoration period in France?
- What does this novel's continued status as a foundational work of psychological realism suggest about the durability of Stendhal's specific achievement, combining social critique with unusually detailed attention to a single character's genuinely complex inner motivation?
