## Overview

About a hundred and fifty pages, Friedrich Schiller's last completed play, first performed in 1804, a drama in five acts about the legendary founding of the Swiss Confederation. Under the tyranny of the Habsburg governor Gessler, the men of three forest cantons swear an oath of resistance on the Rütli meadow.

The hunter Tell, who has kept apart from politics, refuses to bow to Gessler's hat on a pole, is forced to shoot an apple from his son's head, and later ambushes and kills Gessler on the road to Küssnacht. The cantons rise, the castles fall, and Tell is hailed as the liberator.

## What happens

**Gessler's tyranny established.** The play opens under the tyranny of the Habsburg governor Gessler, establishing the oppressive political conditions that will drive both the collective and individual resistance the drama traces across its five acts.

**The Rütli oath.** The men of three forest cantons swear an oath of resistance on the Rütli meadow, a collective founding ritual establishing organized political rebellion against Gessler's arbitrary authority.

**Tell's refusal to bow.** The hunter Tell, who has deliberately kept apart from politics, refuses to bow to Gessler's hat placed on a pole, a small but decisive act of individual defiance against demanded symbolic submission.

**The apple-shooting trial.** Gessler punishes Tell's refusal by forcing him to shoot an apple from his own son's head, transforming tyranny's cruelty into unforgettably concrete and personal form beyond abstract political grievance.

**Tell kills Gessler.** Tell later ambushes and kills Gessler on the road to Küssnacht, converting his personal grievance into decisive individual action against the tyrant who had subjected him to such cruel and arbitrary trial.

**The Confederation's founding.** The cantons rise, the castles fall, and Tell is hailed as the liberator, converging his personal act of vengeance with the collective political rebellion into the Swiss Confederation's actual legendary founding.

## How to read it

Read Tell's refusal to bow to Gessler's hat on a pole as staging resistance to tyranny beginning in a small, almost trivial refusal rather than dramatic organized rebellion, individual conscience asserting itself before any larger collective action forms.

Pay close attention to how forcing Tell to shoot an apple from his own son's head transforms Gessler's punishment into a genuinely sadistic test of parental love and steady nerve under absolute coercion, giving tyranny's cruelty unforgettably concrete form.

Notice how the oath sworn on the Rütli meadow gives collective resistance a specific founding ritual moment, distinguishing organized political rebellion from Tell's own more personal, apparently spontaneous confrontation with Gessler on the road.

Consider how Schiller's choice to dramatize the Confederation's founding as his own last completed play connects personal heroic action and collective political founding within a single unified dramatic structure, converging toward shared eventual liberation.

Machiavelli's Prince offers a useful contrast for this play's political imagination: where Machiavelli analyzes how power is actually seized and held, Schiller dramatizes legitimate resistance rising from a tyrant's own excess, individual defiance and collective oath converging on the same outcome.

## Questions it raises

- Does Tell's initially apolitical stance, keeping apart from the organized resistance until directly provoked, strengthen or weaken the play's argument about political resistance's proper origins? Does personal grievance provide more compelling motivation than collective ideological commitment?
- How does the apple-shooting trial's combination of public spectacle and intimate parental stakes shape your understanding of Gessler's tyranny specifically? Does this particular cruelty capture something essential about arbitrary power's actual character?
- What does the Rütli oath accomplish dramatically that Tell's individual confrontation with Gessler could not? Does the play suggest collective political organization and individual heroic action are equally necessary for successful resistance to tyranny?
- How should a reader weigh Tell's killing of Gessler as legitimate self-defense and communal liberation against its status as premeditated assassination? Does the play adequately justify this violence, or does it leave genuine moral tension unresolved?
- Given that Schiller wrote this as his last completed play, dramatizing a legendary national founding, what does this choice suggest about his own final artistic and political concerns near the end of his considerable career?
- What does this play's continued significance to Swiss national identity and broader European ideas about legitimate resistance suggest about the durability of Schiller's specific dramatic treatment of tyranny, individual conscience and collective political founding?
