## Overview

About one page, a single paragraph signed on 11 November 1620 aboard the Mayflower, anchored off Cape Cod, by forty-one of the men on board. Having landed outside the territory of their Virginia patent, the settlers, Separatists and others together, covenanted in the presence of God and one another to combine into a civil body politic and to enact and obey just and equal laws for the general good of the colony.

The original document is lost; the text is known only from William Bradford's later history of the Plymouth plantation, meaning modern readers encounter this foundational political document entirely through a subsequent historical account rather than any preserved original manuscript.

## What happens

**Landing outside the patent's territory.** The Mayflower's passengers find themselves anchored off Cape Cod, outside the territory their existing Virginia patent had legally authorized them to settle, creating a genuine practical and legal uncertainty about the actual basis for their intended colonial government.

**A religiously mixed group facing shared uncertainty.** The settlers aboard the ship, a religiously mixed group including Separatists seeking religious freedom and others who had joined the voyage for more ordinary economic reasons, face this shared legal uncertainty together regardless of their differing original motivations for the journey.

**Covenanting together.** Forty-one of the men sign the compact, covenanting in the presence of God and one another to combine into a civil body politic, a formal agreement addressing the practical gap their unexpected landing location had created in their original colonial authorization.

**Agreeing to just and equal laws.** The signers agree specifically to enact and obey just and equal laws for the general good of the colony, establishing the substantive commitment to self-governance that would structure the Plymouth colony's subsequent political development.

**Government by consent at a dateable moment.** This agreement establishes government by consent among the actual governed at a specific, documented moment, a genuinely foundational act of political self-organization occurring under conditions of practical necessity rather than prior theoretical commitment.

**Survival through Bradford's history.** The compact's original document does not survive, and its text is known only through William Bradford's later written history of the Plymouth plantation, an unusual preservation history for a document that would come to carry such considerable subsequent symbolic weight.

## How to read it

Read the compact's actual origin, a practical response to genuine legal uncertainty about landing outside their Virginia patent's territory, as more significant than any prior theoretical commitment to self-government the signers might be imagined to have carried with them from England.

Pay close attention to how explicitly religious the compact's language remains even while addressing a fundamentally practical political and legal problem; the settlers frame their civil covenant in the presence of God, blending religious and political commitment within a single brief document.

Notice how the compact's considerable brevity, a single paragraph rather than any elaborate constitutional structure, contrasts with the substantial symbolic weight later American political tradition would place upon it as a founding precedent for government by consent.

Consider the compact's unusual survival, known only through Bradford's later historical account rather than any preserved original, as relevant to how you weigh its exact original wording; modern readers encounter this document already mediated through a subsequent historical narrative rather than direct manuscript evidence.

Read this compact alongside the Declaration of Independence and the United States Constitution to trace a longer American tradition of founding documents establishing government by explicit consent, from this brief practical seventeenth-century covenant through the more elaborate eighteenth-century instruments that would eventually follow it.
