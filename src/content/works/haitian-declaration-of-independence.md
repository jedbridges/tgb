## Overview

About five pages, the Haitian Declaration of Independence, proclaimed at Gonaïves on 1 January 1804 following the defeat of Napoleon Bonaparte's invasion force, drafted for Jean-Jacques Dessalines by his secretary Louis Boisrond-Tonnerre after Dessalines reportedly rejected an earlier, more measured draft and demanded a document written, as later accounts put it, in blood rather than ink, with a skin used for parchment and a bayonet for a pen.

The declaration renounces France forever, recalls the specific cruelties of slavery and the recent war of independence in considerable and unsparing detail, and swears the assembled generals and people to die rather than ever again submit to French rule, before formally naming the new nation Haiti, recovering the island's indigenous Taino name in place of any designation associated with French colonial Saint-Domingue.

## What happens

**Following military victory.** The declaration follows directly from Haiti's decisive defeat of the large French invasion force Napoleon had dispatched in 1802, an expedition that had initially captured Toussaint Louverture but ultimately failed to restore French colonial control or reinstate slavery against the sustained resistance of Dessalines and his fellow generals.

**Dessalines's demand for a fiercer text.** According to accounts of its composition, Dessalines rejected an earlier, more measured draft of the declaration and instructed Boisrond-Tonnerre to write a document with genuine fury behind it, reportedly demanding blood rather than ink, skin rather than parchment, and a bayonet rather than a pen, a demand the surviving text's unsparing anger reflects directly.

**Renouncing France forever.** The declaration renounces France in absolute terms, recalling the specific cruelties of slavery and the recent war in considerable detail rather than reasoning abstractly through political philosophy before listing grievances, a structural choice that keeps the document's fury directly grounded in remembered, specific atrocity throughout.

**The oath to die rather than submit.** The declaration's climactic passage has Dessalines and the assembled generals swear collectively to die rather than ever again live under French domination, binding the new nation's leadership to permanent, unconditional resistance against any future attempt at reconquest, an oath considerably more absolute than typical declarations of political independence.

**Naming the nation Haiti.** The declaration formally names the new nation Haiti, recovering the island's original indigenous Taino name in place of Saint-Domingue, the name associated with French colonial rule, making the document's political break from France symbolically as complete as the military and political break the revolution had already achieved on the battlefield.

## How to read it

Read the declaration's considerably angrier tone, compared with the American Declaration of Independence it is often set alongside, as a direct consequence of what it is actually responding to; Jefferson's document reasons through abstract political philosophy before listing grievances against a distant king, while Boisrond-Tonnerre's writes from the immediate memory of slavery and an ongoing colonial war against an entire nation.

Pay close attention to how specifically the declaration names France itself, rather than any individual monarch or minister, as the object of the new nation's enduring hatred, a choice that insists genuine justice for slavery and colonial violence requires holding an entire colonial power accountable rather than isolating blame to particular rulers who might simply be replaced.

Notice how the declaration's renaming of the nation as Haiti functions as an act of symbolic decolonization running alongside its political declaration, erasing French colonial naming in the same founding gesture that erases French colonial governance.

Consider the reported circumstances of the declaration's composition, Dessalines demanding a fiercer document after rejecting an initial measured draft, as relevant to how you read the text's unusual intensity; this was a document deliberately shaped to match the actual anger and urgency of its historical moment rather than softened for diplomatic effect.

Read this declaration alongside Louverture's 1801 constitution and Dessalines's own 1805 constitution to trace the Haitian Revolution's full arc from careful strategic ambiguity about French sovereignty, through this unambiguous 1804 declaration of independence, to the fuller constitutional refounding of the state the following year.

- What does the reported circumstance of the declaration's composition, Dessalines demanding a fiercer document written essentially in blood and violence as its own metaphor, suggest about the relationship between a founding document's actual content and the specific historical trauma it emerges from?
- How does renaming the nation Haiti, recovering an indigenous name erased by centuries of French colonial rule, function differently from the political and military declarations that surround it in the same document? Does this act of naming accomplish something the declaration's more explicitly political language cannot?
- Given how frequently this declaration is compared with the American Declaration of Independence, what does the comparison actually reveal, and what does it obscure, about the fundamentally different circumstances, an ongoing war against actual re-enslavement rather than a dispute over taxation and representation, that produced each document?
- Why do you think this declaration remains considerably less widely taught and referenced than the American or French declarations of the same revolutionary era, despite founding the first nation to achieve independence through a successful slave revolt?
