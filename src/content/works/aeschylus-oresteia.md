## Overview

*The Oresteia* is three plays meant to be seen in one day, and it is the only Greek trilogy that survives entire. A satyr play, *Proteus*, followed it and is lost. Aeschylus won first prize with it in 458 BCE, two years before his death, and it was performed in a theatre holding many thousands of Athenian citizens who had recently given juries of ordinary men the power to decide capital cases.

The three parts get progressively shorter, faster and more public. *Agamemnon* is the longest and the strangest, dominated by choral odes that circle the past instead of advancing the plot; the murder is over in minutes and the play is mostly dread. *The Libation Bearers* is a revenge play with recognition, disguise and a false messenger, tightly plotted. *The Eumenides* leaves the palace entirely, moves from Delphi to Athens, and ends in procession and song, with the Furies in new robes and the audience addressed as the city.

The imagery links them. Nets, blood soaking into ground, a beacon chain, a snake in the house, light after dark: Aeschylus repeats images across plays so that a phrase in the third recalls a scene in the first.

## What happens

*Agamemnon.* A watchman sees the beacon that means Troy has fallen after ten years. Clytemnestra has ruled Argos in her husband's absence and has taken his cousin Aegisthus as her lover. The chorus of old men recalls the fleet becalmed at Aulis and Agamemnon's decision to sacrifice his daughter Iphigenia for a favourable wind. The king arrives in triumph with the Trojan princess Cassandra as his prize. Clytemnestra welcomes him with a speech of enormous false warmth and spreads cloths for him to walk on. Cassandra, given prophecy by Apollo and cursed never to be believed, sees the killing before it happens, describes the house's history of slaughtered children, and goes in to die. Agamemnon is trapped in a robe and struck in his bath. Clytemnestra comes out with the body and defends herself as the instrument of justice for her child. Aegisthus arrives to gloat. The chorus warns that Orestes will come.

*The Libation Bearers.* Years later, Orestes returns from exile with his friend Pylades and lays a lock of hair on his father's grave. Electra, sent by their mother to pour offerings after a bad dream, finds it. Brother and sister recognise each other and, with the chorus, call on their father's spirit in a long ritual lament. Orestes enters the palace disguised as a stranger bringing news of his own death, kills Aegisthus, and then faces his mother, who bares her breast and reminds him who fed him. Pylades speaks one line, recalling Apollo's command, and Orestes kills her. The Furies appear to him at once, visible to no one else, and he runs.

*The Eumenides.* At Delphi the Furies sleep and the ghost of Clytemnestra wakes them. Apollo sends Orestes to Athens; the Furies follow, claiming him as a mother-killer. Athena refuses to judge the case alone and empanels a jury of Athenians on the Areopagus. The Furies prosecute, Apollo defends with an argument about paternity that is almost embarrassing, and Orestes is asked directly whether he did it. He says he did. The votes come out even. Athena's vote acquits. The Furies threaten to blight the land, and Athena, over several hundred lines, offers them permanent honour and a home beneath the city instead. They accept, become the Kindly Ones, and the trilogy ends in torchlight.

## How to read it

Read all three in a week if you can, and *Agamemnon* twice. The first play rewards rereading more than any other Greek tragedy, because on a second pass the choral odes stop being obstacles and become the argument.

The hardest stretch for most readers is the parodos of *Agamemnon*, the long entrance ode about the eagles, the hare and Aulis. Do not look for a plot in it. It is establishing that this family's disasters are a pattern, and that the pattern includes choices.

Track who claims to be an agent of justice. Agamemnon at Troy, Clytemnestra over the body, Orestes at his mother's throat, the Furies, Apollo, Athena. Each has a real claim. The trilogy's point is that having a real claim is exactly the problem.

Notice what the trial does not settle. The jury deadlocks. Apollo's argument about mothers is bad and everyone in the theatre knew it. Orestes is acquitted by procedure, not by proof of innocence, and the Furies are compensated rather than refuted. Whether that counts as progress is the question Aeschylus leaves open.

Fagles is the usual assignment and the easiest to read aloud. Lattimore is the one to have open beside it if you are writing about the net imagery or the language of the choral odes, because he preserves the difficulty rather than smoothing it.

## Questions it raises

- Is Clytemnestra's case against Agamemnon good? Does the trilogy ever answer it, or only outvote it?
- Orestes obeys a god and is polluted anyway. What does that say about the relation between divine command and guilt?
- The jury ties and a goddess breaks it. Does that make the court's authority stronger or weaker?
- Why must the Furies be honoured rather than defeated? What are they for once law exists?
- Cassandra is believed by no one and is right about everything. What is she doing in the design of the play?
- The trilogy was staged for men who served on juries. Is it a defence of Athenian institutions or a warning about what they cost?
