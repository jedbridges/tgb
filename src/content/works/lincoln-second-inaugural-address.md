## Overview

About two pages, Abraham Lincoln's second inaugural address, delivered on 4 March 1865, six weeks before his assassination and with the Civil War nearly won. This speech of some seven hundred words declines to celebrate, marking its considerable strangeness among victory addresses, choosing instead a theological reckoning with the war's actual cause and cost.

Lincoln recalls that both sides read the same Bible and prayed to the same God, names slavery as the cause of the war, and suggests that God may be exacting from the whole nation, North and South, payment for the wealth piled up by two hundred and fifty years of unrequited toil. He closes by urging the country to finish the work with malice toward none and charity for all.

## What happens

**Declining to celebrate.** Lincoln opens by explicitly declining the triumphant celebration a victory address might ordinarily offer, choosing instead a more measured, reflective tone despite the war's nearly certain favorable outcome for the Union by this point.

**Both sides praying to the same God.** Lincoln observes that both sides in the war read the same Bible and prayed to the same God, each invoking divine aid against the other, refusing to let Northern listeners hear the war's outcome as simple vindication of one side's righteousness over the other's wickedness.

**Naming slavery as the cause.** Lincoln names slavery directly as the actual cause of the war, stating plainly what years of political caution had kept him from asserting so directly, a clear historical judgment delivered without equivocation at this late stage of the conflict.

**God's judgment on the whole nation.** Lincoln suggests that God may be exacting from the whole nation, North and South together, payment for the wealth piled up by two hundred and fifty years of unrequited toil, extending moral responsibility for slavery beyond the Confederacy alone to the entire nation's shared economic history.

**The closing charge.** Lincoln closes by urging the country to finish the work it is in, to bind up the nation's wounds, and to act with malice toward none and charity for all, a reconciliation grounded in the speech's preceding argument about shared national responsibility.

## How to read it

Read Lincoln's refusal to celebrate as the speech's genuine and deliberate rhetorical choice rather than mere modesty; declining triumphant language at the exact moment military victory seemed near required real restraint, and this restraint shapes everything the speech subsequently argues about the war's meaning.

Pay close attention to how the observation that both sides prayed to the same God functions to complicate rather than simplify the war's moral meaning; Lincoln refuses the comfortable framing that would let Northern listeners hear their victory as uncomplicated divine vindication against a purely wicked opponent.

Notice how directly naming slavery as the war's cause, after years of more cautious public language, changes the speech's rhetorical stakes; this plain historical judgment, delivered so late in the conflict, carries genuine weight precisely because Lincoln had previously avoided stating it with comparable directness.

Consider the suggestion that God may be exacting payment from the whole nation as the speech's most theologically daring move; extending responsibility for slavery beyond the Confederacy to the entire nation's shared economic history reframes the war's cost as shared national judgment rather than one-sided punishment.

Read this address alongside Lincoln's own broader body of speeches collected in Selected Speeches to see how this final, theologically reflective statement relates to and culminates the political arguments about slavery, union and equality he had been developing across his entire public career.

- How does the observation that both sides prayed to the same God change the speech's overall argument about the war's moral meaning? Does this observation genuinely complicate simple moral judgment, or does Lincoln's subsequent naming of slavery as the cause ultimately restore a clear moral verdict regardless?
- What does the suggestion that God may be exacting payment from the whole nation, rather than the Confederacy alone, accomplish rhetorically and theologically? Does extending shared responsibility to the North risk understating the specific, concentrated moral culpability of those who actually practiced slavery?
