## Overview

About twenty pages, a poem of roughly four hundred and thirty lines composed in heroic couplets by Oliver Goldsmith, published in 1770, addressed directly to Auburn, an idealized English village drawn substantially from Goldsmith's own memories of childhood, though scholars have long debated exactly how much the poem's specific setting reflects Goldsmith's native Ireland, his later English experience, or a deliberately composite imagined landscape. The poem's dedication to the painter Joshua Reynolds insists directly and pointedly that the depopulation the poem describes reflects genuine, observed social reality rather than merely poetic invention or nostalgic exaggeration, a claim that framed the work from its first publication as social protest and documentary witness as much as personal elegy.

The poem's structure enacts directly the loss it describes, moving across its considerable length from loving, richly specific memory of Auburn's former daily life and community in its first half toward a starker, more explicitly political second half addressing the village's actual depopulation, identifying a single mechanism, the enclosure of common agricultural land by a wealthy landowner, as the specific economic cause of the community's destruction.

## What happens

**Remembering Auburn.** The poem's first half recalls, in loving and specific detail, Auburn's former daily community life, its parson, its schoolmaster, its village alehouse, and the evening games and gatherings that once filled the village green, establishing through accumulated concrete detail exactly what kind of settled, functioning community the poem's second half will describe as having been destroyed.

**The enclosure.** Goldsmith identifies the specific cause of Auburn's depopulation directly: a single wealthy landowner has enclosed the common agricultural land that had once supported numerous small farming families, converting land previously shared among the village's many residents into private pleasure grounds serving that single owner's leisure and aesthetic preference rather than the community's continued subsistence.

**Exile and dispersal.** The poem traces the scattered former villagers' actual departure following this enclosure, some driven toward the considerable poverty and moral hazard of city life, others crossing the ocean entirely to seek new lives in America, transforming what had been a settled, multigenerational rural community into a genuine diaspora scattered across continents.

**The departure of Poetry.** The poem closes with a striking personification, Poetry herself departing England entirely for wilder, less civilized shores, a closing image suggesting that the same forces of luxury and concentrated wealth responsible for Auburn's destruction are simultaneously destroying the broader cultural and social conditions that had once sustained genuine poetic and artistic culture within England itself.

## How to read it

Read the poem's dedication to Reynolds, insisting on the factual reality of the depopulation described, as establishing the work's genuine claim to social documentary alongside its more purely elegiac and nostalgic register, a claim worth taking seriously even where the specific historical accuracy of Goldsmith's own account remains genuinely debated by scholars.

Pay close attention to how the poem's formal structure, moving from detailed positive memory toward starker loss and dispersal, itself enacts the depopulation it describes, giving the poem's argument a formal as well as purely thematic dimension worth tracking deliberately across its considerable length.

Notice how specifically Goldsmith identifies enclosure, a concrete economic and legal mechanism, as Auburn's actual cause of destruction, rather than leaving the village's decline as vaguely attributed nostalgic loss; this specificity gives the poem real political and economic argument alongside its more purely emotional register.

Consider the poem's closing personification of Poetry's departure as extending its critique beyond rural community life specifically into a broader claim about luxury's corrosive effect on cultural and artistic conditions generally, connecting the poem's particular social observation to considerably larger claims about civilization's own trajectory.

Read this poem alongside Thomas Gray's Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard, another major eighteenth-century poem meditating on rural life, obscurity and loss, to see how differently two contemporary poets treated related concerns about rural community, memory and mortality within the same broader poetic tradition.

- What does identifying enclosure specifically, rather than more general historical or economic change, as Auburn's cause of destruction suggest about Goldsmith's own understanding of who bears responsibility for rural community's decline? Does this specific attribution hold up as accurate social and economic analysis?
- How does the poem's closing personification of Poetry's departure connect its particular observation about one village's destruction to a broader claim about luxury and civilization? Does this closing move feel like a natural extension of the poem's argument or an overreaching final gesture?
- Given ongoing scholarly debate about how much the poem's Auburn reflects Goldsmith's native Ireland, his English experience, or an entirely composite imagined landscape, how should a reader weigh the poem's claimed factual basis against its evident degree of poetic idealization and construction?
- What does the poem's enduring popularity and continued relevance to discussions of rural depopulation and economic displacement suggest about how effectively eighteenth-century poetic elegy can speak to considerably later readers facing structurally similar, if historically distinct, experiences of community loss and economic displacement?
