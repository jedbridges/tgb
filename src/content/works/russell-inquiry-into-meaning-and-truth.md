## Overview

About three hundred and fifty pages, Bertrand Russell's treatise based on his William James Lectures at Harvard, published in 1940. The book asks what it means for a sentence to be true and how language connects to experience. It works upward from object-words learned by direct confrontation with things, through sentences describing experience, to logical words, egocentric particulars such as this and now, and the basic propositions on which empirical knowledge rests.

Russell defends a correspondence theory of truth against the verificationism of the logical positivists, and argues that truth must be allowed to outrun what we can know.

## What happens

**Object-words and direct confrontation.** Russell begins with object-words, terms learned through direct confrontation with things themselves, establishing the most concrete foundation for his systematic ascent toward increasingly abstract linguistic and logical vocabulary.

**Sentences describing experience.** Russell moves to sentences describing experience, examining how individual object-words combine into more complex linguistic structures capable of expressing genuine propositions about the world.

**Logical words and egocentric particulars.** Russell addresses logical words and egocentric particulars such as this and now, terms whose meaning depends crucially on the speaker's own immediate perspective and situation within experience.

**Basic propositions of empirical knowledge.** Russell identifies the basic propositions on which empirical knowledge ultimately rests, completing his systematic account of how language's most abstract elements connect back to concrete experiential foundation.

**Defending correspondence theory.** Russell defends a correspondence theory of truth, arguing genuine truth requires actual correspondence between statement and fact rather than reducing to available verification procedures alone.

**Arguing truth outruns knowability.** Russell argues that truth must be allowed to outrun what we can know, insisting statements can be genuinely true or false independent of our actual capacity to confirm them.

## How to read it

Read Russell's decision to work systematically upward from object-words learned by direct confrontation with things toward increasingly abstract logical vocabulary as giving meaning's foundation a genuinely empirical grounding, built from the ground up rather than assumed secure.

Pay close attention to how the defense of a correspondence theory of truth against the logical positivists' verificationism stakes out a considered position in analytic philosophy's central methodological disputes, insisting truth requires genuine correspondence to fact.

Notice how the argument that truth must be allowed to outrun what we can know challenges verificationism's core commitment directly, insisting statements can be genuinely true or false independent of our actual capacity to confirm them.

Consider that delivered as the William James Lectures at Harvard, the book connects British analytic philosophy's technical concerns directly to the American philosophical tradition James himself had helped establish.

The technical machinery built here, from object-words up through basic propositions, underwrites the far broader epistemological project Russell would undertake eight years later in Human Knowledge, where these same foundational questions get tested against the specific problem of scientific justification.

## Questions it raises

- Does Russell's systematic ascent from object-words learned by direct confrontation to increasingly abstract logical vocabulary successfully establish meaning's empirical foundation, or does this construction smuggle in assumptions about direct confrontation's own reliability that require further justification?
- How persuasive is Russell's defense of correspondence theory against verificationism? Does insisting truth requires genuine correspondence to fact, independent of verification procedures, adequately answer the logical positivists' concerns about meaningful versus meaningless statements?
- What does the argument that truth must outrun what we can know accomplish philosophically? Does this position successfully preserve a robust notion of objective truth, or does it risk making truth claims unfalsifiable and therefore philosophically problematic in their own way?
- How does treating egocentric particulars like this and now as a distinct category shape Russell's broader account of how language connects to experience? Does this treatment adequately capture how such indexical terms actually function in ordinary communication?
- Given that these lectures were delivered at Harvard as the William James Lectures, how does this specific American philosophical context shape Russell's argument? Does his engagement with pragmatist concerns visibly inform his own analytic treatment of meaning and truth?
- What does this book's continued relevance to philosophy of language debates suggest about the durability of Russell's specific framework? Do contemporary theories of meaning and truth still engage productively with his systematic ascent from experience to abstract logical vocabulary?
