## Overview

About a hundred and forty pages, George Bernard Shaw's comedy, written in 1912, staged in 1913 and published in 1916. Henry Higgins, a professor of phonetics, bets Colonel Pickering that in six months he can pass off Eliza Doolittle, a Covent Garden flower girl, as a duchess by teaching her to speak. He succeeds at an ambassador's party, then treats the triumph as his own and Eliza as a finished experiment.

She walks out and confronts him with the question of what she is to become now that she is fit for neither her old life nor his. The play ends without the marriage the audience expects, and Shaw wrote a sequel insisting on the point.

## What happens

**Higgins's wager with Pickering.** Henry Higgins, a professor of phonetics, bets Colonel Pickering that within six months he can pass off Eliza Doolittle, a Covent Garden flower girl, as a duchess purely by teaching her to speak differently.

**Eliza's speech training.** Eliza undergoes intensive speech training under Higgins, a rigorous process transforming her linguistic presentation while raising genuine questions about what, if anything, this transformation actually changes about her.

**Success at the ambassador's party.** Higgins succeeds at an ambassador's party, passing Eliza off convincingly as a duchess and winning his wager with Pickering through her successfully performed transformation.

**Higgins claims the triumph.** Higgins treats the resulting triumph as entirely his own personal achievement, treating Eliza as a finished experiment rather than acknowledging her as an actual person who underwent this considerable transformation.

**Eliza's confrontation.** Eliza walks out and confronts Higgins directly, demanding to know what she is now to become, since she remains fit for neither her old working-class life nor his own privileged social world.

**The unmarried ending.** The play ends without the marriage audiences conventionally expect between Higgins and Eliza, a deliberate refusal Shaw reinforced with an insistent prose sequel explaining precisely why this outcome makes sense.

## How to read it

Read Higgins's wager with Pickering as treating Eliza's transformation as pure scientific experiment, staging class itself as fundamentally performable through learned speech rather than fixed by birth, a genuinely radical social claim.

Pay close attention to how Higgins treating his own triumph as personal achievement while treating Eliza as a finished experiment exposes how thoroughly intellectual accomplishment can blind its own author to the actual person that accomplishment was performed upon.

Notice how Eliza's confrontation demanding to know what she is to become stages social mobility's genuine cost with unusual directness, her transformation producing displacement between two worlds rather than simple, uncomplicated advancement into a new one.

Consider how the play's refusal to provide the marriage audiences conventionally expect deliberately denies romantic comedy's standard resolution in favor of a genuinely harder question about a transformed woman's actual independent future.

Where Major Barbara tests a theorist's convictions against economic reality, this play tests Higgins's scientific confidence against a human being who refuses to remain merely his finished experiment once the wager is won.

## Questions it raises

- Does Higgins's wager with Pickering successfully demonstrate that class is fundamentally performable through speech alone, or does the play's own conclusion suggest genuine class transformation requires more than linguistic training can actually accomplish?
- How should a reader weigh Higgins's scientific triumph against his evident indifference to Eliza's actual welfare and feelings throughout the experiment? Does his intellectual achievement excuse or worsen his treatment of her as merely an experimental subject?
- What does Eliza's confrontation, demanding to know what she is to become, accomplish that a more conventional romantic resolution could not? Does this ending's refusal of marriage strengthen the play's critique of Higgins, or does it simply leave Eliza's actual future genuinely uncertain?
- How persuasive is Shaw's insistence, reinforced in his prose sequel, that Eliza should not marry Higgins? Does this authorial intervention successfully close off a romantic reading many audiences and productions have nonetheless continued to prefer?
- Given that this play interrogates class as performance through language specifically, how does its treatment of speech and accent relate to broader questions about social mobility and authentic identity beyond the specific British class context Shaw depicts?
- What does this play's continued popularity, including its transformation into the musical My Fair Lady, suggest about audiences' persistent desire for the romantic resolution Shaw himself deliberately withheld? Does this reception history reveal something about how adaptation can override authorial intention?
