## Overview

About eight hundred and seventy pages across eighteen books, published in 1749, Henry Fielding's most structurally ambitious and most fully realized novel, extending the comic epic prose form he had first tested seven years earlier in Joseph Andrews into a considerably larger, more architecturally elaborate work. Each of the novel's eighteen books opens with a reflective essay in the narrator's own voice, addressing questions of literary craft, morality or the reader's own developing expectations directly, a level of formal, self-conscious narratorial commentary genuinely unusual in English fiction before this novel and significantly influential on the developing novel form for at least a century afterward.

The novel follows Tom Jones, a foundling of unknown parentage discovered as an infant in the bed of the benevolent Squire Allworthy and subsequently raised alongside Allworthy's actual nephew Blifil as presumptive co-heirs to the Allworthy estate, a foundational mystery of origin that structures the entire narrative's eventual resolution, connecting the question of Tom's actual birth directly to both his romantic prospects with Sophia Western and his claim on Allworthy's affection and inheritance.

## What happens

**Tom and Blifil's contrasting characters.** Raised together from infancy, Tom and Blifil develop into sharply contrasting figures: Tom warm-hearted, generous and impulsively imprudent, repeatedly creating crises through poor judgment that his fundamentally good nature must then survive, and Blifil cold, calculating and outwardly pious, presenting a surface of moral respectability that conceals genuine self-interest and, as the novel gradually reveals, active scheming against Tom's own interests and reputation.

**Banishment.** Blifil's carefully managed manipulation, presented to Squire Allworthy as pious concern for both his uncle's welfare and Tom's own moral correction, gradually poisons Allworthy's opinion of Tom until Tom is banished from the household entirely, an injustice the narrator makes explicit stems from Blifil's calculated self-interest rather than from any genuine moral failing on Tom's part serious enough to actually warrant such severe consequence.

**Sophia's resistance and flight.** Sophia Western, daughter of a neighboring squire and Tom's beloved, resists her father Squire Western's determination that she marry Blifil instead, a match motivated by property and social convenience rather than any genuine regard for Sophia's own actual happiness, and eventually flees the family home rather than submit to the forced marriage, setting her own journey in motion alongside and repeatedly intersecting with Tom's parallel travels.

**The road to London.** The novel's extensive middle section follows Tom's and Sophia's separately converging journeys toward London, structured around a long sequence of inns, crossroads and near-misses that became a genuinely influential model for the English picaresque road novel, set against the real historical backdrop of the Jacobite rising of 1745, which provides both immediate plot complications and a broader sense of genuine historical stakes and uncertainty running alongside the more purely personal romantic plot.

**London and decline.** Once in London, Tom's characteristic imprudence leads him into a genuinely compromising affair with the sophisticated and manipulative Lady Bellaston and eventually into prison on a false charge, apparent narrative low points that seem, for a considerable stretch, to move him further rather than closer to any eventual reunion with Sophia or restoration to Allworthy's favor.

**Revelation and resolution.** The novel's climactic final books resolve both the mystery of Tom's actual parentage and the full extent of Blifil's earlier deceptions and manipulations, revelations that restore Tom to Allworthy's genuine affection and finally clear away the obstacles, social, familial and romantic, that had kept him from Sophia throughout the preceding narrative, resolving in his eventual marriage to her.

## How to read it

Read the narrator's opening essays to each of the eighteen books as integral to the novel's actual meaning and method rather than as separable digressions to be skimmed past; Fielding's direct commentary on craft, morality and reader expectation is doing genuine interpretive work, shaping how the following narrative material should actually be understood and evaluated.

Pay close attention to how consistently the novel inverts conventional expectations about virtue and vice, Tom's generous imprudence repeatedly proving more genuinely admirable than Blifil's calculated, outwardly respectable self-interest, and consider how thoroughly Fielding builds the entire plot architecture specifically to justify and demonstrate this inversion.

Notice the deliberate structural parallel between Tom's and Sophia's separately converging road journeys, tracking how their repeated near-misses and eventual intersections at inns and crossroads create sustained narrative tension while also allowing Fielding to survey, through the varied travelers and innkeepers they encounter, a genuine cross-section of contemporary English social life.

Consider the Jacobite rising's specific historical presence within the novel's middle section as more than mere period color; the genuine historical uncertainty and disruption of 1745 provides real stakes running alongside the personal romantic plot, connecting Tom's individual story to a broader moment of genuine national crisis and uncertainty.

Read this novel directly alongside Fielding's earlier Joseph Andrews to trace how the comic epic prose form he first tested there develops into a considerably more architecturally ambitious and more fully integrated achievement here, particularly in how the narrator's direct commentary becomes a far more developed and more consistently deployed structural device.

## Questions it raises

- Does the novel's consistent inversion of conventional virtue and vice, Tom's flawed generosity proving more valuable than Blifil's calculated respectability, hold up as a persuasive moral argument, or does the plot's machinery work too obviously in Tom's favor to constitute a genuinely fair test of the two characters' actual comparative worth?
- What does the narrator's direct, essayistic commentary at the opening of each book accomplish that a more purely narrated, less self-consciously commented novel could not? Does this formal device strengthen the novel's moral and comic argument, or does it risk over-explaining what the narrative itself already demonstrates?
- How does the extended parallel structure of Tom's and Sophia's separately converging road journeys function beyond generating narrative suspense? What does this structure reveal about eighteenth-century English social life that a more direct, less digressive narrative might not capture as fully?
- Given that Tom's own poor judgment repeatedly creates the very crises his good nature then must survive, does the novel ultimately argue that generous imprudence is simply preferable to calculated prudence, or does it suggest a more complicated relationship between good character and good judgment that neither Tom nor Blifil fully embodies?
- What is the significance of setting the novel's central road journey against the real historical backdrop of the 1745 Jacobite rising? Does this historical context do genuine thematic work connecting personal and national uncertainty, or does it function primarily as incidental period detail?
- How does Tom Jones's considerably greater structural ambition and length, relative to Fielding's earlier Joseph Andrews, change what the comic epic prose form is actually capable of achieving? Does the expanded scale represent unambiguous improvement, or does something get lost in the transition from the earlier novel's looser, more purely episodic structure?
