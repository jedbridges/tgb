## Overview

About two hundred and twenty pages, published in 1833, one of the earliest and most tightly constructed novels in Balzac's vast Human Comedy, and, for many readers, the best introduction to his work precisely because of that compactness; where his later novels sprawl across hundreds of pages and dozens of characters, Eugénie Grandet concentrates its full effect into a single household, a handful of characters, and a story told with real economy and control.

Set in the small provincial town of Saumur, in the Loire valley, the novel is Balzac's study of avarice as a psychological and social force, embodied in the figure of old Grandet, a former cooper who has, through a combination of shrewdness, luck and the confiscation of church property during the Revolution, become one of the wealthiest men in the province while maintaining, deliberately, the outward appearance and habits of a modest tradesman. Balzac was in his early thirties when he wrote it, still establishing the ambitious scheme of the interconnected Human Comedy, and the novel's careful, almost mathematical construction, tracing exactly how a single dominant character trait shapes and eventually damages everyone in a household, became a model for much of what he wrote afterward.

## What happens

**The Grandet household.** Old Grandet, a former cooper who made his first fortune buying confiscated church vineyards cheaply during the Revolution and has multiplied it many times since through decades of shrewd, relentless accumulation, rules his household with an absolute and minutely detailed control over every expenditure. His wife, gentle, pious and entirely without independent will after decades of subjection to her husband, and his daughter Eugénie, now twenty-three, live in conditions of real material deprivation despite the family's enormous and largely secret wealth, rationed candles, counted lumps of sugar, cold unheated rooms, all imposed by a father whose exact, almost ritualistic management of these tiny household economies Balzac renders in extraordinary, faintly hypnotic detail.

**Charles arrives.** On the evening of Eugénie's birthday, her ruined Parisian cousin Charles Grandet arrives unexpectedly, sent by his father, who has just gone bankrupt and, unknown to Charles at the point of his arrival, has shot himself rather than face the disgrace. Charles is everything the Saumur household is not, fashionable, worldly, elegantly dressed, and Eugénie, who has never before been permitted anything resembling her own desire, falls immediately and completely in love with him.

Old Grandet, informed of his brother's death and ruin, calculates coldly how to manage the situation to his own advantage and reputation, eventually engineering a scheme, through his notary and banker, to settle his dead brother's debts in a way that costs Grandet himself almost nothing while preserving the family name, all while showing his devastated, penniless nephew a chilly, calculated hospitality that never risks an actual sou of his own fortune.

**Eugénie's gift.** Before Charles departs for the Indies, where he intends to rebuild his fortune, Eugénie secretly gives him her entire private hoard, gold coins given to her over the years as birthday and New Year gifts, money that is, within the household's peculiar economy, genuinely her own to dispose of. It is the first and only significant act of independent will in Eugénie's entire adult life, and Balzac is careful to present it not primarily as romantic folly but as something closer to a first assertion of selfhood, a use of her own resources according to her own desire rather than her father's, in a household where every other resource and every other desire has always belonged to him.

**Grandet's fury, and the mother's death.** When old Grandet discovers that Eugénie's gold is gone, his rage is immediate, extreme and sustained; he confines his daughter to bread and water in her room for months, in a domestic tyranny Balzac describes with real, uncomfortable specificity. Eugénie's mother, already frail and worn down by decades under Grandet's rule, is devastated by the conflict between husband and daughter, and her health, already failing, declines further; she dies before the novel's central crisis fully resolves, a death Balzac presents as the final, cumulative cost of a lifetime spent entirely subordinated to her husband's will.

**Betrayal, and the inheritance.** Charles, in the Indies, prospers considerably, but prospers by abandoning the values, and eventually the memory, of the cousin who financed his new start; he returns to France years later engaged to a wealthy, aristocratic woman whose family connections will advance him socially, writing Eugénie a cold, businesslike letter proposing, in place of the marriage she had waited faithfully to have with him, merely to repay the money she once gave him.

Old Grandet dies not long afterward, having spent his final months obsessively counting and recounting his fortune even as his own physical strength failed him, and Eugénie inherits the whole of his enormous wealth, becoming, in the novel's own phrase, the richest woman in the province. The novel's final and most devastating irony is what she does with this inheritance: rather than using it to escape the frugal, joyless household of her childhood, Eugénie continues to live largely as her father lived, in careful, habitual austerity, her vast means seemingly incapable of undoing the pattern the house itself instilled in her, devoting much of her fortune instead to charitable and religious purposes, in a life the town regards, not entirely wrongly, as a kind of quiet, lifelong penance for a love that was never returned.

## How to read it

Read the opening chapters describing the Grandet household's daily economies slowly, without rushing toward the plot. Balzac's minute, almost obsessive rendering of exactly how avarice organizes a household's smallest details, the counted sugar lumps, the rationed candles, is doing real characterization work, not merely atmospheric decoration, and it establishes the logic that governs everything that follows.

Notice that Balzac never makes Grandet simply ridiculous or purely comic, in the way misers are often played for laughter in earlier literary tradition. His avarice has an internal coherence, each small cruelty following reasonably from principles Grandet genuinely holds, and taking that coherence seriously is what makes the novel disturbing rather than merely amusing.

Read Eugénie's gift to Charles as an act of self-assertion rather than only as romantic naivety. In a household where every resource has always belonged, in practice, to her father's will rather than her own, giving away money that is genuinely hers is the first time she exercises anything like independent agency, and the novel's later tragedy depends on recognizing what that gesture actually cost her to make.

Pay close attention to the novel's final pages and to what Eugénie does with her inheritance. The apparent irony, that vast wealth changes nothing about how she actually lives, is the novel's real subject, more than either the miser's comic vices or the young woman's thwarted romance individually.

Read this novel alongside Père Goriot, written not long after, to see two different Balzac fathers, one who beggars himself entirely for his daughters' advancement in society, one who impoverishes his family while sitting on a fortune, and consider what the comparison reveals about the range of ways paternal power distorts a household in Balzac's fiction.

## Questions it raises

- Is Grandet's avarice best understood as a personal vice, or as a logical, if extreme, extension of the same acquisitive values the whole surrounding society organizes itself around?
- Eugénie's gift to Charles is her first act of independent will. Does that make it more admirable, more understandable, or does it simply show how narrow her range of choice actually was?
- Why does inheriting enormous wealth change nothing about how Eugénie actually lives? What does that suggest about how deeply a childhood household can shape a person regardless of later circumstance?
- Charles abandons the values that once bound him to Eugénie in favor of social advancement. Is he simply weak, or is he doing exactly what the novel's surrounding society trains everyone to do?
- Eugénie's mother is destroyed by decades of subjection with no act of resistance at all, while Eugénie's one act of resistance also ends badly. What is the novel suggesting about the available options for a woman in this household?
- The town regards Eugénie's later charitable austerity as a kind of quiet penance. Is that an accurate reading of her choices, or does it impose a sentimental narrative on what might simply be habit she never learned to break?
