## Overview

About a hundred and ten pages, John Stuart Mill's political treatise, published in 1869. Mill argues that the legal subordination of women to men is a survival of the law of force, the last relic of a world in which the strong simply ruled the weak, and that it should be replaced by perfect equality before the law.

He attacks the claim that women's present character shows their nature, since that character has been produced by the very conditions under discussion, and insists that no one can know what women can do until the artificial barriers come down. He describes marriage as it then stood as a form of legal despotism, and argues that equality would benefit men as much as women.

## What happens

**Subordination as a survival of force.** Mill opens by characterizing women's legal subordination as a survival of the law of force, the last relic of a world in which the strong simply ruled the weak, rejecting any claim that this arrangement rests on genuine natural or rational foundation.

**Attacking character as evidence of nature.** Mill attacks the claim that women's present observed character reveals their true underlying nature, arguing this character has itself been produced by the very conditions of subordination under discussion, exposing a circular argument.

**Insisting on genuine uncertainty.** Mill insists that no one can actually know what women are capable of until the artificial barriers currently constraining them are removed, refusing premature certainty about women's capacities in either direction.

**Describing marriage as legal despotism.** Mill describes contemporary marriage as it then legally stood as a form of legal despotism, examining the specific legal structures that placed married women under their husbands' near-total legal authority and control.

**Arguing equality benefits men too.** Mill argues that establishing genuine equality between men and women would benefit men as much as women, refusing to present the proposed reform as pure sacrifice by men for women's sake alone.

**Calling for perfect legal equality.** The treatise concludes by calling for perfect equality before the law between men and women, a comprehensive legal reform rather than merely incremental adjustment to the existing system of subordination.

## How to read it

Read Mill's characterization of subordination as a survival of the law of force as a deliberately damning historical judgment rather than neutral description; this framing treats women's legal subordination as illegitimate holdover from pure might-makes-right arrangement rather than any considered rational system.

Pay close attention to how Mill's attack on character as evidence of nature makes a genuinely sophisticated methodological point extending well beyond its original context; recognizing that constrained conditions produce their own apparent confirming evidence remains relevant to evaluating similar arguments in other contexts.

Notice how Mill's insistence on genuine current ignorance about women's capacities refuses easy certainty in either direction; this position neither assumes equal capacity as already proven nor accepts claimed inferiority, insisting instead on honest acknowledgment of what remains genuinely unknown.

Consider Mill's argument that equality would benefit men as much as women as central to the treatise's actual persuasive strategy; refusing to frame reform as pure sacrifice by the empowered group makes the case for change rest on mutual interest rather than appeal to men's charity alone.

Read this treatise alongside Wollstonecraft's own earlier Vindication of the Rights of Woman to compare how two major writers, separated by roughly eight decades, each constructed arguments for women's equality within considerably different historical and intellectual contexts.

## Questions it raises

- Does characterizing women's subordination as a survival of the law of force successfully explain its historical persistence, or does this framing oversimplify what may have been a more complex combination of economic, religious and social factors sustaining the arrangement across many centuries?
- How persuasive is Mill's methodological argument against using women's observed character as evidence of their true nature? Does this argument successfully expose genuine circularity, or does it risk making any claim about gender difference unfalsifiable regardless of actual evidence?
- What does Mill's insistence on genuine current ignorance about women's capacities accomplish rhetorically and philosophically? Does refusing premature certainty in either direction represent intellectual honesty, or does it function as a strategic middle position avoiding stronger claims Mill might have made?
- How does describing contemporary marriage as legal despotism shape the treatise's broader argument? Does this specific characterization of marriage law add persuasive force beyond the more general argument about subordination as historical survival of force?
- Given that Mill argues equality would benefit men as much as women, how persuasive is this specific claim? Does this argument genuinely identify mutual interest, or does it risk understating the real costs to men's existing privilege that genuine equality would actually require?
- What does this treatise's continued relevance to contemporary discussions of gender equality suggest about the durability of Mill's specific arguments? Do his methodological points about character and nature, and his call for genuine equality before the law, still offer useful resources for addressing comparable contemporary debates?
