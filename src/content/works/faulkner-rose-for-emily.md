## Overview

About fifteen pages, William Faulkner's first published short story, appearing in Forum magazine in April 1930, set in Faulkner's fictional Jefferson, Mississippi, part of the same broader Yoknapatawpha County setting that would organize much of his subsequent major fiction. The story is narrated collectively in an unusual and immediately distinctive first-person plural voice, we, representing the town's shared perspective on Miss Emily Grierson rather than any single, individually named observer, a technique that lets Jefferson's collective gossip, speculation and selectively unreliable memory stand in for direct knowledge of Emily's actual interior life and private experience.

The story's structure is deliberately non-chronological, opening with Emily's funeral before moving backward and forward across decades of her life in a sequence organized around thematic and emotional association rather than strict time order, a structure that allows Faulkner to withhold the story's central, devastating revelation until its very final paragraph, transforming a reader's understanding of everything that came before once that revelation finally arrives.

## What happens

**Emily's funeral and the town's collective curiosity.** The story opens with Emily's death and funeral, establishing immediately the town's collective, largely voyeuristic curiosity about a woman who has lived in near-total isolation inside her family home for decades, a house no one besides an elderly manservant had entered in at least ten years before her death.

**The Grierson family's decline.** The narrative traces Emily's family background as one of Jefferson's old, once-prominent families, now reduced considerably in actual wealth and standing even as Emily herself, and to some extent the town around her, continues treating the family name as carrying inherited social significance it no longer fully possesses, a genteel decline the story presents with considerable Southern Gothic atmosphere.

**Her father's death and Homer Barron's courtship.** Following her domineering father's death, which Emily initially refuses to acknowledge for several days before finally allowing the body to be buried, Emily eventually begins an unconventional courtship with Homer Barron, a Northern construction foreman working in town, a relationship the more socially conservative elements of Jefferson regard with open disapproval given both Homer's outsider status and the apparent impropriety of the unmarried courtship itself.

**The arsenic purchase.** Emily purchases arsenic from the local druggist, refusing, despite legal requirements, to state what purpose she intends for the poison, and the town speculates extensively about her reasons without ever actually confirming them, a deliberately planted and deliberately unresolved clue whose full significance the story withholds until its closing paragraph.

**Homer's disappearance and Emily's isolation.** Homer Barron disappears from the narrative shortly after the arsenic purchase, apparently having left town, and Emily subsequently withdraws almost entirely from public life, living in near-total isolation for the remaining decades of her life while the town continues to observe and speculate about her from a permanent, unbridgeable distance.

**The final revelation.** Following Emily's funeral, the townspeople finally break open a long-locked upstairs room in her house and discover Homer Barron's decayed remains laid out on a bed, and, on the pillow beside his skull, a single long strand of Emily's own iron-gray hair, a revelation that recontextualizes everything the story has previously narrated, confirming that Emily poisoned Homer decades earlier rather than losing him to abandonment, and had continued, in some form, to keep him with her ever since.

## How to read it

Read the story's deliberately scrambled chronology as central to its actual effect rather than as mere stylistic ornament; withholding the final revelation until the last paragraph while scattering clues, the arsenic purchase, the smell from the house, the locked room, throughout the preceding non-chronological narrative rewards and requires rereading the entire story with this final knowledge in view.

Pay close attention to the unusual collective we narration, and consider what this technique accomplishes that a single named narrator could not; the town's shared, curious, ultimately limited perspective becomes as much the story's actual subject as Emily herself, since readers never gain any direct access to Emily's own interior consciousness beyond what the town's external observation and speculation can supply.

Notice how the story roots Emily's isolation and her final, horrifying act in the broader context of the Grierson family's genteel decline and the changing modern South around her; Emily's refusal to accept her father's death, her father's own domineering influence over her earlier romantic prospects, and her subsequent inability to accept Homer's departure all connect to a broader pattern of refusing to acknowledge change and loss.

Consider the arsenic purchase and the townspeople's persistent but ultimately passive speculation about it as a study in collective complicity through inaction; the town suspects enough to gossip extensively but never intervenes decisively, allowing Emily's isolation and its eventual horrifying consequence to continue undisturbed for decades.

Read the story as an early, compressed demonstration of narrative techniques, non-chronological structure, collective or otherwise unconventional narration, that Faulkner would develop considerably further and at much greater length in his subsequent major novels, making this short early story a genuinely useful introduction to his broader technical concerns.

## Questions it raises

- Does the story's deliberately scrambled, non-chronological structure serve its central revelation more effectively than a straightforward chronological telling would, and what does rereading the story's earlier scenes with the final revelation in mind actually change about how those earlier scenes read?
- What does the unusual collective we narration accomplish that a single, individually named narrator could not? Does this technique implicate the town itself in Emily's isolation and its consequences, or does it simply provide a convenient vehicle for withholding information from the reader?
- How does the story's Southern Gothic atmosphere, the decaying old family, the genteel decline, the buried horror, function as more than mere setting or mood? Does this atmospheric mode do genuine thematic work connecting Emily's personal story to broader questions about the changing South?
- What does the town's persistent but ultimately passive speculation about the arsenic purchase suggest about collective complicity through inaction? Could more decisive intervention at any point have changed Emily's eventual fate, or does the story present her isolation as effectively inevitable regardless of the town's attention?
- How should a reader interpret Emily's earlier refusal to accept her father's death alongside her later, far more devastating refusal to accept Homer's departure? Does the story present these as directly connected instances of a single underlying psychological pattern, or as separate, only loosely related episodes?
- Given that this was Faulkner's first published story, what does it suggest about techniques, non-chronological structure, unconventional narration, withheld revelation, that would become increasingly central to his much longer and more structurally ambitious subsequent novels?
