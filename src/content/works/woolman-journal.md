## Overview

About two hundred and thirty pages with A Plea for the Poor, the spiritual journal of a New Jersey Quaker tailor, written over the last twenty years of his life and published in 1774. Woolman records his growing conviction that slaveholding is incompatible with Christianity, and the decades he spent travelling the American colonies persuading Friends one household at a time to free their slaves, refusing to write wills that conveyed them and declining hospitality paid for by slave labour.

He also refuses dyed cloth, silver vessels and war taxes, travels among the Delaware at Wyalusing, and dies of smallpox in York on a visit to English Friends.

## What happens

**Youth and calling.** Woolman describes his childhood in New Jersey, his early sense of the divine presence, a boyhood cruelty to a robin that he never forgot, and his settling as a shopkeeper and tailor, deliberately keeping his business small so that it would not consume him.

**The bill of sale.** Asked by his employer to write a bill of sale for a slave, he writes it but declares his belief that slavekeeping is inconsistent with Christianity, and afterward refuses every such instrument, including wills, explaining his reasons to the men who ask.

**The southern journeys.** Woolman travels to Maryland, Virginia and Carolina, stays with slaveholding Friends, speaks with each about the weight on his conscience, and pays the enslaved who serve him, publishing Some Considerations on the Keeping of Negroes on his return.

**The yearly meetings.** At Philadelphia Yearly Meeting he speaks against slaveholding, and the Society moves over years from discouragement to prohibition, Woolman visiting slaveholders in committee to labour with them.

**Plainness and Wyalusing.** He gives up dyed clothes, silver and the payment of taxes for the French and Indian War, and travels in 1763 to the Delaware town of Wyalusing to sit with the Indians in their meetings and learn from them.

**England and death.** Woolman sails to England in steerage in 1772, refuses to ride in the painted coaches, walks between meetings, and dies of smallpox in York, tended by Friends.

## How to read it

Read the episode of the bill of sale as the journal's turning point, a principle first acted on in a small transaction and then carried into every later one.

Pay close attention to the method of the southern journeys, one household at a time, and consider what it accomplished that public campaigns did not.

Notice the extension of the scruple from slavery to dyed cloth, silver and war taxes, and read A Plea for the Poor as the argument that connects them.

Consider the prose, so plain that Lamb told readers to learn it by heart, and ask what the plainness does that eloquence could not.

Read this journal alongside Franklin's Autobiography to compare two Pennsylvanians of the same generation keeping account of their lives, one for worldly improvement and one for the inner light.

## Questions it raises

- Is persuasion one household at a time a model for reform or a method that only works within a community already disposed to listen?
- What does it mean that the Quaker renunciation of slavery came by conscience rather than decree, and did it make the renunciation more or less secure?
- Is Woolman's connection of slavery to luxury, dyed cloth and silver, a moral insight or a scruple that risks making every comfort a sin?
- What does the journal's plainness of style tell us about the relation between the way a man writes and the way he lives?
- How should a reader take the visit to Wyalusing, where Woolman goes to learn from the Indians rather than to convert them, in the year of Pontiac's war?
- Why does a tailor's spiritual journal stand at the head of the Harvard Classics, and what did Eliot want readers to take from it first?
