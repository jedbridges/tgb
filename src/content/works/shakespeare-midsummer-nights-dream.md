## Overview

About two hundred pages, Shakespeare's comedy, written around 1595 to 1596. Hermia, ordered by her father to marry Demetrius, runs into the woods outside Athens with Lysander, followed by Demetrius and by Helena, who loves him. In the same woods Oberon and Titania, king and queen of the fairies, are quarrelling over a changeling boy, and a troupe of Athenian workmen are rehearsing a play for Duke Theseus's wedding.

Oberon's servant Puck misapplies a love potion, the lovers' affections tangle, and the weaver Bottom is given an ass's head and Titania's love. All is put right by morning.

## What happens

**Hermia's flight into the woods.** Hermia, ordered by her father to marry Demetrius rather than her chosen Lysander, flees into the woods outside Athens with Lysander, followed by Demetrius and by Helena, who loves him despite his rejection.

**Oberon and Titania's quarrel.** In the same woods, Oberon and Titania, king and queen of the fairies, quarrel over a changeling boy, establishing a parallel supernatural conflict running alongside the human lovers' own confusion.

**The Athenian workmen rehearse.** A troupe of Athenian workmen rehearse their own play for Duke Theseus's upcoming wedding, offering comic meta-theatrical commentary through their earnest but genuinely incompetent dramatic efforts.

**Puck's misapplied potion.** Oberon's servant Puck misapplies a love potion intended to resolve the lovers' confusion, instead tangling their affections further and creating even greater romantic chaos in the enchanted forest.

**Bottom's transformation.** The weaver Bottom is given an ass's head by Puck's mischief, and Titania, under the potion's influence, falls genuinely in love with him despite his grotesque and absurd transformed appearance.

**All is put right by morning.** By morning, the potion's effects are reversed, the lovers' proper pairings restored, and the entire episode resolves into the harmonious conclusion appropriate to the play's comic structure.

## How to read it

Read Puck's misapplication of the love potion as staging romantic desire as genuinely unstable and manipulable through purely external mechanism, love's apparent depth exposed as vulnerable to trivial accident rather than fixed, essential attraction.

Pay close attention to how Oberon and Titania's quarrel over the changeling boy running parallel to the human lovers' confusion suggests fairy and human worlds share the same fundamental capriciousness about desire and attachment.

Notice how Bottom receiving an ass's head alongside Titania's genuine love pushes the play's exploration of desire's arbitrariness to its most extreme and comic limit, a fairy queen's passion attaching itself to grotesque transformation with total sincerity.

Consider how the Athenian workmen's rehearsal of their own deliberately terrible play offers pointed meta-theatrical commentary on dramatic craft itself, amateur enthusiasm staged for comic effect alongside the more polished verse surrounding it.

Read this comedy alongside Shakespeare's own Twelfth Night to compare two treatments of love's essential instability and confusion, one staged through supernatural magic in an enchanted forest, the other through mistaken identity and disguise in Illyria.

## Questions it raises

- Does Puck's misapplication of the love potion successfully convey something true about romantic desire's actual instability, or does attributing the lovers' confusion to magical accident let the play avoid harder questions about how such confusion might arise without supernatural intervention?
- How does the parallel between Oberon and Titania's fairy quarrel and the human lovers' own confusion shape your understanding of desire more broadly? Does this parallel suggest something universal about attachment, or simply provide convenient dramatic structure?
- What does Titania's genuine love for the transformed Bottom accomplish comedically and thematically? Does this extreme case illuminate something true about desire's fundamental arbitrariness, or does it function primarily as broad physical comedy?
- How does the workmen's rehearsal and eventual performance relate to the main plot's more elevated romantic concerns? Does this comic subplot merely provide contrast, or does it offer genuine commentary on the nature of theatrical representation itself?
- Given that the play resolves with all confusion reversed by morning, what does this tidy restoration suggest about the actual stability of the affections the lovers eventually settle into? Does the ending fully dispel the doubts the woods' chaos had raised?
- What does this play's continued status as an accessible entry point into Shakespeare suggest about its particular combination of romantic comedy, supernatural fantasy and broad physical humor? Does this combination make the play's more serious undercurrents easier or harder to notice?
