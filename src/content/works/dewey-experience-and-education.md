synopsis: >-
  A late, short book in which Dewey answers both the traditional schooling he had spent thirty years criticising and the progressive schools that claimed him. Neither imposing subject matter from outside nor simply following the child's impulses will do. What is needed is a criterion for which experiences educate, and Dewey offers two: continuity, meaning an experience must lead somewhere further, and interaction between the learner and the conditions around them. From these he derives an account of freedom, of the teacher's authority, and of why subject matter still has to be organised.
