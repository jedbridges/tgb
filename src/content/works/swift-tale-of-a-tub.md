## Overview

About one hundred fifty pages, Swift's first major work, published anonymously in 1704, is a prose satire on corruptions in religion and learning. Its narrative thread is an allegory of three brothers, Peter, Martin and Jack, standing for the Roman, Anglican and dissenting churches, who twist their father's will to justify altering the coats he left them.

This story is repeatedly interrupted by digressions in the voice of a modern hack writer, on critics, on madness, on digressions themselves, that parody the pedantry and self-importance of contemporary authors. It was published with The Battle of the Books.

## What happens

**Allegorizing the churches as three brothers.** The narrative sets up an allegory of three brothers, Peter, Martin and Jack, whose story stands for the Roman, Anglican and dissenting churches across the centuries following their father's death.

**Twisting the father's will.** The brothers twist their father's will to justify altering the coats he left them, each finding self-interested interpretive grounds for departing from the plain instructions they inherited.

**Interrupting with digressions on critics.** The main narrative is repeatedly interrupted by a digression in the voice of a modern hack writer on critics, parodying contemporary literary pretension in a register entirely separate from the allegory.

**Interrupting with digressions on madness.** A further digression addresses madness directly, in the same hack-writer voice, extending the work's satire of pedantry into a mock-treatise on reason's collapse.

**Digressing on digressions themselves.** The hack writer digresses even on the subject of digression itself, the self-parody turning fully reflexive and making the interruptions as much the book's actual subject as the allegory they interrupt.

**Pairing with The Battle of the Books.** The work was published together with The Battle of the Books, extending its satirical method from religious corruption to the contemporary quarrel between ancient and modern learning.

## How to read it

Read the allegory of Peter, Martin and Jack, twisting their father's will to justify altering their inherited coats, as reducing centuries of religious schism to a single comic family quarrel over clothing.

Pay close attention to how the digressions in the voice of a modern hack writer, on critics, on madness, on digressions themselves, parody the pedantry and self-importance of contemporary authors by enacting the very faults they describe.

Notice how the digression on digressions turns the satire fully reflexive, the interruptions becoming as central to the work's meaning as the allegorical narrative they repeatedly derail.

Consider how the work's anonymous publication, as Swift's first major effort, let him attack corruptions in religion and learning from a position of complete deniability before his authorship was known.

Read this work alongside Gulliver's Travels to compare an earlier, denser satire built from allegory and digression against the later work's more accessible fantastic-voyage structure aimed at a broader satirical target.

## Questions it raises

- Does reducing centuries of religious schism to a comic allegory about three brothers and a disputed will trivialize genuine doctrinal difference, or does the comic register expose something true about the self-interest beneath theological argument?
- How do the digressions on critics, madness and digression itself change a reader's experience of the main allegorical narrative? Does the interruption strengthen the satire or fracture the work past coherence?
- What does publishing anonymously suggest about the risk Swift perceived in attacking religious and literary corruption directly? Does the work's satirical daring depend on that protective anonymity?
- How should a reader weigh the allegory of the three brothers against the digressions in the hack writer's voice? Are these two satires of equal weight, or does one ultimately serve the other?
- Given that the work was published alongside The Battle of the Books, how does the quarrel between ancient and modern learning relate to the allegory's quarrel between religious traditions? Do both target a shared underlying corruption?
- Does the work's turn toward reflexive self-parody, digressing on digression itself, represent a genuine satirical achievement, or a point past which the satire risks losing its object entirely?
