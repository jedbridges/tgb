## Overview

This entry names a specific bibliographic artifact rather than a separate work: Joan Riviere's 1920 English translation of the twenty-eight Vienna lectures Freud delivered during the two wartime winters covered in this catalog's companion entry, published in the United States by Boni and Liveright under the title A General Introduction to Psychoanalysis, a title distinct from the Introductory Lectures on Psycho-Analysis used for the same translation in Britain and for James Strachey's later, now more commonly cited Standard Edition translation. The Great Books of the Western World editors selected Riviere's translation under this American title for their own collection, which is why readers working through that particular set encounter Freud's lectures under a name many readers who first met the same material elsewhere, through Strachey's translation or through later citations, will not immediately recognize as the identical work.

Riviere herself brought unusually direct qualifications to the translation beyond ordinary linguistic skill: she had personally undergone analysis with Freud before undertaking the project, and went on to become a significant psychoanalytic thinker and practitioner in her own right within the British psychoanalytic establishment, giving her rendering of Freud's technical vocabulary and characteristic lecturing voice a practitioner's direct familiarity that a purely literary translator approaching the material from outside the field would likely not have possessed.

## The argument

Because this title designates a specific translation of the same twenty-eight lectures covered fully in this catalog's separate entry for Introductory Lectures on Psychoanalysis, the actual content, argument and structure, the psychology of errors, the theory of dreams, the general theory of the neuroses, is identical between the two entries rather than representing genuinely separate material requiring its own independent summary here. Readers seeking the lectures' actual argument, Freud's account of slips and parapraxes, his theory of manifest and latent dream content, his treatment of libido, transference and the famous comparison to Copernicus and Darwin, should consult that companion entry directly, since any translation of the complete lecture series covers this same ground.

What genuinely distinguishes this particular bibliographic entity is its specific publication and translation history rather than its content. Riviere completed her translation in 1920, working directly from Freud's German text and drawing on her own considerable firsthand psychoanalytic experience, both as someone who had herself been analyzed by Freud and as a practitioner already developing her own standing within the psychoanalytic community. The British and American publishers of her single translation chose different titles for their respective editions, a marketing and editorial decision common enough in early twentieth-century publishing but one that has left a lasting bibliographic trace, since works assembled decades later, including the Great Books of the Western World set, sometimes preserved the specific title under which they had encountered or acquired rights to a given translation rather than standardizing toward whichever title had become most common by the time of their own compilation.

Strachey's later Standard Edition translation, completed considerably after Riviere's and eventually becoming the version most professional psychoanalytic scholarship and most general readers now cite, worked with greater editorial apparatus and more extensive scholarly annotation than Riviere's earlier translation had provided, reflecting both Strachey's own different translating priorities and the psychoanalytic field's own developing scholarly infrastructure across the intervening decades between the two translations.

## How to read it

Read this entry primarily for its bibliographic and historical content, the specific story of how Riviere's translation reached English-language readers under two different titles and how one of those titles came to be preserved in the Great Books of the Western World set, rather than expecting a full independent treatment of the lectures' actual psychoanalytic argument, which the companion Introductory Lectures entry covers in complete detail.

If you are working specifically from the Great Books of the Western World edition and encountering Freud's lectures under this particular title, know that you are reading the identical twenty-eight lectures, covering the identical material in the identical order, that appear under the more commonly cited Introductory Lectures title elsewhere, and that any reliable complete translation, Riviere's original or Strachey's later Standard Edition version, will serve equally well for actually engaging with Freud's argument.

Consider Riviere's own biographical position, an analysand of Freud's who became a significant psychoanalytic thinker in her own right, as worth knowing directly, since her translation was produced by someone with genuinely unusual direct, practitioner-level familiarity with the material she was rendering into English, a qualification worth appreciating even for readers who will ultimately read Strachey's later, more commonly available translation instead.

Recognize this entry's existence as a small but genuine window into how reading lists and canonical collections assembled across different decades and different editorial traditions can preserve small, sometimes puzzling inconsistencies, like a single work appearing under two different titles, that reflect real historical publishing decisions rather than any actual difference in the underlying material itself.
