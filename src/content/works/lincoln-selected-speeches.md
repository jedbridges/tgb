## Overview

About three hundred pages, a collection of Abraham Lincoln's principal speeches and public letters from the Lyceum Address of 1838 to the Second Inaugural of 1865. Reading these documents together across nearly three decades reveals an argument developing with real consistency rather than a politician simply reacting to shifting circumstance, the claim that the Declaration of Independence's promise of equality is the nation's actual founding principle.

The core items are the Peoria speech against the Kansas-Nebraska Act, the House Divided speech, the debates with Douglas, the Cooper Union address, the First Inaugural, the messages to Congress, the Emancipation Proclamation, the Gettysburg Address and the letters to Greeley, Conkling and Mrs Bixby.

## What happens

**Early warnings: the Lyceum Address.** The collection opens with the 1838 Lyceum Address, an early speech warning against mob violence and the erosion of respect for law, establishing themes about constitutional order that would recur throughout Lincoln's later political career.

**The Peoria speech and the House Divided speech.** Lincoln's Peoria speech against the Kansas-Nebraska Act and his subsequent House Divided speech build his sustained argument against slavery's extension through careful constitutional and historical reasoning, establishing the intellectual foundation for his later, more famous statements.

**The Douglas debates.** The debates with Stephen Douglas test Lincoln's developing argument directly against a skilled political opponent's considerably more comfortable position, that popular sovereignty could legitimately let each territory simply vote on whether to permit slavery.

**The Cooper Union address.** The Cooper Union address extends Lincoln's argument with genuine historical rigor, tracing the founders' own actual views on federal power over slavery in the territories to counter Southern claims that restricting slavery's expansion violated the Constitution's original meaning.

**Wartime documents: the Emancipation Proclamation and Gettysburg Address.** The Emancipation Proclamation and the Gettysburg Address mark the collection's wartime culmination, transforming a war initially fought to preserve the Union into one explicitly refounding the nation on the equality principle Lincoln had been developing since his earliest major speeches.

**Personal letters.** The collection includes Lincoln's personal letters to Horace Greeley, James Conkling and the widow Lydia Bixby, revealing the same underlying conviction tested against genuine political pressure and irreducible human grief rather than abstract constitutional argument alone.

## How to read it

Read this collection chronologically to trace the genuine development of Lincoln's central argument across nearly three decades; encountering the speeches in sequence reveals how consistently he builds toward the claim that the Declaration's equality principle constitutes the nation's actual founding commitment rather than mere aspirational rhetoric.

Pay close attention to how the Peoria speech and House Divided speech establish constitutional and historical groundwork that the Douglas debates then test directly against a skilled opponent; this sequence shows Lincoln's argument being refined through genuine political confrontation rather than developing in isolation.

Notice how the Cooper Union address's historical rigor, tracing the founders' actual documented views on federal power over slavery, gives Lincoln's argument a specific evidentiary foundation beyond purely moral or philosophical assertion, directly countering contemporary claims about original constitutional meaning.

Consider how the Emancipation Proclamation and Gettysburg Address transform the war's stated purpose, connecting this wartime shift directly back to the equality argument Lincoln had been developing since his earliest speeches rather than treating it as an entirely new wartime improvisation.

Read this collection alongside Lincoln's own Second Inaugural Address, delivered just weeks before his assassination, to see how this final major speech both culminates and adds genuine theological depth to the sustained political and constitutional argument traced across the rest of his career.
