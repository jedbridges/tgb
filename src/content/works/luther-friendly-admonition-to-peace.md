## Overview

About thirty-five pages, Martin Luther's response in the spring of 1525 to the Twelve Articles, the manifesto of the rebelling Swabian peasants, written genuinely trying to prevent a war rather than choosing a side outright. Luther divides blame between two parties he addresses in turn with pointed, specific criticism rather than blanket condemnation or blanket support for either.

The princes and bishops are told that their greed and tyranny provoked the rising and that God will destroy them if they do not relent. The peasants are told that their grievances may be just but that they have no right to take up the sword in the name of the gospel, and that a Christian suffers wrong rather than avenging it. He urges both sides to arbitration. Within weeks the revolt spread and Luther wrote a far more violent tract against it.

## What happens

**The Twelve Articles.** The tract opens by addressing the Twelve Articles, the manifesto of the rebelling Swabian peasants, laying out the specific grievances against serfdom and feudal obligation that had prompted the growing rural rebellion across the German territories.

**Warning the princes and bishops.** Luther tells the princes and bishops directly that their own greed and tyranny provoked the peasant rising, warning that God will destroy them entirely if they do not relent and address the genuine underlying grievances.

**Acknowledging the peasants' just grievances.** Luther acknowledges to the peasants that their specific grievances may indeed be just, a genuine concession to the substance of their complaint against feudal obligation and economic exploitation.

**Denying the right to armed revolt.** Despite this acknowledgment, Luther insists the peasants possess no right to take up the sword in the name of the gospel, arguing that a Christian properly suffers wrong rather than avenging it through violent rebellion against established authority.

**Urging arbitration.** Luther urges both sides to submit their dispute to arbitration rather than continued escalating conflict, a genuine attempt at reconciliation balancing the princes' need for correction against the peasants' need for restraint.

**The revolt's rapid spread and Luther's harsher response.** Within weeks of this tract's publication, the revolt spread rapidly regardless of Luther's careful balancing act, prompting him to write a considerably harsher, more one-sided tract condemning the peasants that would permanently color how this earlier, more measured admonition gets remembered.

## How to read it

Read Luther's genuinely balanced distribution of blame, criticizing both princes and peasants directly, as the tract's central rhetorical achievement rather than simply diplomatic hedging; this even-handed criticism reflects Luther's actual theological position rather than mere political caution.

Pay close attention to how Luther's acknowledgment that the peasants' grievances may be just, combined with his denial of their right to violent revolt, draws a distinction between legitimate complaint and legitimate means of redress that his own broader theology required him to maintain even at real political cost.

Notice how directly Luther's warning to the princes and bishops accuses established political and ecclesiastical authority of provoking the crisis through their own greed and tyranny, a genuinely bold criticism delivered without the hedging such accusation typically required in this period.

Consider this tract's actual historical fate, quickly rendered irrelevant by the revolt's rapid spread and Luther's own subsequent harsher tract, as relevant to how the document is remembered; this earlier, more measured admonition often gets overshadowed by the considerably more violent condemnation that followed it within weeks.

Read this tract alongside Luther's own Ninety-Five Theses to see how the same theological conviction about legitimate versus illegitimate response to injustice, developed initially against indulgence sellers, extends here into a considerably more politically fraught context involving armed popular rebellion.
