## Overview

*Antigone* was produced around 441 BCE, probably fifteen years before *Oedipus the King* and more than thirty before *Oedipus at Colonus*, even though in the story it comes last. The three plays are not a trilogy and were not written as one. Sophocles wrote them decades apart, for different competitions, and they do not agree with each other in every detail.

The play is about fourteen hundred lines, roughly ninety minutes in performance, with a chorus of Theban elders and originally two or three actors taking all the speaking parts. That constraint matters: Antigone and Tiresias were probably played by the same man, and Antigone leaves the stage more than four hundred lines before the end, which means the last quarter of the play belongs entirely to Creon.

Most readers arrive expecting a heroine and a tyrant. What Sophocles wrote is harder. Creon's opening address to the elders is a reasonable statement of the principle that a city's survival comes first and that no friend of the city's enemy can be a friend of his. Antigone is fearless, contemptuous, unwilling to explain herself twice, and cruel to her sister. The play does not soften either of them. By the end Creon is the one asking for pity, and the chorus has to tell us what to think, which in Sophocles usually means nobody is sure.

## What happens

Before dawn, outside the palace. Antigone tells Ismene what Creon has decreed and asks for help with the burial. Ismene refuses: they are women, the dead are dead, and the living have to obey. Antigone cuts her off and goes alone.

Creon addresses the elders and states his policy. A sentry arrives, terrified, to report that someone has sprinkled dust over the body. Creon accuses the guards of taking bribes and orders the corpse uncovered again.

The chorus sings the ode on man, the creature who sails the grey sea, breaks the earth with the plough, snares birds and fish, tames horses, and has found cures for everything except death.

The sentry returns with Antigone, caught in a dust storm pouring libations over her brother. She does not deny it. She tells Creon that his proclamation had no power to override the gods' unwritten laws, and that she always knew she would die. Creon condemns her and, in a fit, Ismene too, who now claims a share in the act; Antigone refuses to let her have it. Creon releases Ismene.

Haemon enters, calm at first, arguing that the whole city is on Antigone's side and that a man who will not bend breaks. The argument turns vicious. Creon calls him a woman's slave; Haemon leaves saying his father will never see him again.

Creon spares Antigone the stoning he first threatened and orders her shut in a rock tomb with a little food, so the city's hands stay clean. She is led out, lamenting the marriage she will never have and appealing to the strangeness of her family's story.

Tiresias, led by a boy, tells Creon that the altars are fouled with carrion carried by dogs and birds, that the gods are refusing sacrifice, and that he will pay corpse for corpse. Creon insults him, then panics, and on the elders' advice runs to free Antigone and bury Polynices.

He is too late. A messenger reports that Antigone had hanged herself with her own veil, and that Haemon, finding her, spat in his father's face, ran on his own sword and died embracing her. Eurydice hears this, goes inside without a word, and kills herself cursing her husband. Creon comes back carrying his son's body and asks to be taken away. The chorus closes on the lateness of wisdom.

## How to read it

Read the whole play in one sitting, then reread three passages: Creon's first speech, Antigone's defence at lines 450 to 470, and the argument with Haemon. The play's balance is visible only if you take Creon's opening seriously before you know how he ends.

Read the choral odes slowly. They are not summaries. The ode on man is triumphant at exactly the moment the audience knows a law has been broken, and the ode on Eros arrives just before Haemon's death, and both comment sideways on the action rather than explaining it.

Pay attention to the speech at lines 904 to 920, where Antigone argues that she would not have done this for a husband or a child, since those can be replaced and a brother cannot. Goethe found it so strange he hoped it was an interpolation, and it is the place to test whether you think she acts on principle or on something more particular than principle.

Do not import the Hegelian reading before you have read the play. Family against state is a useful frame, but Antigone speaks for the gods more than for kinship, and Creon speaks for himself at least as much as for Thebes.

Fagles is the classroom standard and reads well aloud. Anne Carson's version is a provocation worth reading second, not first, and it is under copyright, so quote it sparingly and by name.

## Questions it raises

- Does Antigone have an argument, or a piety that happens to be right?
- Creon has a city to hold together after a civil war. What should he have done with the body?
- Why does Sophocles remove Antigone from the stage so early and give Creon the ending?
- Ismene refuses and survives. Is her caution cowardice, or the ordinary condition of people under a bad decree?
- Antigone says a brother is irreplaceable and a husband is not. Does that reasoning undermine her appeal to universal law?
- The gods never speak. How do we know they are on her side, other than that the altars fill with carrion?
