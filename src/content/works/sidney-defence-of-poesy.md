## Overview

About sixty pages, Philip Sidney's essay, the first major work of literary criticism in English, written around 1580 in answer to Puritan attacks on the stage and published in 1595 after his death. Sidney argues that poetry is the oldest of learning and the best teacher of virtue, because it joins the philosopher's precept to the historian's example in a golden world of its own making.

He answers the charges that poets lie and corrupt, then turns on the state of English writing, praising Chaucer and the old ballad of Chevy Chase and mocking the disorder of contemporary plays.

## What happens

**Answering Puritan attacks.** Sidney writes this essay specifically in answer to Puritan attacks on the stage, establishing English literary criticism's founding project as genuinely defensive and occasional rather than abstract theoretical exercise.

**Poetry as the oldest learning.** Sidney argues that poetry is the oldest of learning, establishing its historical priority and foundational status among the various forms of human intellectual and creative activity.

**Poetry as the best teacher of virtue.** Sidney claims poetry is the best teacher of virtue because it joins the philosopher's precept to the historian's example in a golden world of its own making, combining abstract instruction with concrete narrative.

**Answering the charge that poets lie.** Sidney directly answers the charge that poets lie, treating poetry's fictional status not as a liability requiring apology but as the very source of its distinctive pedagogical power.

**Answering the charge that poetry corrupts.** Sidney addresses the further charge that poetry corrupts its readers, defending literature's moral value against Puritan suspicion with unusual philosophical seriousness and rigor.

**Praising Chaucer and mocking contemporary plays.** Sidney turns to the state of English writing specifically, praising Chaucer and the old ballad of Chevy Chase while mocking the disorder he finds in contemporary English drama.
