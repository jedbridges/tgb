## Overview

About a hundred and fifty pages, George Bernard Shaw's comedy, first performed in 1905. Barbara Undershaft, a major in the Salvation Army, is the daughter of the millionaire armaments maker Andrew Undershaft, whom she has not seen since childhood. He agrees to visit her East End shelter if she will visit his cannon works.

When the Army accepts money from a distiller and from Undershaft himself, Barbara's faith collapses; at the model town of Perivale St Andrews her fiancé, a Greek scholar, agrees to inherit the business and Barbara resolves to save souls among the well-fed. Shaw's argument that poverty is the worst of crimes.

## What happens

**Barbara and Undershaft's exchange.** Barbara Undershaft, a Salvation Army major who has not seen her father since childhood, arranges an exchange of visits, he to her East End shelter, she to his considerable cannon works.

**The Army's East End shelter.** Barbara's work at the Salvation Army shelter establishes her genuine religious commitment to saving souls among London's poorest residents, the mission that will soon face a genuine institutional crisis.

**Undershaft's cannon works.** Barbara visits her father's cannon works, encountering the industrial machinery and considerable wealth that fund his own life, directly confronting the economic reality underlying her family's fortune.

**The Army accepts tainted money.** The Salvation Army accepts money from a distiller and from Undershaft himself, exposing the charitable institution's uncomfortable dependency on exactly the industries producing considerable social harm.

**Barbara's faith collapses.** Barbara's faith collapses upon discovering this funding arrangement, her spiritual mission revealed as dependent on the capitalist machinery she had believed herself working against.

**The resolution at Perivale St Andrews.** At the model town of Perivale St Andrews, Barbara's fiancé agrees to inherit Undershaft's business, and Barbara resolves to save souls among the well-fed rather than the poor.

## How to read it

Read Shaw's provocative argument that poverty is the worst of crimes as inverting conventional moral hierarchy, treating economic deprivation itself as society's central ethical failure requiring urgent remedy rather than wealth accumulation alone.

Pay close attention to how the Salvation Army accepting money from a distiller and from Undershaft himself stages a genuine institutional crisis, charitable good works funded by the very industries producing considerable social harm.

Notice how Barbara's faith collapsing upon discovering this funding source demonstrates moral idealism's genuine vulnerability to economic reality, her spiritual mission revealed as dependent on exactly the capitalist machinery she believed herself working against.

Consider how her fiancé's eventual agreement to inherit Undershaft's business, and Barbara's own resolve to save souls among the well-fed, stages Shaw's genuinely counterintuitive conclusion about poverty's moral priority over spiritual rescue alone.

This play's argument sits naturally beside Pygmalion's own concerns with class and transformation: both interrogate what genuinely changes a person's social position, one through armaments money and institutional compromise, the other through speech training and social performance.

## Questions it raises

- Does Shaw's argument that poverty is the worst of crimes successfully justify prioritizing economic remedy over spiritual rescue, or does this position risk excusing the armaments industry's own genuine moral complications simply because it generates wealth capable of addressing poverty?
- How should a reader weigh the Salvation Army's acceptance of tainted money against its genuine charitable mission? Does this funding arrangement fatally compromise the organization's work, or does practical necessity justify accepting resources regardless of their problematic source?
- What does Barbara's faith collapsing upon discovering the Army's funding reveal about the relationship between religious idealism and economic reality more broadly? Does this crisis suggest genuine spiritual commitment inevitably depends on compromises idealists would prefer not to acknowledge?
- How persuasive is the play's resolution, Barbara redirecting her mission toward saving souls among the well-fed? Does this conclusion successfully resolve the moral tensions the play has raised, or does it merely relocate rather than genuinely address them?
- Given that Undershaft's cannon works fund both his own considerable wealth and, indirectly, the Salvation Army's charitable work, what does this uncomfortable connection suggest about the play's broader argument regarding industry's genuine relationship to social welfare and moral responsibility?
- What does this play's continued relevance to debates about charitable funding sources and corporate philanthropy suggest about the durability of Shaw's specific argument? Do contemporary institutions face comparable tensions between mission and funding source Shaw identified over a century ago?
