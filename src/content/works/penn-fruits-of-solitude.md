## Overview

About ninety pages, a collection of some 850 numbered maxims and reflections by William Penn, published anonymously in 1693, with a second series, More Fruits of Solitude, following in 1702. Penn was in hiding from political enemies in England when he wrote and published this first collection.

The maxims are grouped under headings such as Ignorance, Education, Luxury, Friendship, Government and Religion, and offer plain practical advice in the manner of Proverbs, with a Quaker stress on simplicity, honesty, moderation, the inner light and the equal worth of all people.

## What happens

**Written in hiding.** Penn composes and publishes this collection anonymously while actually hiding from political enemies in England, circumstances that give his practical wisdom about simplicity and patience genuine personal urgency rather than merely abstract instruction.

**Maxims on ignorance and education.** Early sections address ignorance and education, offering practical guidance on cultivating genuine understanding and avoiding the specific errors that untutored judgment and inadequate learning typically produce.

**Maxims on luxury and friendship.** Further sections treat luxury and friendship, warning against excessive worldly attachment while celebrating genuine human connection as among life's most valuable and carefully cultivated goods.

**Maxims on government.** A section addresses government specifically, offering Penn's own practical political wisdom, informed by his considerable experience founding and governing Pennsylvania, on how just authority should actually be exercised.

**Maxims on religion.** The collection's religious maxims carry a distinctive Quaker emphasis on the inner light and the equal worth of all people, grounding practical worldly advice in specific theological commitment to universal human dignity.

**More Fruits of Solitude.** A second series, More Fruits of Solitude, follows in 1702, extending Penn's original collection with further practical reflections composed later in his life as an established colonial founder and governor.

## How to read it

Read Penn's decision to write and publish these maxims anonymously while actually hiding from political enemies as giving the collection's practical wisdom about simplicity and honest living genuine urgency from an author whose own circumstances demanded exactly this prudence.

Pay close attention to how organizing some 850 numbered reflections under headings like Ignorance, Education, Luxury, Friendship, Government and Religion gives the collection genuine thematic structure despite its aphoristic form, letting readers approach specific practical domains directly.

Notice how the collection's Quaker emphasis on the inner light and the equal worth of all people grounds its practical advice in a specific theological commitment to universal human dignity, distinguishing it from more purely secular collections of worldly prudence.

Consider Penn's own position as founder of Pennsylvania writing plain practical guidance readers could apply directly to daily conduct; this connects his considerable political and colonial achievement to a sustained, accessible meditation on how ordinary people should actually live.

Read this collection alongside Franklin's own Autobiography to compare two influential Pennsylvania figures' approaches to practical wisdom, one offering numbered maxims grounded explicitly in Quaker theology, the other narrating self-improvement through a more secular, individually focused account.
