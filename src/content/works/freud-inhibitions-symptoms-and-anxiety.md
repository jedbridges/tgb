## Overview

About a hundred pages, a genuinely dense late theoretical work by Sigmund Freud, published in 1926, undertaking a significant, self-critical revision of his own earlier theory of anxiety, one of psychoanalysis's most foundational concepts. Where his earlier theory had held that anxiety represents a direct transformation of dammed-up, unreleased libido, energy that, unable to find its normal outlet due to repression, converts itself into anxious dread, Freud here abandons this earlier hydraulic model outright, proposing instead that anxiety is an active signal the ego itself generates in response to a perceived situation of danger, and that repression, rather than causing anxiety as his earlier theory held, is actually a consequence of it.

This reversal carries genuine theoretical weight beyond mere technical revision, since it recasts the ego from a comparatively passive site where blocked energy happens to convert into dread into an active, anticipatory agent capable of recognizing danger and mobilizing defensive responses, including repression, before that danger actually materializes, a considerably more purposeful and psychologically sophisticated role than Freud's earlier theory had assigned it.

## The argument

**Reversing the earlier theory.** Freud opens by directly confronting and abandoning his own earlier published account of anxiety as transformed libido, proposing instead that anxiety functions as a signal actively produced by the ego in response to a perceived danger, whether that danger originates from external reality or from internal instinctual pressure the ego judges itself unable to safely manage.

**Repression as consequence, not cause.** Following directly from this reversal, Freud argues that repression, the ego's active exclusion of dangerous instinctual material from conscious awareness, should now be understood as a consequence of anxiety rather than, as his earlier theory held, anxiety's actual cause, a reversal of causal direction with significant implications for how psychoanalytic treatment should approach the relationship between a patient's felt anxiety and their underlying defensive processes.

**Inhibitions distinguished from symptoms.** Freud develops a more precise conceptual distinction than his earlier, less differentiated terminology had provided, separating inhibitions, a simple, precautionary restriction of some normal ego function undertaken to avoid triggering anxiety, from symptoms, which indicate that some repressed psychical process has partially succeeded in breaking through the ego's defenses despite its efforts, treating these as genuinely distinct clinical phenomena requiring different theoretical accounts.

**Testing the theory against Little Hans and the Wolf Man.** Freud returns directly to two of his own earlier, already published case histories, the phobia of horses he had analyzed in the young patient known as Little Hans, and the considerably more extensive neurosis of the patient known as the Wolf Man, reexamining this existing clinical material to test whether his newly revised signal theory of anxiety can actually account for evidence his original, now-abandoned theory had been specifically constructed to explain.

**The sequence of danger situations.** In one of the essay's most genuinely original theoretical contributions, Freud traces a developmental sequence of danger situations that anxiety responds to across the course of psychological growth, beginning with the trauma of birth itself, understood as the prototype for all later anxiety, through the young child's specific fear of losing the mother, later castration anxiety during the Oedipal period, and finally, in fully developed adulthood, anxiety before the superego's own internalized moral condemnation, showing how the specific object anxiety responds to changes considerably across development even as the ego's basic underlying signal function remains structurally constant throughout.

## How to read it

Read Freud's reversal of his own earlier anxiety theory as a genuine act of theoretical self-correction rather than as mere elaboration, and consider what this willingness to abandon and reverse a previously published, foundational claim suggests about Freud's own relationship to his developing theoretical system across his working career.

Pay close attention to how directly Freud tests his revised theory against his own earlier clinical case material, Little Hans and the Wolf Man, since this direct return to existing evidence, rather than simply asserting the new theory's superiority in the abstract, models a genuine, if informal, form of theoretical accountability to clinical observation.

Notice how the developmental sequence of danger situations, birth, loss of the mother, castration, superego condemnation, gives the revised theory considerably more flexibility than the earlier, more mechanically hydraulic account of anxiety as simply transformed libido, allowing anxiety's changing object across a person's actual psychological development to be tracked and explained rather than treated as a single undifferentiated phenomenon.

Consider the essay's genuine technical density and its assumption of considerable prior familiarity with Freud's broader theoretical system as relevant context for approaching it; this is not an introductory text, and readers unfamiliar with Freud's basic concepts and his earlier anxiety theory specifically may find the essay's argument difficult to follow without that prior grounding.

Read this essay alongside Freud's earlier case histories of Little Hans and the Wolf Man directly, if accessible, to see the original clinical material this later theoretical revision is actually working to reinterpret, giving the abstract theoretical argument concrete clinical grounding.
