## Overview

About three hundred pages, published in 1953, two years after Wittgenstein's death, and written against his own earlier Tractatus. In numbered remarks rather than chapters, he attacks the assumption that words work by naming things and that meaning is a mental accompaniment of speech. Meaning is use, learned in the practices he calls language games; rule-following cannot be secured by any further interpretation; a private language would have no criterion of correctness; and philosophical problems are confusions to be dissolved rather than theories to be built.

Part two, on seeing aspects and the inner life, includes the duck-rabbit and the remark that the human body is the best picture of the soul.

## What happens

**Augustine's picture.** The book opens with Augustine's account of learning to speak by having objects pointed out and named, and shows through the builders' language of slabs and blocks that naming is only one of countless things words do.

**Language games and family resemblance.** Wittgenstein multiplies the uses of language, giving orders, describing, reporting, joking, praying, and argues that they have no common essence but a family resemblance, and that the demand for a definition is a demand the practice does not need.

**Understanding and rules.** Understanding is not a mental process accompanying use, and following a rule, as in continuing a series, cannot be secured by any interpretation of the rule, since interpretations can themselves be interpreted; a rule is followed within a practice, blindly, as we have been trained.

**The private language argument.** A language whose words referred to private sensations, known only to its speaker, would have no criterion for using a word correctly and so no distinction between seeming right and being right; the beetle in the box illustrates that the private object drops out of the language game.

**The inner and the outer.** Wittgenstein examines thinking, imagining, intending, remembering and pain, and argues that the inner life is not hidden behind behaviour but expressed in it, within the forms of life we share.

**Seeing aspects.** The second part turns to the duck-rabbit and the experience of seeing something as something, to the way a face can suddenly look different, and to the relation between perception and interpretation, and remarks that the human body is the best picture of the human soul.

## How to read it

Read the opening quotation from Augustine as the picture the whole book is written against, and notice how the builders' language shows that naming is one use of words among many.

Pay close attention to the remark that meaning is use, and to the qualification, for a large class of cases though not all, that most quotations leave out.

Notice the rule-following sections and their conclusion that a rule is followed blindly within a practice, and decide whether this is a solution or the deepest problem in the book.

Consider the beetle in the box and the private language argument, and ask what remains of the inner life once its objects have dropped out of the language game.

Read this book alongside Descartes's Meditations to see the picture of the mind as an inner theatre stated with full conviction, and then to watch the Investigations try to take it apart.

## Questions it raises

- Is meaning use, and if so, what stops any use from counting, and who decides which uses are correct?
- Does the rule-following argument show that rules are grounded in practice, or that nothing grounds them at all, and what is the difference?
- Does the private language argument prove that there could be no private language, or only that we could not know one if there were, and does the distinction matter?
- Is philosophy really the dissolving of confusions rather than the building of theories, and is the Investigations itself a theory of what philosophy is?
- What does it mean that the human body is the best picture of the human soul, and does the second part of the book answer the questions about the inner life that the first part raises?
- Why is a book of numbered remarks, without a thesis stated anywhere, the most influential work of philosophy in the twentieth century, and what would be lost if it were written as a treatise?
