## Overview

About forty pages, a short story by Ernest Hemingway, first published in Cosmopolitan in 1936 and set on an African safari in Kenya. Francis Macomber, a wealthy American, has run from a wounded lion in front of his wife Margot and their professional hunter Robert Wilson, a public act of cowardice Margot punishes that same night by openly spending it with Wilson while Macomber lies awake nearby, powerless to object, establishing from the story's opening the actual balance of power within their marriage.

The following day, hunting buffalo, Macomber discovers that his fear has entirely vanished and feels, for what the story explicitly calls the first time in his life, genuinely happy, a transformation Hemingway ties directly to Macomber finally meeting physical danger without flinching. As a wounded bull charges him, Margot fires from the car and kills her husband, and the story deliberately leaves it to the reader whether she meant to save him or kill him.

## What happens

**Macomber's cowardice.** The story opens in the aftermath of Macomber running from a wounded lion in front of his wife Margot and their professional hunter Robert Wilson, a public humiliation that establishes both the physical danger of the safari setting and the specific dynamic of fear and judgment that will structure the rest of the story.

**Margot's punishment.** That same night, Margot punishes her husband's cowardice openly, leaving their tent to spend the night with Wilson while Macomber lies awake nearby, powerless to object or retaliate, a punishment that reveals the marriage's actual balance of power depends specifically on Macomber's demonstrated fear.

**Hunting buffalo.** The following day, the party goes out to hunt buffalo, and Macomber discovers partway through the hunt that his fear has entirely and unexpectedly vanished, replaced by a calm, active engagement with physical danger he has never previously experienced.

**Macomber's transformation and happiness.** Macomber feels, for what the story explicitly calls the first time in his life, genuinely happy, a happiness Hemingway ties directly and specifically to his newfound courage rather than to any more conventional source of contentment, marking a complete transformation from the story's opening humiliation.

**The charging buffalo and Margot's shot.** As a wounded buffalo bull charges directly at Macomber, now standing his ground rather than fleeing, Margot fires a rifle shot from the car and kills her husband, a shot the story presents with complete factual clarity about its physical outcome while withholding any clear indication of Margot's actual intention.

**The unresolved ambiguity.** The story ends without resolving whether Margot's shot was meant to save Macomber from the charging animal or to kill him now that his sudden courage threatened to upend the marriage's established balance of power, leaving this central ambiguity permanently unresolved rather than answered by any subsequent narrative development.

## How to read it

Read Macomber's transformation from cowardice to courage as the story's actual emotional center, and notice how precisely Hemingway ties his newfound happiness to the specific disappearance of fear in a single decisive moment rather than to any more gradual or conventional process of character development.

Pay close attention to how directly Margot's punishment of Macomber's cowardice establishes the marriage's actual mechanics of power, a dynamic the story suggests depends specifically on her husband's fear, making his sudden courage the following day a genuine threat to the relationship's established equilibrium rather than simply a personal triumph.

Notice how completely Hemingway's spare, understated prose style refuses to resolve Margot's final intention explicitly; the story gives readers every physical fact needed to reconstruct exactly what happened while deliberately withholding the one psychological fact, her actual motive, that would settle the story's central question.

Consider both possible readings of Margot's shot, accidental or deliberate, as equally supportable by the story's actual evidence, and resist the temptation to settle on one interpretation as simply correct; the story's genuine achievement lies in sustaining this ambiguity rather than secretly favoring one resolution over the other.

Read this story alongside Conrad's Heart of Darkness to compare how differently two major writers treated the African setting as a testing ground for European and American masculine identity, courage and moral character under conditions of genuine physical danger.
