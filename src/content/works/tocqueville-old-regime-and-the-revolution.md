## Overview

About three hundred pages, Tocqueville's last book, published in 1856 and written from the archives of the intendants, argues that the French Revolution completed work the monarchy had already begun. Centralisation, the destruction of local liberty, the reduction of the nobility to a caste with privileges but no function, the separation of classes and the growing equality of Frenchmen under a single administration were all in place before 1789.

What the Revolution did was to sweep away the debris and finish the job, which is why the administrative state emerged from it stronger than before. Along the way he explains why the peasants who owned land resented the old taxes most, and why reform made revolt more likely.

## What happens

**What the Revolution was and was not.** Tocqueville opens by clearing away contemporary judgments, arguing that the Revolution was not anti-religious or anarchic in its essence but a political and social revolution that, like a religion, spoke to all mankind, and whose real object was to destroy feudal institutions already dying everywhere.

**Administrative centralisation.** From the archives he shows that the crown's intendants and the royal council already governed France in detail, that Paris had swallowed the provinces, and that local self-government survived only as empty form.

**The nobility without a function.** He shows how the nobles had lost their governing role to the intendants while keeping their exemptions and dues, so that they were resented as a caste rather than obeyed as a ruling class, and how the classes had drawn apart into mutual ignorance.

**Equality before liberty.** Frenchmen had become alike under one administration, with the same ideas and the same grievances, but without the habit of acting together, which Tocqueville sees as the fatal combination.

**The men of letters and the physiocrats.** Excluded from politics, writers turned to abstract theories of government, and the physiocrats imagined a democratic despotism run by an enlightened state, both preparing minds for what came.

**Why then.** In the last book Tocqueville explains that prosperity was rising, that Louis XVI's reforms had raised expectations and loosened old restraints, and that a regime is most in danger when it begins to mend its ways, so that the Revolution came not from despair but from hope.

## How to read it

Read the argument for continuity as the book's central provocation, the Revolution completing the monarchy's work rather than breaking with it, and notice how much of both the revolutionary and the reactionary story it overturns.

Pay close attention to the use of the intendants' archives, and to how the daily paperwork of the old state becomes evidence in a way memoirs and speeches could not.

Notice the account of the nobility reduced to a caste with privileges but no function, and ask whether it explains aristocracies losing legitimacy elsewhere.

Consider the paradox of reform making revolt more likely, and test it against revolutions Tocqueville never saw.

Read this book alongside Democracy in America to see the same mind asking what equality without liberty makes of a people, first in a nation that grew up equal and then in one that was made so.

## Questions it raises

- If the Revolution only completed what the monarchy had begun, why was it so violent, and does Tocqueville's account of continuity leave room for the Terror?
- Is the paradox that reform provokes revolt a general law, or a description of one regime that lost its nerve at the wrong moment?
- What does it mean for a people to become equal before they become free, and does Tocqueville think France ever recovered from that order of events?
- How much of the argument depends on the intendants' archives, and what might a history written from other records, the peasants' own or the parlements', have shown instead?
- Why does Tocqueville blame the men of letters for abstract politics, and is the charge that intellectuals excluded from power grow dangerous one that applies beyond France?
- What did Tocqueville intend for the sequel he did not live to write, and does the finished volume explain the Revolution or only its preconditions?
