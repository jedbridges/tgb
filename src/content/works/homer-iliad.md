## Overview

The *Iliad* covers a few weeks near the end of a ten-year war, and it leaves out the war's most famous episodes. There is no judgement of Paris, no wooden horse, no fall of the city. What it covers instead is a quarrel between two Greeks and everything that follows from it. That narrowing is the poem's first decision and its most important one: it is not a history of a war but a study of what war does to the people inside it.

Two things make the poem strange to a modern reader. The first is the similes. Battle keeps stopping so that a dying man can be compared to a felled poplar, a mule train, a mother shielding her child. These are not decoration. They are the poem's way of keeping the ordinary peaceful world in view while men destroy each other, and the contrast is the argument. The second is the gods, who quarrel, cheat and take sides, and who are also the poem's way of saying that the universe is not on anyone's side. They are immortal, so nothing they do costs them anything. Only mortals can act meaningfully, because only mortals can lose.

## What happens

**Books 1 to 8.** Agamemnon is forced to give up a captive, Chryseis, and compensates himself by taking Briseis from Achilles. Achilles, publicly dishonoured, withdraws his contingent and asks his goddess mother Thetis to persuade Zeus to let the Trojans win until the Greeks understand what they have lost. The poem catalogues the ships, stages a duel between Paris and Menelaus that should have ended the war and doesn't, gives Diomedes a day of glory, and follows Hector home to Troy, where his baby son is frightened by the horsehair crest on his helmet.

**Books 9 to 17.** An embassy offers Achilles a fortune to return and he refuses it, in a speech that rejects the whole economy of honour the poem runs on. The Greeks are driven back behind their wall; Hector breaks through it. Patroclus, unable to watch, persuades Achilles to lend him his armour, drives the Trojans back, and is killed by Hector. The armies fight over his corpse for an entire book.

**Books 18 to 24.** Achilles learns of the death and his grief is terrible. Hephaestus forges him new armour, including a shield that carries the whole world he has given up: a city at peace, a wedding, a harvest, a trial. He returns, kills without mercy, chokes a river with bodies, kills Hector, and drags him behind his chariot. Then Priam crosses the lines alone at night, and the poem's last book is not a battle but two enemies weeping together and a funeral.

## How to read it

Read it in a translation that keeps the line, and read some of it aloud. The poem was composed to be heard, and its rhythm carries meaning that prose flattens. Lattimore is the most faithful and the one most programs assign; Fagles moves faster; Wilson is the clearest in English metre. Any of the three is a real encounter with the poem.

Do not skip the battle books in the middle. They are where the moral seriousness lives. Homer refuses to let any death be anonymous: a minor Trojan gets a name, a father, a home town, sometimes a bride waiting. That accumulation is the point, and it cannot be summarised.

Watch two patterns. First, what honour actually buys. Almost every character is pursuing it and almost every character is destroyed by it. Second, hands: Achilles' hands kill Hector, and in Book 24 Priam kisses those same hands. The poem is built to bring you to that gesture.

If you must read selectively, take Books 1, 6, 9, 16, 18, 22 and 24. Book 24 is not an epilogue. It is the reason the poem exists, and reading the *Iliad* without it leaves you with a different and much smaller work.

## Questions it raises

- Is Achilles right to withdraw? What does Agamemnon owe him, and what does he owe the army?
- In Book 9 Achilles refuses gifts that would restore his honour. Has he seen through the system, or has he simply lost the ability to be satisfied?
- The poem is scrupulously fair to the Trojans, and Hector is its most sympathetic figure. Is it on anyone's side?
- What is the shield of Achilles doing in the middle of the poem? What does it say about what he is fighting for?
- Why does the poem end with Hector's funeral rather than Achilles' death or the sack of Troy?
- Is Achilles' pity for Priam a change in his character, or the completion of it?
