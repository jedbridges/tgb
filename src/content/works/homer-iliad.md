## Overview

The *Iliad* covers a few weeks near the end of a ten-year war, and it does not include the war's most famous events: no judgement of Paris, no wooden horse, no fall of the city. What it covers instead is a quarrel and its consequences. Agamemnon, forced to give up a captive woman, takes Achilles' prize Briseis to compensate himself. Achilles, dishonoured, withdraws his men from the fighting and asks his goddess mother Thetis to persuade Zeus to let the Trojans win until the Greeks realise what they have lost.

The poem then does something strange and generous. For the next dozen books it turns away from Achilles almost entirely and lives inside the war, on both sides of the wall. It follows Hector home to his wife Andromache and their baby, who is frightened by his father's helmet. It gives long, exact, terrible descriptions of how men die, and often tells you where each one came from and who is waiting for him. When Achilles' friend Patroclus is killed wearing his armour, the poem swings back to its hero and stays with him to the end.

## What happens

Books 1 to 8: the quarrel; the catalogue of ships; the duel of Paris and Menelaus that should have ended the war and doesn't; Diomedes' day of glory; Hector's return to Troy; the first Greek reverses.

Books 9 to 17: the embassy to Achilles, who refuses to return even for a fortune in gifts; the night raid; the Greeks driven back to the ships; Hector breaking through the wall; Patroclus persuading Achilles to let him fight in his armour, and dying under Hector's spear; the battle over his body.

Books 18 to 24: Achilles' grief; Hephaestus forging him new armour, including a shield that carries the whole peaceful world he has left; his return; the slaughter in the river; the killing of Hector and the dragging of his body; the funeral games for Patroclus; and Priam's night journey to Achilles' tent, where the two enemies weep together.

## How to read it

Read it aloud where you can, or at least read it in a translation that keeps the line. The similes are not decoration; they are the poem's way of keeping the ordinary world in view while men are dying, and they reward slowing down. Do not skip the battle books in the middle. They are where the poem's moral seriousness lives, in the refusal to let any death be anonymous.

Keep track of what the gods are doing and notice how little it changes the human stakes. Zeus knows how it ends and the humans mostly suspect. The question the poem asks is not whether Achilles can escape his fate but what he will do inside it.

If you have to choose books, read 1, 6, 9, 16, 18, 22 and 24. That is the spine of the story, and Book 24 is the reason the poem exists.

## Questions it raises

- Is Achilles right to withdraw? What does he owe Agamemnon, and what does Agamemnon owe him?
- The poem is scrupulously fair to the Trojans. Is it on anyone's side?
- What does the shield of Achilles, with its cities at peace and at war, tell us about what he is fighting for?
- Why does the poem end with Hector's funeral rather than Achilles' death or the fall of Troy?
- Is Achilles' pity for Priam a change of character or the completion of one?
