## Overview

About a hundred and ten pages, roughly three thousand two hundred lines of alliterative Old English verse, composed by an unknown poet at an uncertain date somewhere between the eighth and eleventh centuries, and preserved in a single surviving manuscript, the Nowell Codex, now held in the British Library, which itself narrowly survived a fire in 1731 that damaged its edges and destroyed a number of other early English texts entirely. The poem's authorship, exact date and place of composition remain genuinely unresolved scholarly questions; what is certain is that it was written down by a Christian scribe, working in a Christian England, about events and a hero belonging to a pre-Christian Scandinavian past.

That gap between the poem's setting and its composition is worth registering before reading. Beowulf, despite its central place in the canon of English literature, is not set in England at all: its hero is a prince of the Geats, a people in what is now southern Sweden, and the action takes place mainly in Denmark, among Danes and Geats whose world, feuds, treasures, mead-halls and monster-haunted wilderness the poem renders with total narrative confidence despite the poet's own considerable historical and religious distance from it. Seamus Heaney's widely used verse translation, the version this entry follows, addresses this directly in its introduction, framing his own work translating an old Anglo-Saxon poem partly through his experience as an Irish poet working in and against the English literary tradition, a doubled relationship to inherited language that Heaney found genuinely resonant with the poem's own layered, culturally mixed origins.

## What happens

**Grendel.** In Denmark, King Hrothgar has built a magnificent hall, Heorot, to celebrate his people's prosperity and to reward his warriors, but the hall's nightly feasting and joy draw the murderous attention of Grendel, a monstrous, man-shaped creature descended, the poem tells us, from the biblical Cain, who begins raiding Heorot at night, killing and devouring Hrothgar's warriors, and terrorizing the Danes for twelve years without any successful resistance.

Beowulf, a young warrior of the Geats renowned already for exceptional strength, hears of the Danes' suffering and sails across the sea with a company of his own men to offer his help, arriving at Heorot and formally requesting the chance to fight Grendel himself. That night, waiting in the hall, Beowulf, refusing to use any weapon since Grendel fights unarmed, grapples the monster bare-handed when it attacks, and in a ferocious struggle tears off Grendel's arm at the shoulder, a wound from which the creature flees, mortally injured, to die in its lair.

**Grendel's mother.** The Danes' celebration is short-lived: Grendel's mother, enraged by her son's death, raids Heorot the following night, killing one of Hrothgar's most trusted counselors in retaliation. Beowulf, undeterred, follows her trail to a terrifying mere, a lake whose waters burn and whose depths conceal her underwater lair, and dives in alone, fighting her in single combat beneath the water using an ancient sword he finds in her hoard when his own weapon fails against her, ultimately beheading both her and Grendel's already-dead body before returning triumphant to the surface with Grendel's head as proof.

Hrothgar rewards Beowulf lavishly with treasure and delivers a long speech of counsel, warning him against the dangers of pride and reminding him that strength and fortune, however great, are always finally subject to fate and mortality, a warning whose full weight the poem will not cash out until its final section.

**Fifty years later: the dragon.** The poem makes an abrupt and deliberate temporal leap, skipping ahead fifty years to Beowulf's own old age. He has ruled the Geats as king for decades, wisely and successfully, when a thief steals a single cup from a dragon's ancient, long-guarded treasure hoard, provoking the enraged creature to begin burning the countryside, including Beowulf's own royal hall, in retaliation.

Beowulf, now an old man, resolves to face the dragon himself, as he faced Grendel in his youth, though the poem makes clear from the outset, through repeated foreboding and elegiac digression, that this fight will end very differently. In the battle, Beowulf's sword fails him, his companions, save for one young warrior named Wiglaf, flee in terror rather than help their king, and Beowulf, though he and Wiglaf together manage to kill the dragon, is mortally wounded by its venomous bite in the process.

**Death and funeral.** Dying, Beowulf asks to see the dragon's treasure one final time, and, satisfied that he has won it for his people even at the cost of his own life, names Wiglaf his successor before dying. Wiglaf then bitterly rebukes the warriors who fled, warning that without Beowulf's protection the Geats now stand exposed to their old enemies, and a messenger delivers an extended prophecy of the wars, invasions and destruction that will follow now that the kingdom's protector is gone.

The poem closes with Beowulf's funeral: his body is burned on a great pyre, and his people build a barrow, a burial mound, over his ashes and treasure on a headland visible to sailors, and twelve warriors ride around it, mourning him and speaking, in the poem's final lines, of him as the mildest and most gentle of men, the kindest to his people, and the most eager for fame, a judgment that holds together, without apparent contradiction, both his gentleness and his consuming desire for glory.

## How to read it

Read Seamus Heaney's translation if you are reading in English; it preserves much of the alliterative pulse and compressed, kenning-rich diction of the original Old English while remaining genuinely readable, and most facing-page editions let you glance at the Old English itself even without knowing the language, to get some sense of the poem's actual sound.

Notice the poem's structure of deliberate temporal compression and expansion: the Grendel and Grendel's mother episodes occupy roughly the first two-thirds of the poem in considerable narrative detail, while the fifty intervening years of Beowulf's kingship are dispatched in a handful of lines before the dragon fight, a structural choice that emphasizes exactly the contrast the poem wants: youthful triumph against age and mortality.

Pay attention to the digressions, the accounts of earlier feuds, other kings, other treasures and other disasters that interrupt the main narrative at several points. These are not padding; they place Beowulf's own story inside a much larger world of Germanic legend and history, and they consistently reinforce the poem's central themes of fate, glory and the fragility of any peace or prosperity, however hard-won.

Watch for the blending of pagan and Christian elements throughout, sometimes uneasy, sometimes seamless: a narrator who refers to God's providence and to biblical figures like Cain, layered over a fundamentally pre-Christian world of wyrd, fate, and heroic, honor-driven values that owe nothing to Christian doctrine. Rather than trying to resolve this tension into one consistent worldview, read it as a genuine feature of a poem written by a Christian poet deeply engaged with, and unwilling to simply discard, an inherited pagan heroic tradition.

Read the ending as the poem's real center of gravity rather than as an afterthought following the more famous monster fights. The dragon episode, with its old, isolated hero, his fleeing retainers, his hard-won but costly victory, and the extended foreboding about what will follow his death, is where the poem's meditation on glory, mortality and the fragility of political order actually lands.

## Questions it raises

- Why does the poem give its hero an easy, nearly effortless triumph in youth and a costly, fatal struggle in old age, rather than making both fights comparably difficult?
- What is the poem's actual attitude toward Beowulf's pursuit of fame? Is it celebrated without reservation, or does the poem's persistent foreboding suggest real ambivalence about what that pursuit costs?
- How should a reader hold together the poem's pagan concept of wyrd, fate, with its narrator's occasional invocations of Christian providence, without simply picking one framework and discarding the other?
- The poem closes with a prophecy of war and ruin now that Beowulf is dead. What does this ending suggest about whether individual heroism can actually secure lasting political order?
- Beowulf's own retainers flee the dragon fight except for Wiglaf. What does the poem think this reveals about the difference between inherited loyalty and loyalty actually earned in the moment it is tested?
- Is Beowulf's decision to fight the dragon himself, at his age and as king, wisdom, pride, or an inseparable mixture of both, and does the poem give you enough to decide which?
