## Overview

About a hundred and ten pages, a comedy by Oliver Goldsmith, first performed in London in March 1773 and an immediate popular success that has remained in continuous theatrical production ever since, one of only a small handful of Georgian comedies still regularly staged rather than studied primarily as historical curiosity. Goldsmith wrote the play deliberately against the sentimental comedy dominating the contemporary Georgian stage, a genre built around tearful moral improvement and characters whose excessive virtue often left little room for genuine comic energy, aiming instead for what Goldsmith and contemporaries termed laughing comedy, built around genuine wit, mistaken identity and comic misunderstanding rather than moral instruction.

The play's central comic mechanism depends on a single elegant device: Young Marlow, painfully and paralyzingly shy in the presence of gentlewomen of his own social rank while perfectly at ease and even flirtatious with women he considers social inferiors, is tricked by the prankster Tony Lumpkin into believing that the Hardcastle family home, where he has actually traveled specifically to court Kate Hardcastle, is instead a country inn, a misdirection that transforms his behavior toward both Kate and her father for the play's entire central action.

## What happens

**Tony's prank.** Tony Lumpkin, the mischievous stepson of Mr. Hardcastle and cousin to Constance Neville, encounters the traveling Marlow and his companion Hastings and, delighting in the opportunity for mischief, deliberately misdirects them, convincing both men that the Hardcastle family home, their actual intended destination, is instead a country inn where they should expect to be treated as paying guests.

**Marlow's mistaken behavior.** Arriving at what he believes is an inn, Marlow treats Mr. Hardcastle, his own prospective father-in-law, with an easy, presumptuous familiarity entirely inappropriate to their actual relationship, ordering him about and generally behaving as though addressing an innkeeper rather than a gentleman whose daughter he has specifically traveled to court, producing sustained confusion and mounting comic frustration on Hardcastle's part.

**Kate's disguise.** Kate Hardcastle, observing how completely and painfully Marlow freezes with shyness whenever he believes he is addressing a lady of his own social rank, contrasted with his easy, confident manner toward women he takes to be social inferiors, deliberately disguises herself as a barmaid specifically to draw out the genuine, unguarded conversation Marlow reserves for women outside his own class, the strategic decision that gives the play both its title and its central romantic solution.

**Constance and Tony's secondary plot.** Running alongside the main Marlow-Kate storyline, Tony assists his cousin Constance Neville in a scheme to elope with her own jewels and with Hastings, her actual romantic choice, evading the plans of Tony's mother Mrs. Hardcastle, who has been scheming to keep Constance and her considerable fortune within the family through an unwanted arranged marriage to Tony himself, a match neither Tony nor Constance actually wants.

**Resolution.** Both plots untangle within the course of a single eventful night, Marlow's actual social position and his genuine feelings for Kate become clear once the confusion about the inn is finally resolved, and Constance and Hastings successfully secure both her jewels and Mrs. Hardcastle's eventual, reluctant consent to their own match, bringing the entire play's accumulated comic confusion to a single satisfying, comprehensive resolution.

## How to read it

Read the play's central comic device, Marlow's radically different behavior toward perceived social equals versus perceived social inferiors, as doing genuine thematic work rather than functioning as mere farcical plot mechanism; the play uses this device to expose how thoroughly class performance shapes even apparently spontaneous romantic and social behavior.

Pay close attention to Kate's strategic decision to disguise herself as a servant as the play's genuine intellectual center, an active, deliberate romantic strategy rather than passive waiting for Marlow's courtship to somehow succeed on its own terms, giving the play's female lead real agency in resolving a romantic plot her own more socially conventional behavior had initially stalled.

Notice how consistently the play positions itself against sentimental comedy's moral earnestness, favoring genuine wit, comic misunderstanding and theatrical energy over tearful moral improvement, a generic choice worth tracking directly through the play's sustained comic momentum and its relative lack of explicit moralizing commentary.

Consider the secondary Constance and Tony plot as more than mere comic filler alongside the main Marlow-Kate storyline; this parallel plot extends the play's broader interest in characters actively maneuvering around imposed social and familial expectation to secure their own genuine romantic preferences.

Read this play alongside other Georgian comedies of manners, particularly Sheridan's The School for Scandal, written a few years later, to see how directly Goldsmith's laughing comedy helped establish an alternative to sentimental comedy that subsequent playwrights would continue developing.

## Questions it raises

- Does Marlow's radically different behavior toward perceived social equals and perceived inferiors represent a genuine character flaw the play asks its audience to find troubling, or does the play treat this divided behavior primarily as comic material to be resolved happily rather than as a serious critique of class-based social performance?
- How does Kate's active strategy of disguise compare to more conventionally passive female romantic roles in contemporary Georgian comedy? Does her deliberate agency in resolving the play's central romantic obstacle represent a genuinely progressive treatment of female initiative for its period?
- What does the play's sustained comic energy and its relative avoidance of explicit moral commentary suggest about Goldsmith's own understanding of comedy's proper social function, compared to the sentimental comedy tradition he was writing directly against?
- How does the secondary Constance and Tony plot, involving elopement and evading an unwanted arranged marriage, relate thematically to the main Marlow-Kate storyline? Do both plots share an underlying interest in characters maneuvering around imposed social expectation to secure genuine personal choice?
- Given the play's continuous theatrical popularity since its 1773 premiere, what specific qualities, sustained wit, clear comic mechanism, satisfying resolution, have allowed it to remain genuinely stageable and entertaining for audiences across more than two centuries, compared to many other Georgian comedies that have not enjoyed comparable lasting popularity?
- Does the play's resolution, in which social confusion is cleared up and conventional marriage still results for both couples, ultimately reaffirm the class hierarchy its central comic device had temporarily disrupted, or does Kate's deliberate strategy of crossing that hierarchy leave a more lasting mark on how the relationship's actual foundation should be understood?
