## Overview

About twelve hundred pages, Samuel Johnson's fifty-two biographical and critical essays on English poets, composed between 1779 and 1781. Originally commissioned by a group of London booksellers as modest prefaces to a collection of English poets' works, the project grew considerably beyond its original commercial purpose in Johnson's hands, becoming a substantial body of biography and literary criticism covering poets from Cowley to Gray that stands now as English literature's first sustained work of literary biography.

Each life mixes considerable narrative of the poet's actual career with an unsparing account of his character and a direct critical judgment of his work, refusing the era's convention of deferential biography that praised subjects wholesale. The longest and most quoted lives, Cowley, with its influential account of the metaphysical poets, Milton, Dryden, Pope, and Savage, Johnson's own impoverished friend from struggling early literary London, show Johnson's critical and biographical method at its fullest development.

## What happens

**Original commission as prefaces.** A group of London booksellers commissions Johnson to write brief biographical prefaces for a planned collection of English poets' works, a modest original purpose that Johnson's actual execution would considerably exceed as individual lives grew into substantial independent essays.

**Fifty-two lives from Cowley to Gray.** Johnson composes fifty-two individual lives spanning English poets from Cowley through Gray, covering a considerable chronological range and combining, for each poet, narrative account of career, assessment of character, and direct critical judgment of the actual poetic work.

**The life of Cowley and metaphysical poetry.** The life of Cowley develops into an extended and influential account of the metaphysical poets, a critical category Johnson defines and evaluates that would shape how subsequent literary criticism classified and discussed this distinct group of seventeenth-century English poets.

**Unhedged critical judgment.** Throughout the collection, Johnson states his critical opinions directly and without diplomatic hedging, criticizing Milton's republican politics while still praising his poetic achievement, and offering pointed, sometimes unexpectedly harsh assessment of poets like Gray whose reputation Johnson's contemporaries generally held in higher regard.

**The life of Savage.** The life of Richard Savage draws on Johnson's own direct personal friendship with Savage from London's struggling early literary scene, combining genuine sympathy and personal knowledge with unflinching honesty about Savage's considerable human failings, producing one of the collection's most personally informed and complex portraits.

**A template for frank literary biography.** Taken together, the fifty-two lives establish a template for honest literary biography that refuses to separate a poet's character from critical evaluation of the actual work, an approach that shaped how subsequent literary biography would treat the relationship between an author's life and their literary achievement.

## How to read it

Read individual lives selectively rather than attempting the entire collection sequentially; Johnson's fifty-two lives vary considerably in length and depth, and the longest, most developed lives, Cowley, Milton, Dryden, Pope and Savage, reward closer attention than the collection's shorter, more perfunctory entries.

Pay close attention to how directly Johnson states critical judgments readers can specifically agree or disagree with rather than diplomatic vagueness; his treatment of Milton's politics and Gray's odes exemplifies a critical directness that remains genuinely useful for developing your own critical response rather than simply accepting received literary reputation.

Notice how the life of Cowley's account of metaphysical poetry functions as genuine literary criticism extending well beyond biographical narrative; Johnson here defines and evaluates an entire poetic category in ways that shaped subsequent critical vocabulary for discussing this specific group of poets.

Consider how the life of Savage's personal knowledge and sympathy, drawn from Johnson's own direct friendship, shapes its particular emotional register compared to lives of poets Johnson knew only through their published work and reputation; this personal connection gives the Savage life a distinctive intimacy worth noticing.

Read this collection alongside Johnson's Preface to Shakespeare to see the same exacting critical method and characteristic directness Johnson brings to literary judgment across considerably different subjects, a single poet's biography here against a single dramatist's entire body of work there.

## Questions it raises

- Does Johnson's refusal to separate a poet's character from critical judgment of their work represent a defensible critical method, or does this approach risk letting personal moral judgment of a poet's life inappropriately color assessment of the actual literary achievement?
- How does Johnson's unhedged critical directness, stating strong opinions about figures like Milton and Gray without diplomatic softening, compare to more contemporary literary criticism's typical approach? Does this directness make Johnson's criticism more useful or more likely to feel dated and overconfident to modern readers?
- What does the considerable growth from modest commissioned prefaces into substantial independent critical and biographical essays suggest about how Johnson understood his own task once he began actually executing it? Did the original commercial purpose meaningfully constrain or shape the final work's actual scope and ambition?
- How does the life of Savage's personal, sympathetic yet unflinching treatment compare to the collection's other lives, where Johnson lacked comparable direct personal knowledge of his subject? Does personal friendship enhance or potentially compromise Johnson's critical objectivity in this specific case?
- Given that the life of Cowley's treatment of metaphysical poetry has proven so influential for subsequent literary criticism, what does this suggest about how effectively biographical writing can also function as genuine literary theory and classification, beyond simply narrating an individual poet's career?
- What does this collection's considerable variation in length and depth across its fifty-two lives suggest about which poets and poetic achievements Johnson himself considered most significant? Does this uneven attention itself constitute a form of critical judgment worth examining alongside Johnson's more explicit critical statements?
