## Overview

A twenty-page essay published in 2017 by the Potawatomi philosopher Kyle Powys Whyte arguing that for Indigenous peoples climate change is not a new crisis but a repetition of colonialism. Settler colonialism, he argues, already worked by disrupting the relationships between Indigenous peoples and their ecosystems, forcing them to adapt rapidly to imposed environmental change, so that anthropogenic climate change intensifies an old injustice rather than starting a new one.

Whyte draws on Anishinaabe examples and on the responsibilities of collective continuance to argue that climate justice must address colonial power and Indigenous self-determination.

## What happens

**The question.** Whyte opens by noticing that Indigenous people often respond to warnings about climate change with a sense of having seen this before, and asks what that déjà vu means.

**Colonialism as environmental change.** He argues that settler colonialism worked by transforming the environments Indigenous peoples depended on, through removal to new lands, the damming and pollution of waters, the destruction of species and the allotment of territory, so that peoples were forced to adapt rapidly to a world they had not chosen.

**The Anishinaabe cases.** Whyte draws on the history of his own and related peoples, the loss of the sturgeon and the wild rice, the flooding of lands, and the disruption of seasonal rounds, to show what imposed environmental change looked like from inside.

**Collective continuance.** He defines a community's capacity to adapt to change while keeping the relationships and responsibilities, to species, waters, seasons and one another, that make it a community, and argues that colonialism attacked exactly this capacity.

**Climate change as repetition.** Anthropogenic climate change, he argues, intensifies the same injustice: it is imposed by others, it disrupts relationships with ecosystems, and it demands adaptation from peoples whose capacity to adapt colonialism already reduced.

**Climate justice.** Whyte concludes that climate justice cannot treat the climate as a problem beginning now, but must address colonial power and support Indigenous self-determination, since the capacity for collective continuance is what both injustices attacked.

## How to read it

Read the title's question as a real one, and notice how the essay answers it by turning a history usually told in terms of land and sovereignty into an environmental one.

Pay close attention to the Anishinaabe examples, and to how a philosophy that begins from a particular place differs from a general theory of justice.

Notice the concept of collective continuance, and ask whether it names something that other accounts of justice leave out.

Consider the conclusion's test for climate policy, that it must address colonial power and self-determination, and apply it to the climate policies you know.

Read this essay alongside Fanon's Wretched of the Earth to compare two accounts of what colonialism does to a people, one written from the war of decolonisation and one from the ecosystems it left behind.

## Questions it raises

- Is climate change really a repetition of colonialism for Indigenous peoples, or does the analogy risk flattening two injustices into one?
- What does it change about climate politics to date the emergency from removal rather than from the industrial age?
- Is collective continuance a concept that applies only to Indigenous communities, or does it name what any community needs to survive change?
- Can climate justice address colonial power and self-determination without ceasing to be climate policy, and what would a policy that met Whyte's test look like?
- What is gained by grounding the argument in Anishinaabe examples, and what is lost for readers who do not share them?
- Why has a short essay in an academic collection become a standard reference, and what does its reception say about who the climate debate had been leaving out?
