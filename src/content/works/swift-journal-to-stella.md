## Overview

About five hundred pages, sixty-five letters that Swift wrote from London between 1710 and 1713, published only in 1766, to Esther Johnson, whom he called Stella, and her companion Rebecca Dingley in Dublin. Written as a nightly diary in a private baby language of pet names and abbreviations, they record Swift's rise to intimacy with the Tory ministers Harley and St John, the pamphlet war he waged for them, the gossip of coffee houses and court, his money worries and his health.

The letters were not written for publication and appeared only after his death.

## What happens

**Writing nightly in a private language.** Swift keeps up the correspondence as a nightly diary, using a private baby language of pet names and abbreviations meant for no eyes but Stella's and Dingley's, an intimacy of form as much as content.

**Rising into the Tory ministry.** The letters record Swift's rise to intimacy with the ministers Harley and St John, tracking his movement from outsider clergyman to trusted political operator across the correspondence's three years.

**Waging the pamphlet war.** Swift describes the pamphlet war he waged for the ministry, the published political writing that made his public reputation shadowed here by the private letters documenting its daily composition.

**Recording coffee-house and court gossip.** The letters relay the gossip of coffee houses and court, situating Swift's political ascent within the ordinary social texture of the London he moved through.

**Confessing money worries and health complaints.** Swift confesses his money worries and his health throughout, the grand political narrative interrupted constantly by the small domestic anxieties of an individual life.

**Publication after death.** The letters were not written for publication and appeared only after Swift's death, a private record read by later generations against its author's original and evidently permanent intention.

## How to read it

Read the letters' nightly diary form, in a private baby language of pet names and abbreviations meant for no eyes but Stella's and Dingley's, as giving access to a Swift unguarded in a way his published satire never is.

Pay close attention to how the letters record Swift's rise to intimacy with the Tory ministers Harley and St John, tracking political events from the inside while their outcome was still genuinely unknown to the writer.

Notice how the pamphlet war Swift waged for the ministry appears here from its private, daily side, dinners and audiences and small favors standing behind the public pamphlets' finished argument.

Consider how the letters were not written for publication and appeared only after Swift's death, a fact that should shape how a reader weighs the intimacy on display against the author's original and unmet expectation of privacy.

Read this correspondence alongside Gulliver's Travels to compare the private, affectionate voice Swift keeps for Stella against the ferocious public voice he directs at humanity at large in his most famous satire.
