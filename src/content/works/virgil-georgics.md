## Overview

About one hundred pages, a didactic poem in four books on farming, finished in 29 BCE, addressed to Maecenas and completed as Octavian was consolidating power. Book one treats crops, weather and the signs of civil war; book two trees and vines, with the praise of Italy; book three cattle and horses, ending in a plague; book four bees and their society, closing with the story of the beekeeper Aristaeus and the descent of Orpheus to recover Eurydice.

The poem's subject is labour, and the cost of the order it wins from nature.

## What happens

**Book one: crops and weather.** Virgil invokes the rural gods and Octavian, sets out the ploughing, sowing and tending of grain, explains that Jupiter ended the golden age so that need would sharpen men's wits, teaches the signs of weather from the sun and moon, and ends with the portents at Caesar's death and a prayer for the young ruler.

**Book two: trees and vines.** The methods of propagating trees lead to the praise of Italy, land of perpetual spring, then to soils, the planting of vines and olives, and close with the praise of the farmer's life, blessed if he only knew his blessings, set against the poet's own wish to understand the causes of things.

**Book three: cattle and horses.** The breeding of horses and cattle, the fury of love in animals and men, and the tending of sheep and goats give way at the end to a plague in Noricum that kills the herds, the ox dying at the plough and the dog going mad.

**Book four: the bees.** The bees' hive is a model commonwealth, with its king, its selfless workers, its battles and its swarms, and Virgil teaches how to keep them and how to restore a dead hive from the carcass of a bullock.

**Aristaeus and Orpheus.** The beekeeper Aristaeus, his bees dead, learns from Proteus that he is punished for the death of Eurydice, whom he pursued; the story within the story tells how Orpheus went down for her, won her with his song, and lost her again by looking back.

**The close.** Aristaeus sacrifices, new bees swarm from the carcasses, and Virgil signs off from Naples, where he flourished in the pursuits of inglorious ease while Caesar thundered in war by the Euphrates.

## How to read it

Read the passage on Jupiter ending the golden age as the poem's theory of civilisation, everything worth having wrested from a nature that resists, and the farmer's year as an image of all effort.

Pay close attention to the close of the first book, where the handbook becomes a poem about the civil wars and the farm the place where Rome might be mended.

Notice how the third book ends in plague and the fourth in the loss of Eurydice, so that the poem's lessons on labour include the labour that fails and the loss that no art recovers.

Consider the bees as a model society and the Orpheus story set inside their lesson, and decide what the poem thinks the one has to do with the other.

Read this poem alongside Lucretius to compare two Latin didactic poems, one that explains the world to free men from fear and one that teaches labour to men who cannot be freed from it.

## Questions it raises

- Is the poem's view of labour hopeful, the discipline that made civilisation, or bleak, the sentence that ended the golden age, and does Virgil decide?
- Why does a poem that praises Octavian as the saviour of a mad world end with a poet at ease in Naples while Caesar thunders abroad?
- What does the plague at the end of book three do to the lessons on breeding that precede it, and why place it there?
- What has Orpheus's loss of Eurydice to do with the death and restoration of Aristaeus's bees, and is the poem's silence on the connection a failure or its meaning?
- Is the hive a model for human society or a warning about what a society without individuals looks like?
- Why did readers from Dryden onward call this the most perfect poem in Latin, and does the judgment survive a reader who has never farmed?
