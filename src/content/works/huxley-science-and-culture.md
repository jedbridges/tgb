## Overview

About twenty pages, an address Thomas Henry Huxley delivered at the opening of Josiah Mason's Science College in Birmingham in 1880, published the following year in Science and Culture, and Other Essays. Huxley used the occasion, marking the founding of a school explicitly dedicated to teaching science rather than classical literature, to challenge directly the entrenched Victorian assumption that only a classical education could actually produce genuine culture.

Answering those who held that classical study alone could cultivate the mind, Huxley argues that training in the methods and results of physical science does so just as well, that the Renaissance humanists who first championed classical learning were themselves rebels against a dead scholastic tradition, and that a man who knows no science is as poorly educated as one who knows no literature. Matthew Arnold replied to Huxley's argument in his own essay, Literature and Science.

## What happens

**The occasion and its stakes.** Huxley delivers his address at the formal opening of Mason College, an institution founded explicitly to teach science, using this specific occasion to defend the new college's founding educational philosophy against contemporary classicist critics who held that only training in Greek and Latin literature could produce genuine cultural formation.

**Challenging the classics-only assumption.** Huxley directly challenges the entrenched Victorian assumption that classical education alone can produce genuine culture, arguing that this assumption rests on an unexamined and increasingly outdated hierarchy privileging literary study over scientific training without adequate justification.

**The Renaissance humanists as rebels.** Huxley turns classical education's own history against its contemporary defenders, arguing that the Renaissance humanists who first championed classical learning were themselves rebels against a dead, exhausted scholastic tradition, suggesting that vigorous intellectual engagement, not any single fixed curriculum, was classical education's original actual justification.

**Science and literature as equally necessary.** Huxley insists directly that a man who knows no science is as poorly educated as one who knows no literature, refusing to treat scientific training as merely practically useful while reserving genuine cultural formation exclusively for classical literary study.

**Matthew Arnold's reply.** Huxley's address prompted a considered response from Matthew Arnold in his own essay, Literature and Science, defending humane letters' distinct and, in Arnold's view, irreplaceable educational value, turning Huxley's address into one side of a genuinely serious ongoing debate about education's actual purpose.

## How to read it

Read Huxley's address as a genuine institutional intervention rather than purely abstract educational philosophy; delivered at the opening of a college founded explicitly around scientific education, the address directly defends this specific new institution's founding premise against contemporary classicist skepticism about its educational legitimacy.

Pay close attention to how Huxley's historical argument about the Renaissance humanists turns classical education's own origin story against its contemporary defenders; if classical learning began as rebellion against dead scholastic tradition, Huxley suggests, then defending any fixed curriculum simply because it is traditional betrays that same tradition's original animating spirit.

Notice how directly Huxley's claim about equivalent educational value, that ignorance of science is as poor an education as ignorance of literature, refuses the common Victorian hierarchy treating scientific training as merely useful rather than culturally formative, a distinction central to the broader debate this address helped crystallize.

Consider Matthew Arnold's reply in Literature and Science as essential context for understanding Huxley's address fully; reading both sides of this exchange reveals a genuinely serious disagreement about education's actual purpose rather than treating Huxley's argument as simply the final word on the matter.

Read this address alongside Whitehead's Aims of Education to compare how differently two influential writers, working roughly half a century apart, addressed related questions about education's proper purpose and the relative value of different kinds of intellectual training.
