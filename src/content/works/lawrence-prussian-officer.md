## Overview

About thirty pages, a short story by D. H. Lawrence, the title piece of his first story collection, published just a few months after the outbreak of the First World War in 1914. A Prussian cavalry captain, cold, aristocratic and unmarried, becomes fixated on his young orderly's easy physical vitality, a quality he cannot bear precisely because he cannot possess or become it himself, and begins tormenting the young man with kicks and accumulated small cruelties.

On a march in oppressive heat, the orderly, half out of his mind from prolonged endurance of this cruelty, suddenly breaks the captain's neck in a wood, wanders afterward in delirium through the mountains, and is found dead. The two bodies are laid side by side in the mortuary, binding captain and orderly together in death as thoroughly as the captain's obsession had bound them in life.

## What happens

**The captain's fixation.** The story opens by establishing the Prussian captain's cold, aristocratic character and his growing, unnamed fixation on his young orderly's easy physical vitality, a quality that draws the captain's attention precisely because it represents something he himself cannot possess or experience directly.

**Accumulated cruelty.** The captain begins tormenting his orderly with kicks and a sustained pattern of petty cruelty, a punishment the story presents as flowing directly from his obsessive fixation rather than from any ordinary disciplinary necessity or legitimate military grievance.

**The march in the heat.** The story's central action unfolds during a military march in oppressive heat, physical conditions that compound the orderly's accumulated suffering under the captain's sustained cruelty and set the stage for the violent eruption that follows.

**The orderly's violence.** Half out of his mind from prolonged endurance, the orderly suddenly breaks the captain's neck in a wood during the march, an act of violence that arrives not as calculated, triumphant retaliation but as a delirious, almost involuntary eruption following sustained psychological pressure.

**Delirious wandering.** Following the killing, the orderly wanders in delirium through the surrounding mountains, his mental state as thoroughly disordered by the encounter as the captain's own obsession had been, before eventually being found dead himself.

**The closing mortuary image.** The story closes with the two men's bodies laid side by side in the mortuary, a final image that binds captain and orderly together in death exactly as the captain's obsession had already bound them together in life, refusing to present the orderly's violence as simple liberation or escape.

## How to read it

Read the captain's obsession with his orderly as genuinely physical and psychologically charged without expecting the story to name this fixation directly; Lawrence lets accumulated kicks and petty cruelty speak for a desire the story's controlled, restrained prose never states outright, a deliberate narrative choice worth noticing throughout.

Pay close attention to how the orderly's climactic violence arrives not as calculated, satisfying retaliation but as a delirious, almost involuntary eruption; this structure refuses to reward the abused party's violence with clear triumph or vindication, complicating any straightforward reading of the story as simple justice delivered.

Notice how thoroughly the story's closing image, the two bodies laid side by side, undoes any clean separation between tormentor and victim; this final juxtaposition suggests the relationship's violence was never simply one person's cruelty directed at another but something closer to mutual, genuinely inescapable destruction.

Consider the story's historical position, published just months after the First World War's outbreak, as relevant to its portrait of Prussian military discipline curdling into obsessive cruelty; this specific psychological pathology would soon play out on a considerably larger scale across the war Lawrence's contemporaries were only beginning to experience.

Read this story alongside Kafka's Metamorphosis to compare how two roughly contemporary writers, working independently in different literary traditions, each used compressed short fiction to examine bodies, control and psychological breakdown within seemingly ordinary institutional or domestic settings.

## Questions it raises

- Does the story's refusal to name the captain's obsession directly, letting accumulated cruelty speak for unstated desire, strengthen its psychological portrait, or does this indirection risk leaving the captain's actual motivation too ambiguous for readers to fully grasp what specifically drives his behavior?
- How should a reader interpret the orderly's violence, arriving as delirious eruption rather than calculated retaliation? Does this framing excuse or complicate his culpability for killing the captain, and does the story ultimately present his act as justified response to sustained abuse?
- What does the closing image of the two bodies laid side by side accomplish that separate, distinct endings for captain and orderly could not achieve? Does this final juxtaposition suggest genuine equivalence between tormentor and victim, or does it work differently than simple moral equation?
- How does the story's specific historical position, published just months after the First World War began, shape your reading of its portrait of military discipline and obsessive cruelty? Does knowing this context add prescient weight to the story, or should it be read independent of this subsequent historical development?
- Given how compressed this story is at only about thirty pages, what does Lawrence accomplish within this short form that a longer treatment of the same material might not achieve? Does the story's brevity intensify its psychological impact or leave certain questions about the characters' motivations underdeveloped?
- What does the orderly's disoriented, delirious wandering after the killing suggest about the actual psychological cost of the violence he commits, even against a genuinely cruel tormentor? Does the story ultimately suggest violence, however provoked, damages the person who commits it as much as the recipient?
