## Overview

The Conics is a treatise in eight books of which seven survive, four in the Greek and three in the ninth-century Arabic translation made by Thabit ibn Qurra in Baghdad. The eighth is lost, though Edmond Halley attempted a reconstruction in 1710. Apollonius worked at Alexandria and Pergamum around 200 BCE, a generation after Archimedes, and was known in antiquity as the Great Geometer.

It is the hardest book on this shelf. Two hundred and sixty pages of Greek geometry with no algebra, no symbols, and no coordinates, in which every relation is expressed as a proportion between rectangles and squares, and every proposition depends on a lettered diagram. Reading it requires drawing.

The honest recommendation is to read the prefaces, which are letters and are wonderful, and then Book I propositions 11 to 14, which contain the central construction and the naming. The programs that assign it, St John's and Thomas Aquinas, do so in tutorial over months, with students reconstructing propositions at a board, and that is really the only way through the rest.

What to know before starting: conics were not new. Menaechmus had discovered them around 350 BCE while working on the duplication of the cube, and Euclid and Archimedes both wrote about them. What Apollonius did was reorganise the whole subject from a better starting point.

## The argument

**The unification.** Before Apollonius, the three curves were obtained by cutting three different cones. You took a cone whose vertical angle was right, acute or obtuse, cut it with a plane perpendicular to a slant side, and got a parabola, an ellipse or a hyperbola respectively. The curves were therefore called the section of a right-angled cone, and so on, and there was no obvious reason why they belonged together.

Apollonius takes one cone, and not necessarily a right one: an arbitrary oblique cone on a circular base. He then varies the cutting plane. If the plane is parallel to exactly one slant side, you get a parabola. If it cuts both sides of the cone on the same side of the vertex, an ellipse. If it is steep enough to meet both nappes, that is, both halves of the double cone extended through the vertex, you get a hyperbola with two branches. The three curves become three cases of one operation, and the hyperbola acquires its second branch, which nobody had had before.

**The symptom, and the names.** For each curve Apollonius establishes a defining relation. Take a diameter of the curve and a point on it; drop an ordinate to the curve; and compare the square on that ordinate with a rectangle built on the intercepted piece of the diameter and a fixed line he calls the upright side, the latus rectum.

For the parabola the square on the ordinate is exactly equal to that rectangle. For the ellipse it falls short of it. For the hyperbola it exceeds it. The Greek for applying an area with a deficiency is elleipsis, for exact application parabole, and for excess hyperbole, and those are the words he gives the three curves. They are still their names in every language.

If you write the relation in modern notation with the diameter as an axis, you get y squared equals px for the parabola and y squared equals px plus or minus a term in x squared for the other two. That is why historians describe the symptom as an equation in oblique coordinates, and why the step to Descartes looks short in retrospect. It was not short. Apollonius has no symbols, no negative quantities, and no notion of a curve as the set of points satisfying a formula; the diameter and ordinate are features of a particular figure, not axes imposed on a plane.

**Books I to IV.** The elementary theory: the sections and their fundamental properties, diameters and conjugate diameters, tangents, the harmonic properties of poles and polars, and the intersections of conics with one another. Book III contains what we would call focal properties, including the sum and difference of the focal distances, though Apollonius never names a focus for the parabola. It also contains the theorems on the three- and four-line locus, and his preface says these are the ones he is proudest of.

**Books V to VII.** These are the advanced books and they are astonishing. Book V is about the longest and shortest straight lines that can be drawn from a point to a conic, which is the theory of normals in disguise, and in working out how many can be drawn from a given point Apollonius effectively determines the evolute of the curve. Nothing else in Greek mathematics approaches this. Book VI treats equal and similar conics, Book VII the properties of conjugate diameters.

## How to read it

Read the prefaces to Books I, IV, V and VII. They are short letters, they explain what he thinks is new, they name the friends the copies were sent to, and one of them mentions that an earlier draft had been circulated before it was ready by someone who got hold of it.

Then read Book I definitions and propositions 11, 12 and 13, drawing the cones and the cutting planes yourself. That is where the three curves are produced and named, and it is the intellectual centre of the whole work.

If you go further, use Taliaferro's translation from Green Lion, which is the one the tutorial colleges use and which has diagrams laid out for study. Heath's Apollonius of Perga is a different thing: a paraphrase of the whole work in modern notation, invaluable for orientation and not a translation.

Learn the vocabulary first. Diameter, ordinate, abscissa, upright side and transverse side all have precise meanings here that are not quite the modern ones, and a proposition is unreadable until they are settled.

Read it after Euclid and before Newton. Books I and III of the Principia assume exactly this material, and Newton's proofs of the orbit theorems are Apollonian geometry applied to a moving body.

## Questions it raises

- Is the symptom an equation? It has the content of one and none of the notation. What does the difference actually amount to?
- Apollonius proves the three-line locus theorems and says Euclid could not. Why did nobody take the next step for eighteen centuries?
- The double cone gives the hyperbola two branches. Why had that not occurred to anyone, and what does it take to see a curve as one object rather than two?
- Book V finds the normals and their envelope. How does mathematics of that sophistication get done without algebra?
- None of this had any application when it was written. What does that suggest about the relation between pure mathematics and its later uses?
- Descartes said he could solve in a few lines what the ancients found hardest. What was gained by the algebraic method, and is anything lost?
