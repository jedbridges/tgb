## Overview

About thirty-five pages, a poem of 744 lines in heroic couplets by Alexander Pope, published anonymously in 1711 when Pope was twenty-three, on how to judge poetry and the faults of critics. Part one argues that good criticism follows nature and the rules the ancients drew from her.

Part two lists the causes of bad judgement, pride, imperfect learning, love of parts rather than the whole, and fashion, with examples of sound echoing sense; part three describes the ideal critic and gives a short history of criticism from Aristotle to Boileau.

## What happens

**Criticism follows nature.** Part one argues that good criticism follows nature and the rules the ancients drew from her, establishing that genuine critical judgment rests on principles discovered in nature rather than arbitrary preference or convention.

**The rules of the ancients.** Pope treats the ancients' inherited critical rules as legitimate discoveries about nature's own patterns rather than external constraints, giving classical authority genuine grounding rather than mere traditional deference.

**Pride as a cause of bad judgment.** Part two identifies pride as a primary cause of bad judgment, critics whose excessive self-regard distorts their capacity to assess poetry fairly and accurately according to proper standards.

**Imperfect learning and partial focus.** Pope catalogues imperfect learning and love of parts rather than the whole as further causes of bad judgment, critics fixating on isolated details while missing a work's genuine overall design and purpose.

**Fashion's distorting influence.** Fashion appears as a further cause of poor critical judgment, contemporary taste and trend distorting critics' capacity to assess a work's actual and lasting merit beyond momentary popularity.

**The ideal critic and history of criticism.** Part three describes the ideal critic's proper qualities and gives a short history of criticism from Aristotle to Boileau, situating Pope's own theoretical claims within an extended inherited critical tradition.

## How to read it

Read Pope's composition of this poem at twenty-three, published anonymously with remarkable confidence and technical polish, as itself demonstrating within the poem's own execution the very judgment and craftsmanship it theoretically prescribes for good criticism and poetry alike.

Pay close attention to how Pope's argument that good criticism follows nature and ancient rules stakes out a considered middle position between purely subjective taste and rigid rule-following, treating inherited principles as genuine discoveries about nature rather than arbitrary constraint.

Notice how the catalogue of bad judgment's causes, pride, imperfect learning, love of parts rather than the whole, and fashion, diagnoses specific and recognizable critical failures that remain instructive well beyond Pope's own early eighteenth-century literary context.

Consider the poem's remarkable density of quotable formulations, a little learning is a dangerous thing, to err is human, to forgive divine, fools rush in where angels fear to tread, phrases that entered English as proverbs whose specific poetic origin has been largely forgotten.

Read this poem alongside Pope's own later Essay on Man to see how the confident, systematic verse essay form developed here for literary criticism specifically gets applied at greater philosophical scope to human nature and providence in that subsequent major work.
