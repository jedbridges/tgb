## Overview

Two hundred and ten pages in three unequal parts. The Window takes about half the book and covers a single afternoon and evening at a holiday house on Skye. Time Passes covers ten years in roughly twenty pages. The Lighthouse covers one morning.

There is almost no plot. A family and their guests are staying by the sea; a six-year-old wants to sail to the lighthouse and his father says the weather will not allow it; ten years later some of them go. Everything else is thought, feeling and the movement between one mind and another.

Woolf's parents were Leslie and Julia Stephen, and the Ramsays are recognisably them: a demanding intellectual husband, a beautiful and endlessly giving wife who died suddenly when Woolf was thirteen. She wrote in her diary that making the book laid them to rest.

## What happens

The Window. Mrs Ramsay tells James, aged six, that they may sail to the lighthouse tomorrow. Mr Ramsay says flatly that the weather will not be fine, and James wants to kill him. Around them are eight children and a collection of guests: Charles Tansley, poor and prickly, who keeps saying women cannot paint or write; Lily Briscoe, who is painting Mrs Ramsay and James and cannot solve the composition; the aged poet Augustus Carmichael; and Paul Rayley and Minta Doyle, whom Mrs Ramsay is quietly manoeuvring into an engagement.

Mr Ramsay is a philosopher who fears he will not last, and who requires reassurance repeatedly and gets it. The section builds to a dinner party where the boeuf en daube is served and, for a few minutes, everything coheres: Mrs Ramsay feels the moment set, and knows it will not last, and knows too that this is what will be remembered. The section ends with the Ramsays alone, and her refusal to say she loves him, and his knowing anyway.

Time Passes. The house is left empty. Wind comes through, plaster falls, books mildew, a shawl unwinds over ten years. In square brackets, almost in passing: Mrs Ramsay dies, Prue marries and dies in childbirth, Andrew is killed by a shell in France. The war is a sound offstage. Mrs McNab, the old cleaner, heaves herself through the rooms and at the last moment saves the house from collapse for a family that is coming back.

The Lighthouse. Mr Ramsay, two of the children and a party finally sail. Lily sets up her easel on the lawn and tries again at the painting she abandoned ten years earlier. Mr Ramsay stands over her demanding sympathy and she cannot give it, and instead says something about his boots. In the boat, James steers well and his father, at last, praises him. On shore Lily sees what the picture needs, draws a line in the centre, and the book ends.

## How to read it

Let go of who is thinking. Woolf moves between consciousnesses without warning, sometimes within a sentence, and the instinct to stop and assign each thought to an owner will make the book unreadable. Read at speed on a first pass and the perspective shifts become audible, like changes of key.

Read Time Passes twice. It is the most radical thing in the book and the easiest to read past. Notice what Woolf puts in the main clause, weather and decay and an empty house, and what she puts in brackets. That inversion is the whole argument about how time treats human importance.

Watch Lily's painting as the plot. The question of whether she can finish it is the only suspense the novel has, and the answer arrives in the final line. The technical problem she keeps returning to, how to connect the two masses, is also the novel's problem.

Notice who makes the dinner work. Woolf is precise about the labour of Mrs Ramsay's sympathy: the effort, the depletion, and the fact that no one sees it as work. Read alongside A Room of One's Own if you can.

The common misreading is that nothing happens. Three people die, a war passes, a marriage is quietly assessed and a work of art is completed. What is missing is not event but explanation: Woolf never tells you how to feel about any of it.

## Questions it raises

- Why are the deaths in brackets? What would be lost if Mrs Ramsay's death were given a scene?
- Lily's painting will be rolled up and forgotten, and she finishes it regardless. Is that an answer to mortality or a refusal of the question?
- Mrs Ramsay gives sympathy continuously and it costs her. Does the novel admire her, pity her, or both?
- Mr Ramsay's praise of James arrives ten years late, in one sentence, and is enough. Why?
- If consciousness really moves like this, what does that do to the idea of a character?
- Woolf said writing this freed her from her parents. Can a novel do that, and does knowing it change how you read the Ramsays?
