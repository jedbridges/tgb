## Overview

About two hundred pages, John Stuart Mill's account of his own formation, published in 1873, the year he died. He describes the extraordinary education his father James Mill gave him, Greek at three and logic at twelve, with no religion and almost no company of other children, a genuinely extreme educational experiment conducted directly on his own son.

He then narrates the mental crisis of his twentieth year, when he realised that reaching all his aims would not make him happy, and his recovery through Wordsworth's poetry and the cultivation of feeling. The later chapters cover his years at the East India Company, his work with the Philosophic Radicals, his long attachment to Harriet Taylor, and how his books came to be written.

## What happens

**The extraordinary education.** Mill describes the extraordinary education his father James Mill gave him, Greek beginning at age three and logic at twelve, conducted with no religious instruction and almost no company of other children, an education deliberately designed to produce exceptional intellectual capacity.

**The mental crisis.** Mill narrates the mental crisis of his twentieth year, when he suddenly realized that achieving all the aims his education had carefully prepared him to pursue would not actually make him happy, a genuine breakdown exposing a limitation in his purely rational upbringing.

**Recovery through Wordsworth.** Mill describes his recovery from this crisis through reading Wordsworth's poetry and the deliberate cultivation of feeling, an intellectual correction he would subsequently build directly into his mature philosophical views.

**Years at the East India Company.** The later chapters cover Mill's considerable professional career at the East India Company, the institutional setting within which he conducted much of his working life alongside his more purely intellectual and philosophical pursuits.

**Work with the Philosophic Radicals.** Mill describes his engagement with the Philosophic Radicals, the reforming political and intellectual movement he was closely associated with throughout much of his active public career.

**Harriet Taylor and the writing of his books.** Mill describes his long attachment to Harriet Taylor, treated with genuine seriousness as intellectual partnership rather than merely romantic footnote, and gives an account of how his major books actually came to be written.

## How to read it

Read Mill's account of his extraordinary childhood education as tracing both remarkable intellectual achievement and genuine human cost with equal honesty; the Autobiography does not simply celebrate his father's experiment but examines directly what this education produced and what it may have foreclosed.

Pay close attention to how the mental crisis exposes a specific limitation within Mill's own inherited utilitarian framework; a life organized entirely around calculated aims had somehow never asked whether the person doing the calculating could actually feel satisfaction in achieving them.

Notice how Mill's recovery through Wordsworth and cultivated feeling connects directly to his subsequent mature philosophy rather than remaining a separate personal episode; this experience shaped his later insistence that feeling and aesthetic experience mattered alongside calculated reasoning.

Consider Mill's treatment of Harriet Taylor as genuine intellectual partnership, worth reading carefully rather than passing over as simple romantic narrative; this relationship's significance for Mill's own intellectual development receives serious, sustained attention in the Autobiography's later chapters.

Read this work alongside Mill's own On Liberty and Subjection of Women to see how the personal experiences and intellectual development traced here in autobiographical form connect directly to the mature philosophical positions Mill developed in his other major published works.

- How does the mental crisis's specific resolution, through Wordsworth's poetry and cultivated feeling, relate to Mill's broader philosophical commitment to utilitarianism? Does this personal recovery represent a genuine correction to utilitarian calculation, or a supplement that leaves the underlying framework fundamentally unchanged?
- What does Mill's serious treatment of Harriet Taylor as genuine intellectual partner suggest about his own understanding of collaborative philosophical work? Does this account adequately establish her specific contributions, or does it remain difficult to assess precisely what she contributed versus what Mill developed independently?
- How should a reader weigh an autobiography written by its subject, particularly one as intellectually self-aware as Mill, against more independent biographical accounts? Does Mill's own retrospective narrative of his mental crisis and recovery shape how subsequent readers have understood this episode in ways worth questioning?
- Given that this Autobiography was published the year Mill died, what does this timing suggest about his own sense of needing to provide a definitive account of his intellectual formation before his death? Does this circumstance shape the text's tone or apparent completeness?
- What does Mill's willingness to expose his mental crisis so directly suggest about his own understanding of intellectual honesty and the proper relationship between personal experience and public philosophical argument? Does this candor distinguish his autobiography from more guarded accounts by other major philosophers of his period?
