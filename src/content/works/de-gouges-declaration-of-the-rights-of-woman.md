## Overview

About fifteen pages, a pamphlet published in September 1791 by Olympe de Gouges, a self-educated playwright and political writer who had become an increasingly prominent voice in French revolutionary public life, structured as a direct, article-by-article rewriting of the 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, the founding document of the French Revolution's claimed universal rights. De Gouges's technique is formally simple and rhetorically devastating: she takes the earlier document's own language, article by article, and inserts woman alongside man, exposing precisely how the supposedly universal principles of the original Declaration had, in actual practice, excluded half of humanity without ever explicitly acknowledging that exclusion.

The pamphlet's dedication to Marie Antoinette, the deeply unpopular queen who would herself be executed within two years of the pamphlet's publication, was a provocative and politically risky choice, urging the queen to use her position to support women's rights rather than treating her simply as a symbol of the old regime's excess. De Gouges herself would be guillotined in November 1793, executed by the same revolutionary government whose founding principles she had insisted, in this pamphlet, be applied with genuine consistency.

## The argument

**Rewriting the Declaration.** De Gouges's central structural move is to take the 1789 Declaration's seventeen articles and rewrite each one, substituting explicitly gendered inclusive language, woman and man, for the original document's supposedly universal but in practice male-only terms, man and citizen. This article-by-article parallel construction makes visible, simply through direct comparison, exactly how much the original Declaration's claimed universality had actually excluded, without requiring de Gouges to construct an entirely separate philosophical argument from scratch; the existing document's own logic, applied consistently, does most of the argumentative work.

**Equal birth and equal rights.** Following the original Declaration's opening claim that men are born and remain free and equal in rights, de Gouges's own opening article declares directly that woman is born free and remains equal to man in rights, establishing from the outset that her document's central demand is consistency rather than an entirely new set of principles, women deserve exactly the rights the Revolution had already claimed to establish universally.

**The scaffold and the rostrum.** The pamphlet's single most quoted and most historically resonant line argues that since women are already held fully accountable under capital law, subject to execution exactly as men are, they must correspondingly possess full political rights, since a legal system willing to hold women to the law's most severe consequences has no coherent basis for denying them participation in making that law. This argument would prove starkly, tragically literal when de Gouges herself was executed less than two years after writing it.

**A direct call to women.** The pamphlet's preamble addresses women readers directly, urging them to recognize their own situation clearly and asking pointedly what genuine advantages the Revolution had actually brought them beyond, in de Gouges's own characterization, more pronounced scorn and more marked disdain, a direct challenge to any complacent assumption that revolutionary change had automatically or naturally extended its benefits to women alongside men.

**Reimagining marriage.** The pamphlet's postscript extends its political argument for equality directly into domestic and economic life, proposing a model marriage contract structured as a genuine partnership holding property jointly and equally between spouses, a direct alternative to the standard legal arrangement of the period, in which a wife's separate property and legal existence were largely absorbed into her husband's upon marriage, showing that de Gouges understood women's equality as requiring transformation of private, domestic legal structures as much as public, political ones.

## How to read it

Read the document side by side with the 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man it rewrites, article by article, since the pamphlet's rhetorical force depends almost entirely on this direct structural parallel; reading de Gouges's text in isolation loses much of what makes her specific intervention so pointed and so difficult to dismiss on the Revolution's own stated terms.

Notice how de Gouges's method largely avoids constructing an entirely new philosophical case for women's rights from first principles, relying instead on exposing the gap between the original Declaration's claimed universality and its actual practical exclusion of women, a strategy that argues for consistency rather than for an entirely separate set of new claims.

Pay attention to the pamphlet's dedication to Marie Antoinette, a genuinely risky rhetorical choice given the queen's deep unpopularity, and consider what de Gouges may have hoped to achieve by appealing directly to a woman holding real, if precarious, political power rather than addressing her argument solely to male revolutionary leaders.

Read the postscript's model marriage contract as continuous with, rather than separate from, the document's more explicitly political articles; de Gouges's vision of equality extends deliberately into domestic and economic arrangements, treating legal reform of marriage as no less politically significant than reform of formal citizenship rights.

Read this pamphlet with full knowledge of de Gouges's subsequent execution in view, since her argument that women's full legal accountability, including capital accountability, entails full political rights reads very differently, and considerably more starkly, once a reader knows how directly and how soon this argument would apply to its own author.

## Questions it raises

- Does de Gouges's strategy of exposing the 1789 Declaration's internal inconsistency, rather than constructing an entirely independent argument for women's rights, represent a stronger or a more limited rhetorical approach? What does this method gain, and what might it risk conceding, by working so closely within the original document's own terms?
- How does the scaffold and rostrum argument function once its author was herself executed under exactly the legal system she was describing? Does this outcome vindicate her argument, or does it reveal the argument's tragic limits as actual political persuasion within a revolutionary context that was not, in practice, listening?
- What does de Gouges's provocative dedication to Marie Antoinette suggest about her assessment of where genuine political leverage for women's rights might actually be found during the Revolution, given how limited formal political power available to any woman, queen or commoner, actually was at the time?
- How does the postscript's model marriage contract, extending equality into domestic and economic arrangements, compare to the document's more explicitly political articles? Does treating marriage reform as equally significant to formal citizenship rights strengthen or complicate the pamphlet's overall argument?
- The 1789 Declaration proclaimed universal rights while excluding women in practice without ever explicitly acknowledging that exclusion. What does this gap between stated universal principle and actual exclusionary practice suggest about how revolutionary and founding political documents more generally should be read and evaluated?
- De Gouges's pamphlet remained largely marginal to actual revolutionary policy despite its rhetorical force. What does this limited immediate practical impact, contrasted with the document's later historical and scholarly significance, suggest about the relationship between the strength of a political argument and its actual capacity to produce change in the moment it is made?
