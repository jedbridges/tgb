## Overview

About eighty pages, Martin Luther's appeal to the German princes and knights to reform the church themselves, since Rome will not, published in 1520. Luther addresses the treatise directly to German lay political authority rather than to fellow clergy, a deliberate rhetorical choice that already announces its actual argument, since appealing to lay rulers for church reform presupposes exactly the kind of legitimate lay standing Rome's claimed institutional structure denied.

Luther begins by knocking down what he calls the three walls the papacy hides behind: that clergy outrank laity, that only the pope may interpret scripture, and that only the pope may call a council. Against the first he argues that every baptised Christian is a priest. He then lists grievances, from papal taxation and the traffic in benefices to clerical celibacy and the state of the universities, and proposes concrete measures for each.

## What happens

**Addressing German princes and knights.** Luther opens by addressing the treatise directly to German lay political authority, the princes and knights, rather than to fellow clergy, establishing from the outset his central rhetorical strategy of appealing to legitimate lay standing for church reform.

**Knocking down the first wall.** Luther identifies the first of the papacy's three protective walls, the claim that clergy outrank laity, and argues against it directly that every baptised Christian is a priest, removing any fundamental spiritual distinction between clergy and laity.

**Knocking down the second and third walls.** Luther proceeds to demolish the remaining two walls, the claims that only the pope may interpret scripture and that only the pope may call a reforming council, arguments that depend directly on the foundational claim about universal priesthood already established.

**Papal taxation and the traffic in benefices.** Luther catalogues specific grievances against Rome, beginning with papal taxation draining German wealth and the traffic in benefices, the practice of treating church offices as purchasable commodities rather than genuine spiritual appointments.

**Clerical celibacy and university reform.** Luther extends his catalogue of grievances to clerical celibacy's actual practical consequences and the genuine intellectual poverty of the German universities, proposing concrete specific measures to address each identified problem.

**A programmatic reform agenda.** Taken together, the treatise's movement from foundational theological argument to itemized political complaint with proposed remedies gives it genuine programmatic force, a concrete reform agenda rather than theological protest alone.

## How to read it

Read the treatise's choice of audience, German lay rulers rather than clergy, as itself a significant argumentative move; addressing princes and knights directly for church reform presupposes the legitimate lay authority the rest of the treatise's theological argument then explicitly justifies.

Pay close attention to how the argument that every baptised Christian is a priest functions as the foundation for everything that follows; demolishing this first wall removes the premise underlying the papacy's other two claimed institutional privileges, giving the subsequent argument genuine structural momentum.

Notice how directly the treatise moves from abstract theological principle to concrete, itemized political complaint; the catalogue of specific grievances, taxation, benefices, celibacy, universities, transforms foundational theological argument into an actual practical reform program with proposed remedies.

Consider the treatise's German language, addressed to a broad lay audience rather than Latin-reading theologians, as relevant to its rhetorical strategy; this linguistic choice matches its content, a direct political appeal meant to reach and persuade German princes and knights rather than academic specialists.

Read this treatise alongside Luther's own On the Freedom of a Christian, published the same year, to see how the same underlying theological conviction about universal priesthood, developed here for explicitly political purposes, receives a more purely spiritual treatment in the later, more famous and more widely read companion work.

## Questions it raises

- Does Luther's demolition of the papacy's three protective walls succeed as systematic argument, or does the entire case ultimately rest so heavily on the foundational priesthood-of-all-believers claim that the subsequent two walls fall almost automatically once readers accept this first premise?
- How does addressing German princes and knights directly, rather than clergy or a general audience, shape the treatise's actual argument and rhetorical strategy? Does this choice of audience reveal genuine confidence in lay political legitimacy, or primarily strategic calculation about where effective reforming power actually resided?
- What does the treatise's movement from foundational theological argument to itemized practical grievance suggest about Luther's own understanding of the relationship between theological conviction and concrete political program? Does effective reform require this kind of specific, actionable complaint beyond abstract principle?
- How persuasive are Luther's specific proposed remedies for the grievances he catalogues, papal taxation, the traffic in benefices, clerical celibacy, university reform? Do these concrete proposals hold up as practical political program, or do they remain more aspirational than genuinely implementable given the era's actual political constraints?
- Given that this treatise directly appeals to national political authority against an international religious institution, what does this specific national framing suggest about how the Reformation's early development connected to emerging concepts of German political and cultural identity?
- How does this treatise's confident appeal to lay authority compare to the caution Luther would show five years later in his Friendly Admonition to Peace regarding the Peasants' War? Does the earlier treatise's endorsement of lay political action against Rome sit comfortably alongside his later denial of lay peasants' right to violent political action against their own princes?
