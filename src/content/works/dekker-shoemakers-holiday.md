## Overview

About a hundred pages, a citizen comedy by Thomas Dekker, first performed by the Admiral's Men in 1599, belonging to a distinctive Elizabethan dramatic genre that, unlike the era's more familiar tragedies and histories centered on nobility and royalty, took London's actual working tradesmen and craftsmen as its central subject and its central heroes rather than relegating them to comic subplot serving a noble main story. Dekker draws loosely on Thomas Deloney's earlier prose work about legendary shoemakers for some of his material, but builds around it an original, tightly constructed double plot that interweaves a romantic story involving a gentleman and a citizen's daughter with the more purely comic story of a shoemaker's rise to civic power.

The play's title character in practice, though not formally its protagonist, is Simon Eyre, a boisterous, endlessly quotable master shoemaker whose rise through a lucky cargo deal to become Lord Mayor of London gives the play its most memorable comic energy and its clearest statement of a specifically urban, commercial model of social advancement, success achieved through trade, luck and civic prominence rather than through inherited status, military glory or noble birth.

## What happens

**Lacy's disguise.** The gentleman Rowland Lacy loves Rose, the daughter of a London citizen, but his uncle the Earl, disapproving of the match, arranges for Lacy to be sent to fight in France specifically to separate the couple. Rather than comply, Lacy deserts the army and disguises himself as Hans, a Dutch shoemaker, taking work in the workshop of Simon Eyre specifically to remain near Rose, a disguise plot that lets a nobleman move, temporarily and entirely by his own choice, into the working commercial world the play otherwise treats as its genuine center of interest.

**Simon Eyre's rise.** Eyre, a boisterous and endlessly energetic master craftsman introduced through a torrent of characteristic, half-nonsensical exclamations that quickly became among the most quoted and most performed elements of the play in its own period, secures a lucky, highly profitable cargo deal that transforms his fortunes dramatically, propelling him through a rapid series of civic offices and eventually to the position of Lord Mayor of London, a rise the play presents with genuine warmth and evident comic delight rather than as satire of tradesmen's social ambition.

**Ralph's war and return.** A parallel, more somber plot follows Ralph, one of Eyre's journeymen, who is pressed into military service in France, the same war Lacy has just deserted, and returns home lame from injury after an extended absence to discover that his wife Jane, believing him dead, is on the point of remarrying another man, a plotline that keeps the ordinary human cost of the period's wars and social disruption honestly present alongside the play's more purely comic main action.

**Resolution through community.** Ralph's marital crisis is resolved not through any dramatic action taken by Ralph himself but through the direct intervention of his fellow shoemakers, who recognize Jane and act collectively to prevent the second marriage and reunite her with her actual husband, a resolution that emphasizes communal solidarity among the tradespeople rather than individual heroic action as the mechanism restoring proper order.

**The feast and the pardon.** The play closes with Eyre, now Lord Mayor, hosting an enormous celebratory feast for London's shoemakers, an occasion so significant that the king himself appears, and uses the opportunity to pardon Lacy for his earlier desertion from the army, allowing his marriage to Rose to proceed with royal blessing, a resolution that unites the play's citizen comedy and its noble romantic subplot in a single shared moment of celebration crossing the class lines the play has been navigating throughout.

## How to read it

Read Simon Eyre's characteristic exuberant speech, his catchphrases and boisterous greetings to his workers, as central to the play's comic method and its genuine affection for its tradesmen characters, rather than as mere comic filler surrounding a more serious romantic main plot; Eyre's energy is, in practice, the play's real center of gravity.

Notice how the play structures its double plot so that the noble romantic story, Lacy and Rose, and the citizen comic story, Eyre's rise and Ralph's return, remain genuinely interwoven throughout rather than running as separate, only loosely connected threads, with Lacy's own disguise physically placing him within Eyre's shop and therefore within the citizen world for most of the play.

Pay attention to how Ralph's plotline is resolved, through the collective action of his fellow tradesmen rather than through any dramatic individual heroism on his own part, and consider what this method of resolution suggests about the play's broader values around community, solidarity and mutual obligation among working people.

Consider the play's genuinely warm, largely unironic treatment of tradesmen's social ambition and civic pride, distinguishing it from dramatic traditions that more typically used such ambition as an object of satire or comic mockery; Dekker's affection for his shoemaker characters runs consistently through the play rather than curdling into condescension.

Read the closing feast and royal pardon as a deliberate, symbolically significant crossing of class lines, king, nobleman and tradesmen sharing a single celebratory occasion, and consider what this closing image suggests about how the play imagines the relationship between London's commercial, working world and the formal structures of royal and noble authority above it.

## Questions it raises

- Does the play's genuinely warm treatment of Simon Eyre's tradesman ambition and civic pride represent a meaningfully different attitude toward social advancement than more conventional Elizabethan drama centered on nobility, or does the play ultimately still require royal sanction, the king's appearance and pardon, to fully validate its citizen comedy resolution?
- What does Ralph's plotline, resolved through his fellow workers' collective intervention rather than through his own individual action, suggest about how the play understands agency, solidarity and community among ordinary working people compared to the more individually driven romantic plot involving Lacy and Rose?
- How does Lacy's temporary disguise as a Dutch shoemaker function dramatically? Does his voluntary, reversible entry into the working world validate or subtly undercut the play's more genuine, permanent celebration of tradesmen's actual lives and labor?
- What does the play's closing feast, uniting king, nobleman and tradesmen in shared celebration, suggest about early modern London's own self-understanding of the relationship between commercial and working life and the more formal structures of royal and noble authority?
- Ralph's return from war, lame and nearly having lost his marriage, introduces a genuinely somber note into an otherwise largely comic play. How does this tonal shift affect a reader's or audience's overall experience of the play's resolution, and does the comic ending fully absorb or only partially soften this earlier hardship?
- Citizen comedy as a genre elevated tradesmen to genuine dramatic centrality in a period whose theater more typically centered nobility and royalty. What does the eventual decline of this genre's prominence suggest about broader shifts in what English drama considered worthy of sustained, sympathetic dramatic attention in the centuries that followed?
