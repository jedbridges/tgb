## Overview

About two hundred pages, not a single book but a course reader of English Romantic poems, roughly the generation between the French Revolution and the Reform Act, from around 1789 to 1832. A typical selection includes Blake's Songs of Innocence and of Experience, Wordsworth's Tintern Abbey and the Immortality Ode, Coleridge's Rime of the Ancient Mariner and Kubla Khan.

The reader also typically includes Byron, Shelley's Mont Blanc and Ode to the West Wind, and Keats's odes. Common threads are nature, the imagination, childhood, revolution and the poet's own mind.

## What happens

**Blake's Songs.** A typical selection opens with Blake's Songs of Innocence and of Experience, contrasting states poems tracing childhood's transformation from unguarded openness toward experience's more complicated and compromised knowledge.

**Wordsworth's meditative poems.** The selection includes Wordsworth's Tintern Abbey and the Immortality Ode, meditative autobiographical poems tracing memory, nature and the developing relationship between mature consciousness and childhood perception.

**Coleridge's supernatural poems.** Coleridge's Rime of the Ancient Mariner and Kubla Khan bring supernatural strangeness and visionary intensity into the anthology, contrasting notably with Wordsworth's more grounded meditative approach despite their close collaboration.

**Byron's poetry.** Byron's inclusion extends the anthology beyond the Lake poets' more meditative concerns toward his own distinctive combination of satirical wit, romantic self-dramatization and considerable formal range.

**Shelley's odes.** Shelley's Mont Blanc and Ode to the West Wind bring the movement's second generation into the anthology, extending Romantic concerns with nature and imagination into more explicitly political and philosophical territory.

**Keats's odes.** Keats's odes conclude a typical selection, bringing sensuous intensity and sustained meditation on beauty, mortality and art to complete the anthology's representative range across major Romantic voices.

## How to read it

Read gathering Blake, Wordsworth, Coleridge, Byron, Shelley and Keats within a single course reader as letting students trace shared preoccupations, nature, imagination, childhood, revolution, across genuinely distinct individual voices rather than isolated single-author study.

Pay close attention to how spanning roughly the generation between the French Revolution and the Reform Act situates these poems within a specific political framework, treating Romantic poetry's formal innovations as responses to genuinely revolutionary historical circumstance.

Notice how placing Wordsworth's Tintern Abbey alongside Coleridge's Rime of the Ancient Mariner reveals how differently these close collaborators and friends pursued shared Romantic concerns, supernatural strangeness against meditative autobiography, complicating any single unified definition.

Consider how including Shelley's Mont Blanc and Ode to the West Wind alongside Keats's odes extends the anthology's scope into the movement's second generation, tracing how Romantic concerns evolved and intensified across roughly four decades of development.

Read this anthology's selections alongside Coleridge's own Biographia Literaria to see how his critical and theoretical account of imagination and poetic composition illuminates the actual poems, his own and Wordsworth's, that the anthology gathers for direct comparative study.
