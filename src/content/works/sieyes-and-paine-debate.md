## Overview

About fifteen pages, a brief exchange in the Paris press in July 1791, after Louis XVI's flight to Varennes. Thomas Paine, in Le Républicain and the Moniteur, argued that the king's flight had abolished the monarchy and that France should become a republic. The Abbé Sieyès replied that he preferred monarchy, understood as government by a single responsible executive, to the republic's committee of executives, and that either could be representative.

Paine's rejoinder insisted that hereditary rule of any kind was incompatible with representation.

## What happens

**The flight to Varennes.** Louis XVI's flight to Varennes in June 1791 creates the immediate political crisis occasioning this brief but consequential exchange between two major revolutionary-era political theorists.

**Paine's argument for abolition.** Thomas Paine argues in the Parisian press that the king's flight had already abolished the monarchy, treating this attempted abandonment as itself forfeiting whatever legitimacy the office had previously claimed.

**Paine calls for a republic.** Paine argues that France should become a republic in the monarchy's place, drawing the practical political conclusion from his argument about the king's self-forfeited legitimacy.

**Sieyès's preference for single executive.** The Abbé Sieyès replies that he prefers monarchy, understood specifically as government by a single responsible executive, over a republic's committee of executives, on functional rather than purely ideological grounds.

**Separating representation from executive structure.** Sieyès argues that either monarchy or republic could be representative, separating the question of genuine representation from the specific question of how many executives a government should actually have.

**Paine's rejoinder on heredity.** Paine's rejoinder insists that hereditary rule of any kind remains incompatible with representation, sharpening the debate's central disagreement to heredity itself as the actual disqualifying condition.

## How to read it

Read Paine's argument that Louis XVI's flight to Varennes had already abolished the monarchy as treating a king's own attempted abandonment of his post as forfeiting whatever legitimacy that office originally claimed, political events themselves dissolving authority.

Pay close attention to Sieyès's reply that he preferred monarchy, understood as government by a single responsible executive, to a republic's committee of executives; this complicates any simple opposition between monarchist and republican positions on functional grounds.

Notice how Sieyès's claim that either monarchy or republic could be representative separates the question of representation from the specific question of how many executives a government should have, a genuinely important conceptual distinction.

Consider how Paine's rejoinder insisting hereditary rule of any kind was incompatible with representation draws the debate's sharpest line, treating heredity itself, rather than the number of executives, as representative government's actual disqualifying condition.

Read this brief exchange alongside the Declaration of the Rights of Man to see how the same revolutionary moment's foundational document relates to this more immediate, occasional debate about monarchy's specific legitimacy following the king's attempted flight.
