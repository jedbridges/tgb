## Overview

About one hundred and thirty pages, a Jacobean tragedy written around 1613 to 1614 and published in 1623, based on a true story from sixteenth-century Italy. The widowed Duchess of Malfi secretly marries her steward Antonio against the wishes of her brothers, the Cardinal and the unstable Duke Ferdinand, who set the malcontent Bosola to spy on her.

When the marriage is discovered, Ferdinand torments her with madmen and wax corpses and has Bosola strangle her and her children. Ferdinand goes mad, believing himself a wolf; Bosola turns on the brothers, and the last act kills nearly everyone.

## What happens

**Act One.** At the court of Malfi, Antonio describes the corrupt Cardinal and his brother Ferdinand, who hire Bosola, a former galley slave, to spy on their widowed sister. The brothers warn the Duchess against remarrying; she summons Antonio and, since he cannot woo a duchess, woos him, and they marry in private.

**Act Two.** Bosola tests the Duchess with apricots and discovers she is pregnant. The birth of a son is betrayed to the brothers, and Ferdinand's rage at the news is out of all proportion to a brother's.

**Act Three.** Years later, with three children, the Duchess is confronted by Ferdinand in her bedchamber. She sends Antonio away and pretends to dismiss him for theft, confides in Bosola, who betrays her, and is taken with her children while Antonio escapes to Milan.

**Act Four.** Imprisoned, the Duchess is shown a dead man's hand in the dark and wax figures of her husband and children, and is surrounded by madmen. Bosola, disguised as a tomb-maker and bellman, brings the executioners; she is strangled, then her children and her maid Cariola. Ferdinand, seeing the body, says her eyes dazzle, and turns on Bosola.

**Act Five.** Ferdinand, mad, believes he is a wolf and digs up graves. The Cardinal, having murdered his mistress, plots to kill Bosola. In the dark Bosola kills Antonio by mistake, then the Cardinal; Ferdinand stabs them both and dies; and Antonio's friend brings the surviving son forward as the play ends.

## How to read it

Read the wooing scene as the play's one moment of tenderness, the Duchess proposing because Antonio cannot, and everything that follows as its price.

Pay close attention to the fourth act, the heroine killed before the play is over, and to why Webster gives the fifth to the murderers' collapse rather than to a revenge.

Notice Bosola, the spy who strangles the Duchess and then avenges her, and ask what service to the powerful has done to a man who knows better.

Consider Ferdinand's rage at his sister's body, which the play never explains, and decide how much the tragedy turns on what it will not name.

Read this play alongside Othello to compare two tragedies in which a woman dies for a marriage she chose, and to see what Webster took from Shakespeare's Iago in making Bosola.

## Questions it raises

- Why does the Duchess die in the fourth act, and what does a tragedy mean by outliving its hero?
- What is Ferdinand's rage, and does the play's refusal to name it strengthen the horror or leave a gap at its centre?
- Is Bosola a villain, a victim, or the play's conscience, and can he be all three?
- What does the Duchess's line, I am Duchess of Malfi still, claim, and is it dignity, defiance, or a title clung to when nothing else is left?
- How much of the play's darkness is Jacobean fashion and how much Webster's vision, and does the distinction matter to a reader now?
- What does the surviving son, brought forward at the end, promise, and is the play's close a restoration or an irony?
