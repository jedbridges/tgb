## Overview

About three thousand pages in the complete work, considerably abridged in the standard modern translation this entry follows, a prose history of the first half of the Hundred Years' War, covering roughly 1322 to 1400, composed by Jean Froissart, a poet and chronicler from Hainault in the Low Countries who traveled extensively between the courts of England, France, Scotland and Spain specifically to gather firsthand accounts from the knights, nobles and other participants who had actually fought in the campaigns he narrates. The Chronicles unfold across four books, moving through the war's major battles, sieges and political crises with a level of vivid, closely observed detail historians have long valued even while distrusting Froissart's precision regarding exact dates and the strict sequence of events.

Froissart wrote from within the chivalric value system his subjects themselves largely shared, treating proper knightly conduct, courage in battle, honor in victory and defeat, mercy appropriately granted or withheld, as the natural, largely unquestioned standard against which the war's participants should be judged, giving the Chronicles their distinctive character as a document written from inside aristocratic warrior culture rather than as detached later historical analysis.

## What happens

**Crécy and the shattering of chivalric cavalry.** Among the Chronicles' most celebrated set pieces, Froissart's account of the Battle of Crécy in 1346 describes English longbowmen cutting down repeated charges of mounted French knights, a battle that shattered French confidence in the traditional cavalry charge and marked a genuine turning point in European warfare's actual conduct, even though Froissart himself frames the battle primarily through individual chivalric conduct rather than through any broader analysis of changing military technology and tactics.

**Poitiers and the capture of the French king.** The Chronicles narrate the Battle of Poitiers in 1356 with similarly close attention to individual conduct and chivalric spectacle, an English victory that resulted in the capture of the French king John II, a genuinely consequential political outcome Froissart narrates alongside detailed attention to the specific knights involved and their individual displays of courage and honor.

**The siege of Calais and the burghers.** Froissart's account of the extended siege of Calais and its eventual surrender includes one of the Chronicles' most frequently retold episodes, six leading burghers of the starved city offering their own lives to Edward III in exchange for sparing the remaining population, a gesture of self-sacrifice Froissart presents as among the war's most morally exemplary single episodes, embodying the chivalric virtues his chronicle exists throughout to celebrate.

**The Black Prince's campaigns.** Froissart devotes considerable attention to the military campaigns of Edward, the Black Prince, son of Edward III, narrating his various expeditions and battles with the same close attention to individual chivalric conduct and spectacle that characterizes the chronicle's treatment of warfare throughout.

**The Jacquerie and the Peasants' Revolt.** When popular uprisings force their way into the narrative, the French Jacquerie of 1358 and, considerably later, the English Peasants' Revolt of 1381 led by Wat Tyler, Froissart's own aristocratic perspective becomes most clearly visible: he narrates these commoner uprisings against social superiors with genuine horror and evident incomprehension, treating them as monstrous inversions of proper social order rather than as grievances meriting serious examination on their own terms, a reaction that reveals more clearly than any explicit statement whose interests and whose values the chronicle was actually written to serve.

## How to read it

Read the Chronicles' famous set pieces, Crécy, the burghers of Calais, the Black Prince's campaigns, as deliberately constructed moral and chivalric exemplars rather than as neutral historical reporting; Froissart is consistently teaching his readers what proper knightly conduct looks like through vivid, memorable narrative example.

Approach the chronicle's specific dates and precise sequence of events with appropriate historical caution, since modern historians have long noted Froissart's unreliability on these details, while still recognizing the genuine historical value of his detailed, firsthand-informed picture of how the war's participants dressed, behaved and understood their own conduct.

Pay particularly close attention to Froissart's treatment of the Jacquerie and the Peasants' Revolt as the clearest available window into his own underlying perspective and values; his genuine horror at these uprisings, compared to his celebratory treatment of aristocratic warfare, reveals the chronicle's consistent aristocratic vantage point more directly than any of its more purely narrative sections.

Notice how much of the chronicle's distinctive texture comes from Froissart's own method of composition, extensive travel and direct interview with actual participants, giving the work a genuinely firsthand quality even where its accuracy regarding specific facts remains questionable.

Read the Chronicles alongside other medieval and early modern accounts of chivalric warfare and courtly life, such as Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, to see how directly Froissart's historical narrative shares assumptions and values with contemporary chivalric romance, even while purporting to describe genuine historical events rather than legendary or fictional material.

## Questions it raises

- Does Froissart's consistent framing of warfare through individual chivalric conduct rather than broader strategic or technological analysis limit or actually enhance the Chronicles' value as a historical source? What does this framing capture that a more strategically focused account might miss?
- How should a modern reader weigh the Chronicles' documented unreliability regarding specific dates and precise sequence of events against its genuine value as a detailed, firsthand-informed picture of chivalric culture and warfare during this period?
- What does Froissart's horror and incomprehension at the Jacquerie and the Peasants' Revolt reveal about the limits of his chronicle's perspective? Does this reaction undermine confidence in his broader narrative, or does it simply mark the boundary of whose experience the chronicle was actually designed to represent?
- How does Froissart's method of composition, extensive travel and direct interview with participants across multiple warring courts, shape the chronicle's actual content and perspective compared to a history written from a single national or partisan vantage point?
- What does the burghers of Calais episode's enduring popularity and frequent later retelling suggest about which specific chivalric virtues, self-sacrifice, mercy, honor, later readers and audiences have found most compelling within Froissart's broader chronicle?
- Given that Froissart wrote from within the very value system his subjects held, rather than from detached historical distance, what genuine advantages and what genuine limitations does this insider perspective bring to understanding the actual lived experience of the Hundred Years' War's participants?
