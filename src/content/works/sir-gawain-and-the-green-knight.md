## Overview

About a hundred and twenty pages, an anonymous Middle English romance, composed around 1375 to 1400. At Arthur's New Year feast a huge green knight offers to take a blow from an axe if the striker will accept one in return a year later. Gawain beheads him; the knight picks up his head and rides off.

Gawain sets out the next winter to keep his word, and at a castle on the way is entertained by a lord who proposes to exchange each day's winnings, while the lady of the house tries to seduce him. He accepts a green girdle that is said to protect him and hides it. At the Green Chapel the knight, who is the lord in disguise, nicks his neck for the concealment, and Gawain returns to Camelot ashamed.

## What happens

**The Green Knight's challenge.** At Arthur's New Year feast, a huge green knight offers to take a blow from an axe if the striker will accept one in return a year later, a strange and binding challenge to the assembled court.

**Gawain beheads the knight.** Gawain accepts the challenge and beheads the Green Knight with a single blow, only to watch the knight pick up his own severed head and ride off, very much alive.

**Gawain's journey to keep his word.** Gawain sets out the following winter to keep his word and receive the return blow, honor demanding he fulfill this commitment despite the genuine danger it now clearly represents.

**The castle and the exchange game.** At a castle along the way, Gawain is entertained by a lord who proposes to exchange each day's hunting winnings, establishing a structural parallel to the more intimate exchanges occurring within the castle itself.

**The lady's seduction attempts.** While the lord hunts, his lady repeatedly attempts to seduce Gawain, testing his loyalty and honor within the castle even as the lord's own hunting winnings are exchanged outside it.

**The girdle and the Green Chapel.** Gawain accepts a green girdle said to offer magical protection and conceals it from the exchange; at the Green Chapel, the knight, revealed as the disguised lord, nicks his neck for this concealment, and Gawain returns to Camelot ashamed.

## How to read it

Read Gawain's initial acceptance of the Green Knight's exchange game as staging honor as binding commitment to future vulnerability rather than mere present-tense display of courage, courtly virtue tested across sustained anticipated consequence.

Pay close attention to how the lord's proposed daily exchange of winnings while his lady simultaneously attempts to seduce Gawain creates a genuinely elegant structural parallel, hunting trophies exchanged directly mirroring the more dangerous exchanges within the castle.

Notice how accepting the green girdle and concealing it represents Gawain's single significant moral failure within an otherwise scrupulously honorable performance, self-preservation overriding his given word when danger finally becomes genuinely real.

Consider how the Green Knight's merciful nick rather than fatal blow, followed by revealing himself as the disguised lord testing Gawain throughout, transforms the poem's apparent life-or-death stakes into an elaborate chivalric examination of integrity.

Read this romance alongside Malory's own Le Morte d'Arthur to see how this earlier, more compact and formally intricate poem's treatment of Arthurian chivalry compares to that later, considerably more expansive prose compilation of the broader Arthurian legend.

## Questions it raises

- Does Gawain's concealment of the green girdle represent a genuinely serious moral failure, or does the poem's own resolution, a mere nick rather than death, suggest this lapse was relatively minor compared to the otherwise impossible standard of perfect integrity he maintained?
- How does the structural parallel between the lord's hunting exchanges and his lady's seduction attempts shape your understanding of the testing Gawain undergoes? Does this doubled structure suggest something about how different forms of temptation operate similarly?
- What does the Green Knight's identity as the castle's lord in disguise reveal about the poem's actual purpose in testing Gawain? Does this revelation suggest the entire ordeal was designed specifically to expose this particular weakness, or was the test more open-ended?
- How should a reader weigh Gawain's own severe shame at the poem's conclusion against his broader success in keeping his word and surviving the ordeal? Does this shame reflect appropriate humility, or does the poem ask readers to judge him too harshly?
- Given the poem's careful, symmetrical structure, exchange games, tests of virtue, deferred consequence, what does this formal precision suggest about its relationship to broader medieval literary conventions concerning chivalric romance and moral testing?
- What does this poem's continued critical and popular interest suggest about the durability of its central concerns, honor, temptation and the gap between absolute ideals and actual human fallibility, for readers considerably removed from its original medieval courtly context?
