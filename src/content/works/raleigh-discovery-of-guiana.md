## Overview

About ninety pages, Walter Raleigh's account of his 1595 expedition up the Orinoco in search of El Dorado, the golden city of Manoa, published in 1596 and written to restore his standing with Elizabeth I. He describes the capture of the Spanish governor at Trinidad, the ascent of the river in open boats, and the tribes he met and enlisted as English allies against Spain.

He recounts the wealth he was told of but never saw, and marvels such as men with heads beneath their shoulders. He urges the queen to conquer Guiana before the Spaniards do. His second voyage in 1617 failed and cost him his head.

## What happens

**Capturing the Spanish governor.** Raleigh's expedition begins by capturing the Spanish governor at Trinidad, a military action opening the way for the subsequent ascent into Guiana's interior in pursuit of the legendary golden city.

**Ascending the Orinoco.** Raleigh and his men ascend the Orinoco river in open boats, an arduous journey through unfamiliar and challenging terrain in continued pursuit of El Dorado, the golden city of Manoa.

**Meeting and enlisting tribes.** Raleigh describes the various tribes he encounters along the river, enlisting several as English allies against Spain, positioning England as potential liberator from Spanish colonial oppression rather than simply another rival colonial power.

**Wealth heard of but never seen.** Raleigh candidly admits that the considerable wealth he was told of throughout the expedition he never actually saw himself, an unusually honest note within a genre otherwise notorious for inflated promotional claims.

**Marvels and wonders.** Raleigh includes marvels such as men reported to have heads beneath their shoulders, blending credulous wonder with his more careful geographical and ethnographic observations throughout the account.

**Urging conquest to the queen.** Raleigh concludes by urging Elizabeth I to conquer Guiana before the Spaniards do, framing his entire narrative as explicit political argument for English colonial action in the region.

## How to read it

Read Raleigh's stated purpose, restoring his standing with Elizabeth I, as giving the narrative genuine political urgency beyond simple travel report, an ambitious courtier using publication itself as calculated instrument for personal rehabilitation after falling from royal favor.

Pay close attention to Raleigh's admission that the wealth he was told of he never actually saw; this introduces an unusual note of honesty into a genre otherwise notorious for inflated promotional claims, even as he continues pursuing El Dorado's legendary reputation.

Notice how enlisting various tribes as English allies against Spain reveals imperial strategy operating through diplomatic alliance rather than only direct conquest, positioning England as potential liberator rather than simply another competing colonial power in the region.

Consider how including marvels such as men with heads beneath their shoulders alongside careful geographical observation shows period travel writing's characteristic blend of credulous wonder and genuine empirical reporting, categories not yet clearly separated as they would later become.

Read this account against what came after: Raleigh's second voyage in 1617 failed outright and cost him his life upon return to England, giving this earlier triumphant narrative of ambition and diplomatic promise a considerable retrospective tragic weight its own confident tone could not anticipate.
