## Overview

About a hundred and ten pages, Jacques Maritain's short treatise applying the aesthetics of Aristotle and Aquinas to modern art, published in 1920. Rather than nostalgic backward-looking traditionalism, Maritain applies scholastic categories to contemporary artistic practice with genuine precision, defining art as a virtue of the practical intellect, a habit of making rather than of doing.

The artist's morality as a maker is distinct from his morality as a man. Beauty is what pleases when seen and requires integrity, proportion and clarity. Later chapters treat the rules of art, Christian art, and the purity of the artist's vocation, with examples from Rouault, Cocteau and Satie.

## What happens

**Art as a virtue of the practical intellect.** Maritain opens by defining art as a virtue of the practical intellect, a habit of making rather than of doing, establishing this foundational Aristotelian and Thomist distinction as the framework for everything that follows in the treatise.

**The artist's separate moralities.** Maritain argues that the artist's morality as a maker is genuinely distinct from his morality as a man, refusing both the assumption that great art requires personal virtue and the opposite assumption that artistic greatness excuses personal vice.

**Defining beauty.** Maritain defines beauty as what pleases when seen, requiring integrity, proportion and clarity as its specific constitutive qualities, giving aesthetic judgment rigorous scholastic structure through applicable criteria rather than purely subjective preference.

**The rules of art.** Later chapters treat the rules of art directly, working out the practical implications of Maritain's foundational definitions for how artistic practice should actually proceed within his broader Thomist framework.

**Christian art.** Maritain addresses Christian art specifically, extending his broader aesthetic framework into questions about how religious commitment and artistic practice should properly relate to one another within a specifically Christian understanding of beauty and making.

**The purity of the artist's vocation, with contemporary examples.** The treatise closes by addressing the purity of the artist's vocation, drawing on genuinely contemporary examples from Rouault, Cocteau and Satie rather than purely historical figures, demonstrating scholastic categories' continued relevance to live artistic practice.

## How to read it

Read Maritain's foundational definition, art as a virtue of the practical intellect, as the treatise's genuine organizing framework; this specific Aristotelian and Thomist category, distinguishing making from doing, structures everything that follows about artistic skill's relationship to moral virtue.

Pay close attention to how the distinction between an artist's morality as maker and as man refuses two common but opposite errors; Maritain neither requires personal virtue to explain artistic greatness nor lets artistic greatness excuse personal vice, insisting these remain genuinely separate domains.

Notice how specifically Maritain's definition of beauty, requiring integrity, proportion and clarity, gives aesthetic judgment applicable structure; these three criteria let a reader actually analyze why something pleases when seen rather than simply asserting subjective preference without further explanation.

Consider Maritain's direct reference to contemporary 1920 artists, Rouault, Cocteau, Satie, as significant evidence for his broader claim; demonstrating that centuries-old scholastic categories could illuminate genuinely live artistic practice shows real application rather than merely historical commentary on inherited philosophy.

Set this treatise beside Hegel's Introductory Lectures on Aesthetics: Maritain's fixed Thomist criteria of integrity, proportion and clarity against Hegel's insistence that beauty itself develops historically through successive artistic forms rather than answering to any single stable standard.
