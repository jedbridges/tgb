## Overview

About a hundred and twenty pages, Laurence Sterne's last work, published in 1768 weeks before his death. Parson Yorick, a character from Tristram Shandy, travels through France without ever reaching Italy, recording not sights but encounters, with a monk in Calais, a lady whose hand he holds, a grisette, a caged starling that cannot get out, and a peasant family at supper.

The book is a comedy of feeling that parodies the grand tour and Smollett's ill-tempered travels, and ends mid-sentence in a shared inn bedroom.

## What happens

**Yorick's journey through France.** Parson Yorick, a character carried over from Tristram Shandy, travels through France without ever actually reaching Italy, his intended but ultimately unrealized destination.

**The monk in Calais.** Yorick's first significant encounter involves a monk in Calais, establishing the book's characteristic method of recording personal interaction rather than sightseeing itinerary.

**The lady whose hand he holds.** Yorick encounters a lady whose hand he holds, a moment of cultivated sentiment typical of the book's sustained interest in feeling over conventional travel documentation.

**The grisette and the caged starling.** Yorick's encounters continue with a grisette and a caged starling that cannot get out, each brief episode developing the book's recurring concern with confinement, freedom and genuine feeling.

**The peasant family at supper.** Yorick shares a meal with a peasant family at supper, extending his encounters across social classes and demonstrating sentiment's capacity to connect genuinely different kinds of people.

**The unfinished ending.** The book ends mid-sentence in a shared inn bedroom, Sterne's own death interrupting composition and leaving his final work permanently, poignantly incomplete.

## How to read it

Read Yorick's journey through France without ever actually reaching Italy as inverting travel writing's conventional expectation of comprehensive geographical coverage, prioritizing intensity of individual encounter over any completed itinerary.

Pay close attention to how recording encounters rather than sights stages sentiment and feeling as travel's genuine object, cultivated sensibility replacing landscape and monument as the primary content worth recording for readers.

Notice how the book's explicit parody of the grand tour and Smollett's own ill-tempered travels positions Sterne's approach as deliberate corrective, substituting cultivated feeling for aristocratic cataloguing or irritable complaint.

Consider how ending mid-sentence in a shared inn bedroom, Sterne's own death interrupting composition, gives the book's already fragmentary form an additional layer of genuine biographical poignancy inseparable from the author's actual unfinished life.

Read this book alongside Boswell's own London Journal to compare two very different eighteenth-century approaches to recording personal experience, one a sustained fictional travel narrative built from encounter, the other an unguarded private diary of daily urban life.
