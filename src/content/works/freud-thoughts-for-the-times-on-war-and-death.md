## Overview

About thirty pages, two linked essays Sigmund Freud wrote roughly six months into the First World War in 1915, addressing directly the psychological shock the war's unprecedented violence had delivered to a generation of educated Europeans who had, before the war, held considerable confidence in European civilization's steady, accumulating moral and social progress. Freud wrote the essays as a psychoanalyst responding to genuinely urgent contemporary crisis rather than as purely abstract theoretical work, addressing two distinct but related disruptions the war had produced, a crisis of confidence in civilization's actual moral achievement, and a forced confrontation with mortality that ordinary peacetime psychological life had successfully managed to avoid.

The two essays divide the war's psychological impact into these two connected registers: the first, on the disillusionment the war had produced, addresses the shock of witnessing supposedly civilized nations behave with a violence and disregard for law many observers had believed civilization had left permanently behind; the second, on humanity's actual ordinary attitude toward death, addresses how war's mass, sudden, inescapable deaths had forced back into view a mortality ordinary peacetime psychological life works constantly, if unconsciously, to keep at a comfortable, abstract distance.

## What happens

**The disillusionment of the war.** Freud's first essay directly addresses the psychological shock many educated Europeans experienced witnessing the war's sheer violence and cruelty, a shock Freud argues reflects a mistaken prior assumption rather than any genuine, inexplicable moral regression unique to this particular conflict. Civilization, Freud argues, has never actually eliminated or destroyed the primitive, aggressive impulses humanity inherited from earlier stages of development, but has only ever managed to overlay and restrain them through social convention, law and internalized moral prohibition, restraints that war's extraordinary pressure and its explicit sanctioning of violence against a designated enemy can strip away with genuinely alarming speed and completeness.

**Primitive impulse beneath civilized restraint.** Freud develops this argument by insisting that the capacity for the war's atrocities was never actually absent from supposedly civilized populations but remained fully intact beneath a comparatively thin covering of social restraint, restraint that functions effectively under ordinary peacetime conditions precisely because it is rarely tested against pressures as extreme as organized warfare actually applies.

**Our attitude toward death.** Freud's second essay shifts from the war's violence specifically to its other great psychological disruption, forcing a confrontation with death that ordinary peacetime life had successfully avoided. Freud observes that in ordinary unconscious mental life, every person proceeds essentially convinced of their own immortality, acknowledging death abstractly as an eventual, inevitable fact while genuinely excluding any felt anticipation of it from how they actually plan and experience their own ongoing lives.

**War forcing death into view.** Freud argues that war disrupts this ordinary psychological denial specifically through the sheer scale, suddenness and proximity of the deaths it produces, overwhelming the usual psychological mechanisms that keep personal mortality at a comfortable abstract distance and forcing death's reality back into direct, immediate and inescapable view for populations who had previously managed to hold it at bay.

**Making room for death.** The essay closes with Freud's genuinely striking suggestion that, rather than continuing the exhausting psychological work of denying death's reality, a healthier approach would give death its proper place within both actual reality and ordinary conscious thought, making more deliberate room in daily life for the awareness of mortality the war had now made considerably more difficult to continue suppressing entirely.

## How to read it

Read the first essay's central claim, that civilization only ever overlays rather than eliminates primitive aggressive impulse, as a genuinely bracing psychological diagnosis rather than either simple cynicism about human nature or naive shock at the war's specific violence, and consider how this framework changes how subsequent instances of supposedly civilized violence might be understood.

Notice how directly Freud connects the war's psychological impact to ordinary peacetime psychology rather than treating wartime experience as an entirely separate psychological register; both essays argue that war reveals and disrupts patterns, aggressive impulse restrained by thin civilized convention, death denied through ordinary unconscious immortality conviction, that were already present, if normally hidden, in peacetime psychological life.

Pay close attention to how the second essay's closing suggestion, making more room for death within ordinary life, offers something closer to practical, even therapeutic guidance than the first essay's more purely diagnostic analysis of civilization's fragility, giving the two connected essays a genuinely different closing register despite their shared underlying concern with the war's psychological impact.

Consider the essays' immediate historical context, written only six months into a war whose full scale and duration Freud himself could not yet have fully anticipated, as relevant to understanding both their genuine urgency and the specific historical moment of disillusionment they were directly responding to.

Read these essays alongside Freud's later, more extended treatment of related themes in Civilization and Its Discontents to see how the wartime diagnosis developed here, of civilization as a fragile restraint over persistent primitive impulse, continues and deepens in his subsequent, more systematic theoretical treatment of the relationship between civilization and human aggression.

## Questions it raises

- Does Freud's claim that civilization only ever overlays rather than eliminates primitive aggressive impulse adequately explain the war's specific atrocities, or does this framework risk normalizing extreme violence as simply an inevitable, unsurprising return to an underlying human nature rather than examining the specific historical and political causes of this particular conflict?
- How persuasive is Freud's claim that ordinary peacetime psychological life proceeds as though each person were secretly convinced of their own immortality? Does this account of unconscious death-denial match genuine lived experience, and how might cultural or individual variation complicate this claim as a universal psychological pattern?
- What does Freud's closing suggestion, making more room for death within ordinary life rather than continuing to deny it, actually recommend in practical terms? Does this suggestion offer genuine therapeutic value, or does it remain too abstract to meaningfully change how a reader might actually relate to their own mortality?
- How do these essays' diagnosis of civilization's fragility relate to Freud's own later, more extended treatment of similar themes in Civilization and Its Discontents? Does the wartime essay's more immediate, urgent register add something the later, more systematic treatment lacks, or does the later work simply develop the same insight more fully?
- Given that Freud wrote these essays only six months into a war that would continue for more than three additional years and prove considerably more devastating than anyone could have anticipated at the time of writing, how should a reader weigh the essays' immediate historical context against their broader, more enduring psychological claims?
- What does Freud's psychoanalytic approach to a contemporary political and social crisis, rather than a purely clinical psychological subject, suggest about how he understood psychoanalysis's proper scope and application? Does this kind of direct engagement with contemporary historical crisis strengthen or complicate psychoanalytic theory's claim to scientific, clinically grounded status?
