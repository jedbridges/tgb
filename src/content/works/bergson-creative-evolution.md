## Overview

About four hundred and ten pages, published in 1907, the book that made Henri Bergson, for roughly a decade, one of the most famous and most publicly discussed philosophers in the Western world, his Paris lectures drawing crowds so large that carriages reportedly lined the streets outside the Collège de France and society figures competed for seats, and his eventual 1927 Nobel Prize in Literature was awarded substantially on the strength of this single book's style and reach as much as for any specific philosophical school he founded.

Bergson had already developed the core methodological distinction the book depends on, between intellect and intuition, in his earlier, shorter works, Time and Free Will and Matter and Memory, but Creative Evolution is where he applies that distinction to its largest possible subject: the history of life itself, and the question of what, if anything, actually drives and explains biological evolution. The book arrived at a moment when Darwinian natural selection was widely, though not universally, accepted as the mechanism of evolution, and when Lamarckian theories emphasizing the inheritance of acquired characteristics, and various finalist or vitalist alternatives positing some guiding purpose behind evolutionary change, remained live competing positions in a debate that had not yet settled into anything like the modern evolutionary synthesis.

## The argument

**Intellect and intuition.** Bergson's foundational distinction, carried over from his earlier work and now extended to its widest application, separates two fundamentally different modes of knowing. Intellect, he argues, evolved specifically as a practical tool for manipulating solid bodies in space, for calculating, measuring, and predicting the behavior of discrete, separable objects that can be recombined and rearranged, which is precisely why intellect excels at science, mathematics and technology, disciplines concerned with exactly this kind of solid, spatializable material. But this same practical origin gives intellect a structural blind spot: it can only grasp time itself, and living, changing processes generally, by treating them as though they too were made of discrete, static, spatially arrangeable units, freezing genuine flow and continuous change into a sequence of separate snapshots strung together, which Bergson insists falsifies the actual character of duration and life.

Intuition, by contrast, is the rarer capacity to grasp duration and change from within, sympathetically entering into a process rather than standing outside it and representing it spatially. Bergson does not present intuition as opposed to or replacing intellect, which remains indispensable for practical action and scientific work; rather, he argues that a complete philosophy requires recognizing intellect's proper domain and limits, and turning to intuition specifically for those aspects of reality, above all life and consciousness in their genuine temporal unfolding, that intellect structurally cannot adequately grasp.

**Against mechanism.** Applying this framework to evolution, Bergson first examines and finds inadequate the strictly mechanistic account of evolution associated with orthodox Darwinism as then understood, pure chance variation sorted by natural selection with no further guiding principle. His central objection turns on the problem of convergent evolution: strikingly similar complex organs, most famously the camera-like eye, have evolved independently along entirely separate evolutionary lines, in mollusks and in vertebrates, for instance, lines whose last common ancestor possessed nothing resembling either resulting eye. Bergson argues that pure chance, operating independently along genuinely separate lines, should not be expected to arrive repeatedly at such similar, highly complex solutions, and that this recurring convergence suggests some further explanatory factor beyond blind mechanical chance and selection alone.

**Against finalism.** But Bergson is equally critical of the opposite extreme, finalist or providentialist theories that posit evolution as the gradual unfolding of a predetermined plan or design, with each stage merely realizing a blueprint already fully specified in advance. This account, he argues, is simply mechanism's mirror image, since it too treats the future as already contained, in principle, within the past, merely locating the determining cause in a plan rather than in prior physical conditions; both mechanism and finalism, on Bergson's analysis, deny genuine, unpredictable novelty, the emergence of something that could not, even in principle, have been fully calculated or foreseen from what preceded it.

**The élan vital.** Bergson's own proposed alternative is the élan vital, an original creative impulse or vital thrust that pushes continuously through matter, generating genuinely new forms as it goes, not according to any fixed predetermined plan, but through a real, ongoing, creative struggle with the resistance matter itself offers. Life, on this account, is not executing a blueprint and is not simply the product of accumulated chance; it is closer to an artist genuinely improvising, where each new creative step is real, adds something to the world that did not exist before, and could not have been predicted with certainty in advance, even though, looking backward once it has occurred, it can be made to appear as though it followed necessarily from what came before.

**The branching of life.** Bergson traces this creative impulse as it divides, under the pressure of matter's resistance, into different evolutionary directions, most importantly the parallel but divergent paths of instinct and intelligence. Instinct, most fully developed in insects, particularly social insects like bees and ants, achieves a kind of direct, unreflective, sympathetic knowledge of its object, doing what it does without needing to represent or calculate it intellectually, but at the cost of remaining largely fixed and inflexible. Intelligence, most fully developed in humans, gains flexibility, the capacity to fabricate new tools and solve novel problems, but at the cost of losing instinct's direct, immediate sympathy with its object, understanding things only from outside, through spatial representation and calculation. Neither instinct nor intelligence, on Bergson's account, is simply higher than the other; each is an incomplete, one-sided development of the same underlying vital impulse, and Bergson suggests that intuition itself is best understood as instinct that has become capable of reflecting on itself, a kind of recovered, self-aware sympathy operating alongside, rather than replacing, developed intelligence.

**The whole universe as duration.** In its final chapters, the book extends Bergson's account beyond biological evolution specifically to the universe as a whole, arguing that reality generally, not merely individual consciousness or the history of life, should be understood as a single, continuous, genuinely creative flow, duration on a cosmic scale, rather than as a fixed mechanical system merely unfolding predetermined states through time, a picture Bergson takes classical physics and its underlying spatialized concept of time to have mistakenly assumed.

## How to read it

Read the first chapter carefully; it lays out the core critique of both mechanism and finalism together, using the convergent-eye example, and establishes the basic terms, élan vital, duration, intellect and intuition, that the rest of the book develops and applies.

Read this book after, or at least alongside, Bergson's earlier and shorter works, particularly An Introduction to Metaphysics, which states the intellect-intuition distinction in its clearest, most compact form, since Creative Evolution largely presupposes that distinction rather than re-deriving it from scratch.

Take Bergson's critique of Darwinian mechanism as a serious philosophical challenge from his own historical moment rather than as a verdict on contemporary evolutionary biology, which has developed considerably since 1907, including genetic and molecular mechanisms unavailable to either Bergson or his Darwinian contemporaries, and which addresses convergent evolution through developmental and genetic constraints Bergson had no access to.

Notice how much of the book's continuing influence has been literary and cultural rather than narrowly scientific or philosophical. Writers including Proust and Woolf are frequently discussed in relation to Bergsonian duration, and the book's vivid, almost novelistic prose style, unusual for serious philosophy, was itself part of why it reached such an enormous general readership in its own time.

Read the treatment of instinct and intelligence as branching, incomplete solutions with particular care; it is one of Bergson's more genuinely productive ideas, resisting the simpler assumption that intelligence simply represents a higher evolutionary achievement than instinct, and it anticipates, in its own vocabulary, later biological and philosophical interest in the genuine diversity of cognitive strategies across different evolutionary lines.

## Questions it raises

- Does the convergent evolution of complex organs like the eye actually require something beyond chance and selection to explain, or has subsequent evolutionary biology found adequate mechanistic explanations Bergson could not have anticipated?
- Is the élan vital a genuine explanatory concept, or does it name the very thing needing explanation, creative novelty in evolution, without actually accounting for how or why it occurs?
- Bergson argues intellect structurally cannot grasp genuine duration and change. Is this a real limitation of a specific cognitive faculty, or does it depend on defining intellect narrowly enough to guarantee the conclusion?
- What would it mean, concretely, to know something through intuition rather than intellect, and is Bergson's distinction between the two ultimately a difference in kind or only a difference in degree of attention?
- Does treating instinct and intelligence as equally valid, incomplete branches of one underlying impulse change how we should evaluate claims about animal cognition relative to human cognition?
- Bergson extends duration from individual consciousness to the entire universe. Is this extension warranted by the earlier arguments about life and mind, or does it require an additional, separately justified leap?
