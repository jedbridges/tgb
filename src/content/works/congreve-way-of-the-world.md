## Overview

About a hundred and ten pages, William Congreve's fifth and final play, first performed in March 1700 at Lincoln's Inn Fields, received with notable coolness by its original audience despite what most later critics have judged to be Congreve's finest achievement, a reception gap often attributed to a shift in public taste toward broader, more sentimental comedy and away from the dense, demanding verbal wit that had characterized Restoration comedy at its height. Congreve, apparently discouraged by the play's reception, wrote no further plays for the stage during the remaining nearly three decades of his life, making The Way of the World both the culmination and the effective end of his active theatrical career.

The play belongs to the tradition of Restoration comedy of manners, plays concerned above all with the social performance, verbal wit and romantic and financial maneuvering of a fashionable London elite, and it is generally considered the genre's most fully achieved example, notable for a plot of real intricacy, involving inheritance, forged consent and blackmail, woven together with dialogue so dense with wordplay, double meaning and social maneuvering that careful, attentive reading is required simply to follow what is actually happening dramatically in a given scene.

## What happens

**The central obstacle.** Mirabell loves Millamant and wishes to marry her, but Millamant's aunt, the vain and status-conscious Lady Wishfort, controls half of her niece's fortune and despises Mirabell, having discovered that he had once pretended to court her himself purely as a strategic cover for his actual courtship of Millamant. Marrying without Lady Wishfort's consent would cost the couple half of Millamant's inheritance, making her consent, or some scheme to obtain it, essential to Mirabell's plans.

**Mirabell's scheme.** To secure the marriage on favorable terms, Mirabell devises an elaborate plot: he arranges for his servant Waitwell to disguise himself as a fictional wealthy uncle, Sir Rowland, and court Lady Wishfort directly, with the intention of exposing the fraud at a strategic moment to blackmail her into consenting to Mirabell's marriage to Millamant as the price of avoiding public humiliation.

**The counter-schemes.** This plan is complicated and nearly destroyed by two other characters working against Mirabell's interests. Mrs. Marwood, who is secretly in love with Mirabell herself and resents his devotion to Millamant, discovers and exposes the disguised-uncle scheme to Lady Wishfort before it can be completed. Simultaneously, Fainall, Lady Wishfort's son-in-law and Mrs. Marwood's own lover, attempts a separate scheme to blackmail Lady Wishfort using damaging knowledge of his own wife's past indiscretions, threatening to expose her unless he is given full control over both his wife's and Millamant's fortunes.

**The proviso scene.** Before these plots come to their resolution, Mirabell and Millamant negotiate, in the play's most celebrated single scene, the specific conditions under which each will agree to the marriage, each setting out demands meant to preserve their own independence within the relationship rather than simply surrendering to conventional romantic dissolution. Millamant insists on retaining control over her own correspondence, her social calendar, her choice of company and her freedom from diminishing pet names; Mirabell answers with his own reciprocal conditions, chiefly that she abandon various fashionable but, in his view, ridiculous habits of the period's female social performance.

**Resolution.** Fainall's blackmail scheme ultimately fails because of a deed of trust that Mirabell, in a piece of careful advance planning revealed only at the play's climax, had quietly arranged earlier, transferring effective control of Millamant's fortune to himself before any of the surrounding schemes came to a head, defeating Fainall's leverage entirely. Lady Wishfort, relieved and grateful to be spared public exposure through Fainall's scheme, finally consents to Millamant's marriage to Mirabell on the terms the proviso scene had already established between the two of them directly.

## How to read it

Read slowly and expect to reread individual exchanges; Congreve's dialogue rewards, and to some extent requires, close attention to double meaning, verbal fencing and characters concealing or testing intentions through wit, and missing this density means missing much of what is actually happening dramatically beneath the surface conversation.

Pay particular attention to the proviso scene as the play's genuine emotional and thematic center, distinct from its more conventional plot mechanics of inheritance and blackmail; the scene's treatment of marriage as a negotiated arrangement between two people each retaining specific freedoms, rather than as romantic self-surrender, is unusually clear-eyed for its period and remains the reason the play is still widely read and performed.

Notice how the play distributes wit unevenly among its characters, reserving the sharpest, most controlled verbal facility for Mirabell and Millamant specifically, while other characters, however scheming, display cruder or less successful attempts at the same social performance, a distinction that functions as the play's real measure of characters' worth and self-possession.

Consider the play's title, The Way of the World, as a genuine thematic claim rather than a generic label; Congreve consistently presents his characters' constant maneuvering, financial calculation and social performance as simply how the world actually works among this class, not as a corruption of some purer alternative the play imagines elsewhere.

Read the play alongside other major Restoration comedies of manners to see how its intricate plotting and unusually dense verbal wit represent something close to the genre's outer limit of sophistication, achieved just as public taste was already shifting away from this style toward the more sentimental comedy that would dominate the eighteenth-century stage that followed.

## Questions it raises

- Does the proviso scene's treatment of marriage as a negotiated arrangement between two independent parties represent a genuinely progressive view of marriage for its period, or does it remain fundamentally bound by the same economic and social calculations, fortune, status, reputation, that drive the play's other, more clearly mercenary schemes?
- Why might the play's original 1700 audience have received it coolly despite its evident sophistication? What does this reception gap suggest about the relationship between critical and popular judgment, both in Congreve's time and since?
- Millamant's specific demands in the proviso scene, control over correspondence, social calendar and company, read as unusually assertive for a woman of her period. How should a modern reader weigh this assertiveness against the fact that her ultimate goal remains a conventional marriage?
- How does the play distinguish morally or dramatically between Mirabell's scheming, which the play treats sympathetically, and Fainall's and Mrs. Marwood's scheming, which it treats as villainous, given that all three characters rely on deception and financial calculation to achieve their goals?
- What does Congreve's decision to stop writing for the stage after this play's cool reception suggest about the relationship between an artist's own judgment of their best work and the audience's actual response to it?
- Congreve's dialogue demands unusually close attention to follow. What is gained, and what might be lost for a broader audience, by writing comedy this dependent on verbal precision and sustained attentiveness rather than more immediately accessible physical or situational humor?
