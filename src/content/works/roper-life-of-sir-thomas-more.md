## Overview

About sixty pages, a short memoir of Thomas More by his son-in-law William Roper, who lived in More's household for sixteen years, written around 1556 under Mary I as a source for a fuller biography, published in 1626. Roper records More's rise to Lord Chancellor and his conversations with Henry VIII in the garden at Chelsea.

The memoir continues with More's refusal to swear the oath acknowledging the king's supremacy over the church, his imprisonment in the Tower, his trial on the testimony of Richard Rich, and his execution in 1535. The scenes of More's parting from his daughter Margaret at the Tower wharf were the source for the play A Man for All Seasons.

## What happens

**More's rise to Lord Chancellor.** Roper records More's rise to Lord Chancellor, tracing his subject's considerable political and legal career before the crisis that would eventually consume it entirely.

**Conversations with Henry VIII.** Roper describes More's conversations with Henry VIII in the garden at Chelsea, giving readers direct access to the personal dynamic between king and chancellor before their eventual, fatal rupture.

**Refusing the oath of supremacy.** More refuses to swear the oath acknowledging the king's supremacy over the church, a principled stand of conscience that will determine the entire remaining course of his life and eventual death.

**Imprisonment in the Tower.** Following his refusal, More is imprisoned in the Tower of London, where he remains as the crisis over his conscience and the king's demand continues to develop toward its inevitable conclusion.

**Trial on Richard Rich's testimony.** More is tried on the testimony of Richard Rich, whose account of a private conversation becomes the legal basis for the charges that will lead directly to More's condemnation and execution.

**Parting from Margaret and execution.** Roper preserves the scenes of More's parting from his daughter Margaret at the Tower wharf before his execution in 1535, a moment of considerable emotional weight that would later inspire A Man for All Seasons.

## How to read it

Read Roper's sixteen years living in More's own household as giving this memoir genuine intimate access unavailable to any outside biographer, a son-in-law's direct firsthand testimony about domestic conversation and private conviction rather than reconstruction from public record.

Pay close attention to how More's refusal to swear the oath, held even as it guaranteed his own eventual execution, stages conscience's resistance to political power with unusual documentary specificity, Roper recording actual arguments rather than idealized retrospective summary.

Notice how the scenes of More's parting from Margaret at the Tower wharf, preserved in enough concrete detail to later inspire A Man for All Seasons, demonstrate how personal memoir can generate literary material centuries after its original composition.

Consider that Roper wrote this memoir under Mary I specifically as source material for a fuller biography, occupying a genuinely distinctive position between private family record and deliberate historical documentation shaping how More's story would subsequently be told.
