## Overview

About twenty pages, an essay by W. E. B. Du Bois first published in The Independent magazine in 1910 and substantially revised for inclusion in his 1920 essay collection Darkwater: Voices from Within the Veil, a revision undertaken specifically in the aftermath of the First World War, whose actual devastating course Du Bois treats as direct historical confirmation of the essay's original 1910 argument rather than as anything requiring significant reconsideration. The essay's title deliberately inverts Du Bois's own earlier and more famous concept, developed at length in The Souls of Black Folk, of the veil separating Black and white American experience, turning the same analytical framework, seeing across or through this veil, toward an examination of whiteness itself as a specific historical and political phenomenon rather than leaving whiteness as the unmarked, unexamined norm against which Black experience had typically been analyzed.

Du Bois's central and genuinely striking claim, developed across the essay's relatively brief length, is that personal whiteness, the idea of a unified white racial identity carrying inherent claims to global authority and civilizational superiority, is a distinctly modern invention, coinciding essentially with the rise of European colonial expansion, rather than any ancient, natural or unchanging category of human difference, a denaturalizing historical argument that lets Du Bois reframe contemporary events, above all the recently concluded World War, in genuinely unsettling terms.

## The argument

**Seeing whiteness from behind the veil.** Du Bois opens by establishing his own distinctive analytical vantage point, writing as someone who has observed white American and European society from behind the veil separating Black and white experience closely enough, and for long enough, to describe what whiteness actually is and does with a clarity he argues white observers, embedded within their own unexamined racial identity, generally cannot achieve about themselves.

**Whiteness as modern invention.** Du Bois's central historical claim locates the actual invention of personal whiteness, understood as a unified racial identity carrying inherent claims to global authority and civilizational superiority, within recent history, essentially coinciding with the rise of European colonial expansion, rather than treating it as any ancient or natural category of human difference. This denaturalizing move is essential to the essay's broader argument, since treating whiteness as historically specific and recently constructed, rather than eternal and natural, opens the category to the kind of historical and political critique the rest of the essay pursues.

**The war as imperial quarrel.** Du Bois's most consequential and most historically striking argument reframes the recently concluded First World War, still raw and immediate for his original readers, not as a tragic aberration from European civilization's stated values and achievements but as their direct, logical culmination: a war fought among competing white imperial powers over the right to divide and exploit the wealth, territory and resources of the colonized darker world they had already spent centuries conquering and extracting from, making the war continuous with, rather than a betrayal of, the underlying colonial logic Du Bois argues had organized European power for generations before 1914.

**Civilization against the colonial record.** Du Bois closes by arguing directly that European claims to a superior, uniquely advanced civilization cannot actually survive honest confrontation with the historical record of colonial exploitation, violence and extraction those very civilizational claims had repeatedly been used to justify and excuse, an argument that connects the essay's analysis of whiteness as constructed racial category directly to a broader political indictment of the entire colonial system that category had underwritten.

## How to read it

Read the essay as a direct companion to and inversion of Du Bois's earlier, more extensively developed concept of the veil in The Souls of Black Folk, applying the same basic analytical framework, observation from a position of enforced marginal visibility, toward examining whiteness itself rather than leaving it as an unexamined, implicitly normal background category.

Pay close attention to how the essay's central historical claim, that personal whiteness is a specifically modern invention rather than a natural or ancient category, does real argumentative work throughout the rest of the essay; denaturalizing whiteness is the necessary foundation that makes the subsequent political and historical critique of colonialism and the World War possible.

Notice how directly and how early Du Bois connects domestic American racism to global European colonialism as manifestations of a single underlying system rather than as separate, merely analogous phenomena, an analytical move that anticipated, by decades, later academic and political frameworks explicitly linking racism and empire.

Read the essay's reframing of the First World War with attention to how genuinely unsettling this argument would have felt to contemporary readers still processing the war's immediate, catastrophic aftermath; Du Bois is not merely criticizing European colonialism in the abstract but directly implicating the war's specific, recent devastation as colonial logic's predictable rather than exceptional outcome.

Read this essay alongside Du Bois's broader body of work, particularly The Souls of Black Folk, to trace how his analytical framework developed and extended over roughly a decade, moving from an initial focus on Black American experience specifically toward an increasingly global analysis connecting American racism directly to European imperial power.

## Questions it raises

- Does Du Bois's claim that personal whiteness is a specifically modern invention, rather than an ancient or natural category, hold up as historical argument? What evidence would be needed to support or challenge this denaturalizing historical claim more fully?
- How persuasive is Du Bois's reframing of the First World War as a direct continuation of colonial logic rather than a tragic departure from European civilization's stated values? Does this argument adequately account for the war's specific European causes, or does it risk overextending a colonial framework onto events with more complex immediate origins?
- What does Du Bois's deliberate inversion of his own veil concept, turning analytical attention from Black experience toward whiteness itself, accomplish that a more conventional analysis focused only on Black experience might not achieve?
- How does connecting domestic American racism directly to global European colonialism, as Du Bois does here, change how a reader understands either phenomenon compared to treating them as separate, merely analogous systems of racial hierarchy?
- Given that Du Bois revised this essay after the war's actual devastating course had unfolded, treating that devastation as confirmation rather than complication of his original 1910 argument, how should a reader weigh this kind of retrospective historical vindication in assessing the essay's persuasive force?
- What does the essay's argument that civilizational claims cannot survive honest confrontation with colonial violence suggest about how societies actually process and account for, or fail to account for, historical wrongdoing committed in the name of claimed civilizational superiority?
