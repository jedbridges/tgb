## Overview

About eighty pages, a narrative poem in dactylic hexameter by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, published in 1847 and, for much of the following century, the most popular American poem ever written. In 1755 the British expel the French settlers of Acadia in Nova Scotia, and Evangeline is separated on her wedding day from her betrothed Gabriel, launching a search spanning years and an enormous geographic sweep.

She searches for him across Louisiana, the western prairies and the Ozarks, always just missing him, and finds him at last as an old woman nursing the poor in a Philadelphia almshouse, where he dies in her arms. The forest primeval opening became a schoolroom staple for generations of American readers.

## What happens

**The expulsion of Acadia.** The poem opens with the British expulsion of Acadia's French settlers in Nova Scotia in 1755, a genuine historical atrocity that separates Evangeline from her betrothed Gabriel on the very day of their planned wedding, the founding catastrophe launching the entire subsequent narrative.

**The years-long search begins.** Evangeline begins an extended search for Gabriel across the displaced Acadian diaspora, a search that will eventually span years and cover an enormous geographic range across the developing American landscape.

**Louisiana and the western prairies.** Evangeline's search takes her through Louisiana and across the western prairies, encountering scattered remnants of the displaced Acadian community and repeatedly gathering word that Gabriel has passed through these same places only recently, always just ahead of her.

**The Ozarks and continued near-misses.** The search continues into the Ozarks, extending the pattern of repeated near-miss that structures the poem's central narrative, Evangeline arriving again and again just after Gabriel has already moved on to another location.

**Reunion in the Philadelphia almshouse.** After decades of searching, Evangeline finally finds Gabriel as an old woman herself, now nursing the poor in a Philadelphia almshouse, where she discovers him among the patients she cares for, an aged man near his own death.

**Gabriel's death.** Gabriel dies in Evangeline's arms shortly after their reunion, a quietly devastating conclusion that refuses melodrama in favor of restrained, understated tragedy after such an extended and devoted search across so many years.

## How to read it

Read the poem's repeated pattern of near-miss, Evangeline arriving just after Gabriel has moved on, again and again across years of searching, as its actual central structural and emotional device rather than simple plot delay; this repetition with variation is what gives the poem its distinctive, accumulating emotional weight.

Pay close attention to how Longfellow's choice of dactylic hexameter, a meter borrowed directly from classical epic and rarely attempted successfully in English, gives this American frontier subject matter a deliberately elevated quality connecting a specific historical tragedy to a much older epic tradition.

Notice how restrained the poem's climactic reunion and Gabriel's death actually are; after building years of accumulated longing through the search's repeated pattern, the poem refuses melodramatic release in favor of quiet, understated final tragedy within the humble setting of an almshouse.

Consider the poem's grounding in genuine historical atrocity, the documented British expulsion of Acadia's French population, as relevant to how you read its more purely romantic and narrative elements; this love story unfolds directly from and remains shaped by actual historical violence and displacement.

Read this poem alongside Virgil's Aeneid to compare how two epic poems, separated by roughly two millennia, each use the dactylic hexameter tradition to elevate a story of displacement, searching and loss into something approaching enduring, culturally foundational significance for their respective audiences.
