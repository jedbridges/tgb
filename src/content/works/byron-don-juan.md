## Overview

About six hundred and fifty pages, sixteen complete cantos and a seventeenth left unfinished, a comic epic in ottava rima, the eight-line Italian stanza rhyming abababcc that Byron handles with unmatched virtuosity, allowing him to build toward a lyrical or grand statement across six lines and then deflate it with a comic, often outrageous rhyme in the closing couplet. Byron began writing it in 1818 and continued until his death in 1824, fighting for Greek independence, leaving the poem genuinely unfinished rather than merely unpolished, its narrative trailing off mid-episode with no indication of how, or whether, Byron intended to bring it to a formal close.

The poem takes its hero's name from the legendary seducer of European literature, most famously dramatized in Mozart and Da Ponte's opera, and immediately and systematically inverts the legend: Byron's Juan is not a calculating seducer but a good-natured, passive, generally well-meaning young man to whom things, including seductions, simply happen, repeatedly, across a series of increasingly far-flung and improbable adventures. The poem is as much about its narrator, a worldly, digressive, constantly interrupting voice closely identified with Byron's own public persona, as it is about Juan's episodic adventures, and the narrator's tangents, on poetry, politics, war, his own aging, and his literary rivals, occupy as much of the poem's actual space as the nominal plot.

## What happens

**Spain, and exile.** Juan, raised in Seville by a mother determined to give him an impeccably virtuous education, has an affair at sixteen with an older married woman, Julia, is discovered, and is sent abroad by his mother to remove him from scandal, setting the pattern the rest of the poem will repeat: Juan does not pursue adventure so much as have it happen to him, usually through the agency of a woman more decisive than he is.

**Shipwreck and Haidee.** Juan's ship wrecks, and after a horrifying episode of starvation and, among the surviving crew, cannibalism, narrated with a directness that shocks after the poem's earlier comic register, he washes ashore on a Greek island and is found and nursed back to health by Haidee, the beautiful daughter of a pirate and slave trader who is believed, and believes himself, to be away. Juan and Haidee fall genuinely in love, in an idyll Byron renders with real lyrical tenderness before her father returns, has Juan captured and sold into slavery, and Haidee, discovering what has happened, dies of grief, one of the poem's few moments of sustained, unironic emotional weight.

**The harem, and Russia.** Sold into a Turkish harem, Juan is disguised as a woman to be smuggled in to the Sultana Gulbeyaz, who has purchased him for her own purposes and who, like every woman in the poem so far, takes the active role in pursuing him rather than the reverse. Juan escapes the harem and is swept up in the Russian army's siege of the Turkish fortress of Ismail, a historical episode Byron researched from actual military accounts, and the cantos covering the siege move without warning from the poem's habitual comedy into genuine, unsparing horror, cataloguing the actual atrocity and slaughter of the siege with a directness that briefly abandons irony altogether.

**Catherine the Great, and England.** Juan distinguishes himself in the siege and is sent to Saint Petersburg, where he catches the eye of the Empress Catherine the Great, who takes him as a favorite, and is eventually dispatched from Russia to England on a diplomatic mission, ostensibly to recover his health. The poem's final complete cantos, set in English aristocratic society, shift the satire from Juan's individual adventures to a sustained social portrait: country house visits, a marriage market operating under a veneer of propriety, and the manners and hypocrisies of the English upper class, observed with the same deflating irony the narrator has applied throughout to every other society Juan has passed through.

**The narrator.** Running through every canto, and arguably the poem's real subject, is Byron's narrator, who interrupts Juan's story constantly to address the reader directly, reflect on his own advancing age and declining passions, attack literary rivals by name, most persistently Wordsworth, Southey and Coleridge, the Lake poets whose solemn Romantic seriousness Byron's comic, worldly, deliberately deflating method stands in open opposition to, and range across subjects from the nature of love to the horrors of war to the unreliability of historical memory, all delivered in a conversational, endlessly digressive voice that treats the nominal plot as one thread among many rather than as the poem's actual center of gravity.

## How to read it

Read for the narrator's voice as much as for Juan's adventures; the poem's real pleasure and its real argument live largely in the digressions, and readers expecting a tightly plotted narrative will find the constant interruption frustrating rather than recognizing it as the poem's essential method.

Watch the ottava rima stanza's characteristic movement, building toward a serious or lyrical statement across the first six lines and then undercutting it with a comic, often bathetic rhyme in the closing couplet. This pattern, repeated with endless variation across the whole poem, is the technical engine of Byron's characteristic tone, simultaneously sincere and self-mocking.

Notice how consistently the poem inverts the Don Juan legend. Byron's Juan does not seduce; he is seduced, by Julia, by Haidee, by Gulbeyaz, by Catherine, and tracking this consistent reversal across such different settings and different women is central to understanding what the poem is actually arguing about desire, agency and who really holds power in these encounters.

Read the Ismail cantos as a deliberate tonal rupture, and consider what it means that Byron, having built an elaborate comic method across the earlier cantos, uses that same poem to deliver some of the most directly horrified writing about war atrocity in the period, without ever fully explaining or resolving the tonal shift.

Accept the poem's unfinished state as part of its meaning rather than a defect to be regretted. A poem this committed to digression, to refusing conventional narrative shape and closure, arguably could not have ended in any way that would feel like a proper conclusion, and its actual ending, cut short by Byron's death, is oddly consistent with everything that precedes it.

## Questions it raises

- Does consistently making Juan the seduced rather than the seducer amount to a genuine argument about gender and desire, or is it primarily a comic device Byron returns to for its own sake?
- What is the relationship between the narrator's constant digressions and Juan's episodic adventures? Is the plot merely an excuse for the digressions, or do the two genuinely need each other?
- How does the sudden shift into unsparing horror during the Ismail cantos change how you read the poem's comic register elsewhere? Does the horror retroactively complicate the comedy, or does the comedy make the horror land harder by contrast?
- Byron attacks Wordsworth, Southey and Coleridge by name throughout the poem. Does this personal literary combat strengthen the poem's larger argument about poetic seriousness, or does it read as score-settling that dates the work to its immediate literary moment?
- Is the poem's genuine lack of a planned ending, cut short by Byron's death, consistent with its whole method, or does it leave real questions about where Byron intended the poem, and Juan's fortunes, to go?
- What does the poem's sustained satire of English society in its final cantos suggest about Byron's own relationship to the aristocratic world he had been exiled from by the time he wrote it?
