## Overview

About a hundred and eighty pages, an encyclical letter by Pope John Paul II, published in 1993 and addressed directly to the bishops, treating the foundations of Catholic moral teaching. The encyclical opens by meditating on the rich young man who asks Jesus what good he must do to have eternal life, treating this gospel scene as the actual philosophical starting point for the document's subsequent argument about the relationship between objective moral truth and human freedom.

The encyclical's core second chapter defends the existence of intrinsically evil acts and objective moral truth against three specific contemporary theological positions, fundamental option theory, proportionalism and consequentialism, and argues that freedom itself depends on truth rather than existing prior to or independent of it. The third chapter treats martyrdom, conscience and the Church's own institutional role in moral life.

## What happens

**The rich young man.** The encyclical's first chapter meditates at length on the gospel scene of the rich young man asking Jesus what good he must do to have eternal life, establishing this specific biblical encounter as the philosophical and theological starting point for the document's entire subsequent argument.

**Defending intrinsically evil acts.** The encyclical's core second chapter defends the existence of intrinsically evil acts, actions whose moral character does not depend on surrounding circumstance or consequence, arguing this category is essential to any coherent account of objective moral truth.

**Against fundamental option, proportionalism and consequentialism.** John Paul II directly engages and argues against three specific contemporary theological positions, fundamental option theory, proportionalism and consequentialism, contending that each, in different ways, would ultimately dissolve objective moral truth into circumstance-dependent calculation.

**Freedom depends on truth.** The encyclical's most philosophically ambitious claim holds that freedom depends on truth rather than existing prior to or independent of it, directly inverting a modern liberal assumption that genuine freedom means choosing among options unconstrained by any external moral reality a person does not construct for themselves.

**Martyrdom, conscience and the Church.** The encyclical's third chapter extends its core philosophical argument into practical and institutional territory, treating martyrdom as witness to objective moral truth, examining the proper role and formation of individual conscience, and addressing the Church's own institutional responsibility in articulating and defending moral teaching.

## How to read it

Read the meditation on the rich young man not as devotional preamble separate from the encyclical's philosophical argument but as its actual starting point; the document treats this specific gospel scene as establishing the basic terms, what good must be done, that structure everything that follows.

Pay close attention to how directly the encyclical's second chapter engages fundamental option theory, proportionalism and consequentialism as live, contested positions within contemporary Catholic moral theology rather than treating them as settled matters; this is an active intervention in ongoing theological dispute rather than a summary of uncontested consensus.

Notice how the claim that freedom depends on truth inverts a common modern liberal assumption; where much contemporary moral and political thought treats freedom as prior to and independent of any externally given truth, the encyclical argues the reverse, that genuine freedom requires recognizing and conforming to truths a person does not get to construct.

Consider the encyclical's address specifically to the bishops as significant; this framing positions the document as an exercise of the Church's teaching authority on a foundational question rather than a narrower disciplinary or pastoral matter, a framing that shapes how the document's philosophical argument connects to its institutional and ecclesial claims.

Read this encyclical alongside Aquinas's Summa Theologica to see how directly John Paul II's argument draws on and extends a much longer tradition of Catholic natural law moral theology, situating this modern encyclical within a considerably longer intellectual lineage addressing similar questions about objective moral truth and human action.
