## Overview

About ninety pages, an essay Sigmund Freud published in 1921, applying psychoanalytic theory to a subject he had not previously addressed at length, the psychology of groups, crowds and mass movements, and situated explicitly as a response to and extension of the French social psychologist Gustave Le Bon's already highly influential earlier account of crowd behavior. Le Bon had described the psychological group as a distinctive, altered state in which individual members lose their ordinary critical faculties and personal responsibility, becoming capable, collectively, of acts of both heroism and atrocity that no individual member would likely commit acting alone, but Le Bon's account, Freud argues, described this transformation vividly without ever adequately explaining the actual psychological mechanism that produces it.

Freud's essay sets out to supply exactly this missing mechanism, developing in the process two concepts, identification and the ego ideal, that would prove foundational to his subsequent theoretical development, leading directly into the structural model of ego and superego he would formalize two years later in The Ego and the Id.

## The argument

**Le Bon's group mind.** Freud opens by summarizing and engaging directly with Le Bon's existing account of crowd psychology, the claim that individuals within a psychological group experience a genuine transformation, losing their ordinary individual critical judgment and sense of personal responsibility, becoming more suggestible, more emotionally volatile and more capable of both self-sacrificing heroism and collective cruelty than they would be acting independently.

**The missing mechanism.** Rather than simply accepting Le Bon's vivid description, Freud asks what specific psychological process could actually produce this collective transformation, and identifies the answer as fundamentally libidinal rather than purely social or contagious in nature, a genuine emotional bond of a specific structural kind must be operating to hold group members together and to produce their altered collective behavior.

**Identification and the ego ideal.** Freud's central structural claim explains group cohesion through a specific mechanism: members of a cohesive group, whether as institutionally different as the church and the army, are held together because each individual member has independently placed the same external figure, a leader, Christ, a commanding officer, in the position normally occupied by his own ego ideal, the internal standard against which a person measures and judges himself. Having each substituted the same figure for this internal standard, group members consequently identify with one another as a secondary, derivative effect, rather than through any direct emotional tie between members themselves.

**Hypnosis and love.** Freud extends this same structural analysis to two apparently distinct individual psychological phenomena, hypnosis and romantic love, arguing that both involve a comparable surrender of independent judgment to an idealized external figure who comes to occupy the position normally held by a person's own self-critical faculty, connecting group psychology directly to these more individually focused psychoanalytic concerns and demonstrating the broader explanatory reach of the identification and ego ideal concepts he has developed.

**Panic and the leader's loss.** Freud applies his structural account to explain group panic specifically as a direct consequence of the libidinal mechanism he has identified, arguing that panic characteristically breaks out when a group's leader is lost or when the members' shared substitution of that leader for their individual ego ideal is otherwise disrupted, since it was precisely this shared substitution, rather than any direct bond between members themselves, that had actually been holding the group together throughout.

## How to read it

Read Freud's engagement with Le Bon as establishing the essay's actual explanatory project; Freud is not simply restating an already influential account of crowd behavior but identifying and filling a specific gap in that account, the missing psychological mechanism actually producing the transformation Le Bon had described vividly but left theoretically unexplained.

Pay close attention to how the concepts of identification and the ego ideal function as the essay's genuine theoretical innovation, and notice how directly these concepts anticipate and lead into the superego concept Freud would formalize two years later, making this essay a genuinely important developmental step within his broader theoretical evolution.

Notice how Freud extends his structural analysis of groups to hypnosis and romantic love, connecting what might initially seem like a purely social or political psychological question to more individually focused psychoanalytic concerns, and consider what this extension suggests about how broadly Freud believed the same underlying psychological mechanisms actually operated.

Consider the essay's account of group panic as a direct application and test of its central theoretical claim, since explaining panic specifically through the loss or disruption of the leader-substitution mechanism demonstrates the theory's explanatory reach beyond simply describing group cohesion in its stable, functioning state.

Read this essay alongside Freud's later Ego and the Id to trace directly how the identification and ego ideal concepts developed here evolve into the more fully formalized structural model of ego, id and superego, seeing this essay as a genuine, necessary developmental stage in Freud's broader theoretical trajectory.

## Questions it raises

- Does Freud's explanation of group cohesion through each member's shared substitution of the leader for his own ego ideal adequately account for groups that lack any single clear leader figure, or does this mechanism depend too heavily on a specific kind of hierarchical, leader-centered group structure to generalize more broadly?
- How persuasive is Freud's extension of the group psychology mechanism to hypnosis and romantic love? Does treating these as structurally similar phenomena illuminate something genuine about all three, or does this extension stretch a single explanatory framework further than the actual evidence supports?
- What does Freud's account of panic as resulting from disrupted leader-substitution suggest about political and social movements more broadly? Does this framework offer genuine predictive or explanatory power for understanding how actual historical crowds and mass movements have formed and dissolved?
- How does this essay's development of identification and the ego ideal function as a genuine theoretical bridge toward Freud's later structural model in The Ego and the Id? What specifically changes or develops between this essay's formulation and the later, more formalized superego concept?
- Does Freud's engagement with Le Bon adequately credit or adequately critique Le Bon's own original account of crowd psychology? Is Freud's psychoanalytic mechanism a genuine improvement on Le Bon's more purely descriptive social psychology, or does it simply add speculative theoretical machinery to an already adequate empirical description?
- What are the political and ethical implications of Freud's account of group psychology, particularly his explanation of how individuals can become capable of collective cruelty through leader-identification? Does this framework offer useful insight into historical instances of mass political violence and atrocity, or does it risk explaining away individual moral responsibility within group contexts?
