## Overview

About forty pages, a mock-epic in five cantos by Alexander Pope, published in 1712 and enlarged in 1714, written to reconcile two Catholic families after Lord Petre cut a lock of hair from Arabella Fermor. Belinda wakes, is warned in a dream by the sylph Ariel, dresses at her toilet as a hero arms for battle, and sails up the Thames to Hampton Court.

After coffee and a game of cards, the Baron snips her lock with a pair of scissors. The gnome Umbriel fetches spleen from the underworld, a battle of fans and frowns follows, and the lock rises to heaven as a star. The 1714 version added the machinery of sylphs and gnomes.

## What happens

**Belinda's warning dream.** Belinda wakes after being warned in a dream by the sylph Ariel, an invented supernatural guardian whose caution establishes the poem's mock-epic register from its opening scene.

**The elaborate toilet.** Belinda dresses at her toilet as a hero arms for battle, the poem describing her preparation with the same formal gravity and ceremonial detail epic tradition reserves for genuine warriors before combat.

**The voyage to Hampton Court.** Belinda sails up the Thames to Hampton Court, a social excursion elevated through mock-epic language into something resembling a heroic journey toward eventual confrontation.

**The cutting of the lock.** After coffee and a game of cards, the Baron snips Belinda's lock with a pair of scissors, the poem's central incident, a trivial social offense treated with disproportionate epic seriousness.

**Umbriel's underworld errand.** The gnome Umbriel fetches spleen from the underworld, introducing further invented supernatural machinery that escalates the social offense into something resembling genuine mock-epic conflict among the assembled company.

**The lock's ascension.** Following a battle of fans and frowns, the lock rises to heaven as a star, transforming the petty social humiliation into permanent cosmic commemoration in the poem's memorable closing image.

## How to read it

Read Pope's decision to write a mock-epic celebrating a trivial domestic incident as demonstrating how epic machinery, invocations, supernatural intervention, elaborate battle scenes, could be redeployed toward social reconciliation rather than genuine martial glorification.

Pay close attention to how Belinda dressing at her toilet as a hero arms for battle stages feminine self-presentation's elaborate rituals with the same formal gravity epic tradition reserves for warriors, a satirical elevation that both mocks and takes the stakes seriously.

Notice how Ariel's warning dream and Umbriel's underworld errand introduce invented supernatural machinery specifically calibrated to this miniature social world's scale, parodying epic convention's grandeur while remaining genuinely inventive on its own comic terms.

Consider the lock's final ascension to heaven as a star as transforming petty social humiliation into cosmic commemoration, Pope's closing gesture suggesting trivial human vanity, properly regarded, still contains something genuinely worth celebrating and remembering.

This poem shows Pope's mock-epic gift at its most sustained; set beside his more directly theoretical Essay on Criticism, it demonstrates the very poetic judgment and craft that essay prescribes, put to comic and narrative rather than purely argumentative use.

## Questions it raises

- Does treating a genuinely trivial social incident, cutting a lock of hair, with full epic machinery successfully defuse real family conflict through shared laughter, or does this elevation risk trivializing the actual offense Lord Petre committed against Arabella Fermor?
- How does Belinda's elaborate toilet, staged with epic gravity, function within the poem's broader treatment of femininity and social performance? Does this scene mock feminine vanity, or does it take seriously the genuine social stakes such careful self-presentation carries?
- What does introducing invented supernatural machinery, sylphs and gnomes specifically calibrated to this social world, accomplish that borrowing directly from classical epic tradition could not? Does this original mythology strengthen the poem's satirical purpose?
- How should a reader weigh the poem's stated purpose, reconciling two actual families, against its considerable comic mockery of the very people involved? Does successful satire require this kind of pointed personal risk?
- Given that Pope enlarged the poem in 1714 specifically to add the sylph and gnome machinery, what does this revision suggest about his own developing understanding of what the poem's satirical effect actually required to achieve full mock-epic force?
- What does the lock's final transformation into a star suggest about the poem's ultimate judgment on the entire incident it satirizes? Does this ending represent genuine celebration, gentle mockery, or some more complicated combination of both attitudes toward human vanity?
