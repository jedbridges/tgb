## Overview

About three hundred and fifty pages, the diary James Boswell kept from his arrival in London in November 1762, at age twenty-two, to his departure for Utrecht in August 1763, written with the explicit intention that a friend back in Scotland, John Johnston, would read it, which shapes its candid, performative, almost novelistic self-presentation throughout. The manuscript disappeared after Boswell's death and remained lost for roughly a century and a half, presumed destroyed along with much of his other private papers, until a substantial cache of Boswell's manuscripts was discovered in the 1920s and 1930s at Malahide Castle in Ireland and at Fettercairn House in Scotland, in the possession of his descendants, who had apparently simply never examined or catalogued what they held.

The London Journal was the first major volume published from this recovered archive, appearing in 1950 under the editorship of Frederick Pottle, and its publication was a genuine cultural event: a two-century-old private diary by a minor Scottish literary figure, known chiefly as Samuel Johnson's biographer, became an unexpected commercial bestseller, its frank, self-exposing account of a young man's romantic and sexual misadventures, financial anxieties and social ambition striking readers as remarkably, even shockingly, immediate and modern for an eighteenth-century document.

## What happens

**Arrival and ambition.** Boswell arrives in London in November 1762, a young Scottish gentleman of good family with money in his pocket and an urgent, somewhat unfocused ambition to secure a commission in the Foot Guards, a position that would let him live in the capital rather than return to the legal career his father wanted for him in Edinburgh. The journal opens with real exuberance, Boswell reveling in the freedom and stimulation of London life, theaters, coffeehouses, the company of wits and actors, and pursuing his commission through a series of social connections and influential acquaintances that never, across the whole span of the journal, actually secure him the position.

**Romantic and sexual pursuits.** A substantial portion of the diary records Boswell's romantic entanglements with unusual, sometimes startling frankness for its period. He conducts an extended, genuinely felt if erratically managed courtship of an actress, Louisa, recording both his infatuation and, later, his bitter disappointment and anger when the relationship sours after he contracts gonorrhea, which he attributes to her, in an episode he narrates with a mixture of self-pity and genuine humiliation. Alongside this he records numerous casual encounters with prostitutes picked up on the London streets, described with a directness that later editors initially found necessary to soften or omit in earlier, incomplete published excerpts of Boswell's papers, before the full, unexpurgated Yale edition restored the original text.

**Financial anxiety and social performance.** Running throughout is Boswell's persistent money trouble, living beyond his means, borrowing, and worrying about how to present himself as a gentleman of quality despite chronically inadequate funds, alongside a constant, almost compulsive concern with how he is perceived by others, recording in detail his own performances in company, when he believes he has appeared witty, dignified or impressive, and when he believes he has embarrassed himself.

**Meeting Johnson.** The journal's most historically significant single entry comes on 16 May 1763, when Boswell, present in the back parlour of the bookseller Tom Davies's shop, is introduced to Samuel Johnson, already the most celebrated man of letters in England. The meeting begins awkwardly: warned in advance not to mention his Scottish origins, Boswell blurts out precisely that, and Johnson responds with one of his characteristic sharp put-downs. But the encounter ends warmly, with Johnson reportedly telling Boswell he has taken a liking to him, and the relationship that begins in this brief, initially uncomfortable meeting would develop, over the following two decades, into the friendship and eventual biographical project that produced Boswell's Life of Samuel Johnson, generally regarded as the greatest biography in the English language. Reading the London Journal's account of this first meeting, written in real time with no knowledge of what it would eventually become, gives a genuinely different and more immediate view of the relationship than the retrospective, carefully composed account in the Life itself.

**Self-examination and self-deception.** Throughout the journal, alongside the events themselves, Boswell records extended passages of self-analysis, resolutions to reform his conduct, be more disciplined, more dignified, more serious, followed reliably by accounts of failing to keep those resolutions, and then further rounds of self-reproach and renewed resolution. This pattern, repeated with real psychological consistency across the whole diary, gives the journal much of its distinctive, almost novelistic character: a narrator who is simultaneously the author of his own story and startlingly honest about how poorly that author manages to control the story's events.

## How to read it

Read the journal as a genuine narrative rather than a collection of disconnected daily entries; Boswell's own literary instincts, the same instincts that would later make him a great biographer, are already visible in how he shapes individual episodes, the courtship of Louisa, the meeting with Johnson, into scenes with real dramatic shape rather than flat chronological record.

Read the account of meeting Johnson on 16 May 1763 with full attention, and consider reading it alongside the corresponding, much later account in the Life of Johnson, written with decades of hindsight and literary polish the diary entry does not have. The contrast between the immediate, uncertain first impression and the retrospective, monumentalizing later account is genuinely instructive about how memory and biography reshape a lived moment.

Notice how unusually candid Boswell is about his own failures, vanity and sexual conduct, by the standards of eighteenth-century diary-writing generally, where self-presentation was far more commonly managed and flattering. This candor is precisely what made the journal's eventual publication in 1950 feel so startlingly modern to its first readers, and it remains the diary's most distinctive quality.

Read the journal partly as social history: Boswell's London, its theaters, its taverns, its street life, its casual and commercial sexual economy, is documented here with a specificity and frankness that few surviving eighteenth-century sources match, making the diary valuable evidence for everyday metropolitan life quite apart from its literary and biographical interest.

Read Boswell's Life of Samuel Johnson afterward, or alongside relevant sections, to see the fuller arc of the friendship this journal only begins, and to appreciate how much of Boswell's later biographical genius, the eye for revealing detail, the willingness to record uncomfortable or unflattering moments, is already visible in this earlier, more purely personal record.

## Questions it raises

- Does Boswell's unusual candor about his own failures and vanity make him a more trustworthy narrator of his own life, or does the fact that he was writing for a friend's eyes suggest the candor is itself a kind of performance?
- What does the gap between Boswell's repeated resolutions to reform and his repeated failure to do so suggest about the genre of the self-improvement diary more broadly?
- How does reading the first meeting with Johnson in real time, without knowledge of what the friendship would become, change your understanding of that relationship compared to reading only the retrospective account in the Life?
- Is the journal's frank treatment of Boswell's sexual conduct, including his encounters with prostitutes, primarily valuable as social history, as psychological self-portrait, or as something else entirely?
- What responsibility did earlier editors bear in softening or omitting the diary's franker material before the complete text was finally published, and what does that editorial history suggest about changing standards of what counts as suitable for publication?
- Boswell's journal became an unexpected bestseller in 1950, two centuries after it was written. What does its appeal to mid-twentieth-century readers suggest about what people look for in a diary, and has that appeal changed further in the decades since?
