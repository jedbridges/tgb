## Overview

About thirty pages, the lecture Max Planck delivered in Stockholm in June 1920 on receiving the Nobel Prize for 1918, published in 1922. He describes how the study of black-body radiation led him, after years of work on entropy, to the hypothesis that energy is emitted and absorbed in discrete quanta.

He describes how Einstein, Bohr and others extended the idea to light, specific heats and the structure of the atom. He ends by confessing that the quantum cannot be reconciled with classical physics and that a new theory is needed.

## What happens

**Years of work on entropy.** Planck describes his years of work on entropy, the theoretical foundation from which his eventual breakthrough concerning black-body radiation would emerge after considerable sustained effort.

**The study of black-body radiation.** Planck's sustained study of black-body radiation, the electromagnetic radiation emitted by an idealized perfect absorber, becomes the specific technical problem whose solution would require his revolutionary quantum hypothesis.

**The quantum hypothesis.** Planck arrives at the hypothesis that energy is emitted and absorbed in discrete quanta rather than continuously, a genuinely novel proposal breaking from classical physics' assumption of continuous energy exchange.

**Einstein's extension to light.** Planck describes how Einstein extended the quantum idea to light itself, proposing that light too behaves as discrete quanta in certain circumstances, expanding quantization beyond Planck's original black-body radiation context.

**Bohr's extension to atomic structure.** Planck describes how Bohr further extended quantum ideas to the structure of the atom, applying quantization to explain atomic stability and spectral emission in ways classical physics could not account for.

**Confessing classical physics cannot be reconciled.** Planck ends by confessing that the quantum cannot be reconciled with classical physics and that an entirely new theoretical framework is needed, a genuinely honest admission from the discovery's own originator.

## How to read it

Read Planck's own account of how years of work on entropy led him to the quantum hypothesis as giving readers rare direct access to a major scientific breakthrough's actual intellectual genesis, told by the physicist who lived through the discovery himself.

Pay close attention to how Planck describes Einstein, Bohr and others extending quantization from black-body radiation to light, specific heats and the structure of the atom, tracing quantum theory's rapid expansion across multiple previously separate physical domains.

Notice Planck's closing confession that the quantum cannot be reconciled with classical physics and that a new theory is needed; this displays genuine intellectual honesty from a Nobel laureate admitting his own foundational discovery had opened problems he could not himself fully resolve.

Consider the lecture's combination of retrospective autobiographical narrative with forward-looking acknowledgment of unfinished theoretical work, capturing physics at a moment of genuine, unresolved transition between classical and quantum frameworks that Planck himself helped initiate but could not complete.

Read this lecture alongside Planck's own later Scientific Autobiography to see how this earlier, more technically focused Nobel address relates to his fuller retrospective account of his scientific career and the philosophical questions his discovery eventually raised for him.

- What does Planck's closing confession that quantum theory cannot be reconciled with classical physics suggest about scientific progress's actual character? Does acknowledging unresolved tension represent admirable honesty, or does it undercut confidence in the theory's validity?
