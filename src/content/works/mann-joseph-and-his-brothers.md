## Overview

About fifteen hundred pages across four novels, Thomas Mann's tetralogy retelling the last fourteen chapters of Genesis at enormous length, written between 1933 and 1943 across Germany, Switzerland and California. Mann spent sixteen years composing this expansion of a spare biblical narrative into something approaching full novelistic psychology without betraying the original story's own mythic structure and weight, writing while his own world underwent catastrophic transformation.

The Tales of Jacob recounts Jacob's theft of the blessing, his years with Laban and the rape of Dinah. Young Joseph shows the favourite son's vanity and his brothers' sale of him into slavery. Joseph in Egypt follows him into Potiphar's house and the temptation by Potiphar's wife, whom Mann names Mut-em-enet. Joseph the Provider covers the prison, the dreams of Pharaoh Akhenaten, Joseph's rise and his reunion with his brothers.

## What happens

**The Tales of Jacob.** The tetralogy's first volume recounts Jacob's theft of the blessing intended for his brother Esau, his subsequent years serving Laban to earn his wives, and the rape of Dinah, establishing the family history and accumulated tension that will shape Joseph's own eventual story.

**Young Joseph's vanity and betrayal.** The second volume develops the favorite son's genuine psychological vanity in considerable detail before his brothers, driven by real accumulated resentment, sell him into slavery, rendering this betrayal with the full emotional texture of an actual family's breakdown.

**Joseph in Egypt and Mut-em-enet.** The third volume follows Joseph into Potiphar's household and the temptation by Potiphar's wife, whom Mann names Mut-em-enet, developing her into a complex figure whose desire for Joseph carries genuine psychological weight beyond the biblical source's schematic treatment.

**Prison and Pharaoh's dreams.** Joseph the Provider covers Joseph's time in prison following his false imprisonment, and his subsequent interpretation of the dreams of Pharaoh Akhenaten, the specific skill that will eventually enable his dramatic rise to power within Egypt.

**Joseph's rise to power.** The final volume traces Joseph's rise from prisoner to Egypt's effective provider, managing the kingdom's resources through the famine his interpreted dreams had predicted, establishing the position from which his eventual reunion with his brothers will occur.

**Reunion with the brothers.** The tetralogy culminates in Joseph's reunion with the brothers who betrayed him decades earlier, a reconciliation Mann renders as genuinely difficult achievement rather than automatic resolution, earned through the full psychological weight the preceding volumes have developed.

## How to read it

Read the tetralogy's considerable psychological expansion of Genesis's spare original narrative as genuine literary achievement rather than mere padding; Mann develops full interior lives for figures the biblical source treats with considerably more economy, without betraying the original story's own mythic structure and weight.

Pay close attention to how Young Joseph's development of the favorite son's genuine vanity transforms his brothers' eventual betrayal from simple plot mechanism into the texture of an actual family's accumulated grievance and breakdown, giving the biblical episode real psychological motivation.

Notice how significantly Mann expands Mut-em-enet, Potiphar's wife, beyond her considerably more schematic role in the original biblical account; this development extends the narrative's moral and emotional range while remaining genuinely connected to rather than simply inventing content unconnected to its biblical source.

Consider the tetralogy's culminating reunion between Joseph and his brothers as earning its emotional resolution through the accumulated weight of everything the preceding volumes have developed; forgiveness here becomes genuinely difficult achievement rather than the more automatic resolution the original spare biblical narrative might suggest.

Read this tetralogy alongside the biblical Book of Genesis itself to see directly how thoroughly Mann's considerable psychological and novelistic expansion transforms without abandoning the original narrative's own essential events and structure, adding interior depth while preserving the underlying story's actual shape.
