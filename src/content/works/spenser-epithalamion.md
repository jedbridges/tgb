## Overview

About twenty-five pages, a wedding song Edmund Spenser wrote for his own marriage to Elizabeth Boyle in 1594, published with the Amoretti sonnets in 1595. Its twenty-four stanzas follow the wedding day from the poet waking the bride before dawn, through the procession, the church service and the feast, to nightfall and the wedding bed, ending with a prayer for children.

The structure tracks the hours of the day and the year, and the refrain about the woods echoing changes as night falls.

## What happens

**Waking the bride before dawn.** The poem opens with the poet waking the bride before dawn, beginning the wedding day's careful progression from its earliest hours through to the celebration's eventual conclusion.

**The wedding procession.** The stanzas trace the wedding procession, following the bride's movement toward the church accompanied by the ceremonial trappings appropriate to this significant public occasion.

**The church service.** The poem describes the church service itself, the formal religious ceremony joining the couple according to the sacred rites their marriage requires.

**The feast.** Following the ceremony, the poem turns to the wedding feast, the celebratory gathering marking the couple's marriage before the day's remaining hours bring the celebration toward its close.

**Nightfall and the wedding bed.** As night falls, the poem moves toward the wedding bed itself, the day's public celebration giving way to the couple's private consummation of their new marriage.

**The prayer for children.** The poem concludes with a prayer for children, extending the celebration beyond the wedding day itself into the couple's hoped-for future generations.

## How to read it

Read Spenser's decision to write this poem for his own actual marriage to Elizabeth Boyle as giving the celebrated wedding song genuine biographical stakes, the poet himself the bridegroom whose wedding day the entire elaborate structure commemorates.

Pay close attention to how the structure tracks the hours of the day and the year simultaneously, giving the wedding cosmic significance beyond private celebration, a single couple's marriage mirrored in the sun's progress and the calendar's rhythm.

Notice how the refrain about the woods echoing changes as night falls, tracking the poem's own developing emotional register through subtle variation within fixed repeated structure as the wedding day progresses toward its culmination.

Consider how concluding with a prayer for children extends the poem's celebration beyond the wedding day itself into future generations, marriage's consummation explicitly oriented toward genuine procreative purpose rather than private pleasure alone.

Read this poem alongside Spenser's own Faerie Queene to see how the formal control and mythological resonance he brings to this personal wedding celebration connects to the considerably more expansive allegorical machinery of his major epic romance.
