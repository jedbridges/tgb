## Overview

About a hundred and eighty pages, first published anonymously in 1757 when Edmund Burke was twenty-seven, before his political career began, revised and expanded in a second edition in 1759 with a substantial new introductory essay on taste. It is a work of philosophical aesthetics written by a man with no formal academic training in philosophy, and it became, on the strength of its clarity and its genuinely original central distinction, one of the most influential aesthetic treatises of the eighteenth century, read closely by Kant, who developed his own, more systematically philosophical account of the sublime partly in response to it, and absorbed into the working vocabulary that Romantic poets, painters and travelers would use a generation later to describe mountains, storms, ruins and vast landscapes as sources of the highest kind of aesthetic experience rather than as merely unpleasant or inconvenient terrain.

Burke's central and most consequential move is to separate two feelings that earlier writers on beauty and taste had generally treated as points along a single scale, more or less intense versions of the same basic aesthetic pleasure. Burke argues instead that the beautiful and the sublime are genuinely different in kind, arising from opposite qualities in their objects and producing opposite effects in the observer, one relaxing and social, the other astonishing and self-preservative.

## The argument

**The sublime.** Burke defines the sublime as whatever is fitted, in any way, to excite ideas of pain and danger, terror, or whatever operates in a manner analogous to terror, and argues this is the source of the strongest emotion the mind is capable of feeling, stronger even than any pleasure beauty can produce. Crucially, the sublime requires that the terror be experienced at a safe remove, exciting the idea of danger without actually placing the observer in genuine peril; a storm viewed from safety, a precipice observed rather than fallen from, produces the characteristic delight of the sublime, while genuine, immediate danger produces only ordinary fear or pain, not the elevated aesthetic pleasure Burke is describing.

He catalogues the specific qualities that reliably produce sublime effect: obscurity, since what cannot be clearly seen or fully comprehended is more terrible than what can be measured and understood; vastness, in dimension, height, depth or extent; power, particularly power that could be turned against the observer; darkness; loudness and sudden, intermittent sound; and a general privation, encompassing vacuity, darkness, solitude and silence, each of which Burke argues produces a related species of terror and grandeur.

The peak effect of the sublime, Burke calls astonishment, a state in which the mind is so completely filled and overwhelmed by its object that all its ordinary motions are suspended, accompanied by a degree of horror, leaving no room for any other thought or reasoning to intrude, which is precisely why the sublime, on Burke's account, bypasses rational judgment and works directly and powerfully on something closer to instinct or physiological reflex.

**The beautiful.** Beauty, by contrast, arises from an almost entirely different set of qualities: comparative smallness, smoothness, gradual and imperceptible variation in form, delicacy, and, frequently, softness of color. Where the sublime is associated with self-preservation, the fear response to what threatens us, beauty is, for Burke, fundamentally a social quality, tied to the pleasures of love, affection and the mutual attraction that draws creatures, human and animal alike, into society with one another. He works through examples from the natural world, birds, animals, the human form, arguing consistently that what produces the pleasure of beauty is smallness and delicate gradation rather than the grandeur, power and obscurity that produce sublime terror.

**Grounded in the nerves.** What distinguishes Burke's treatise most sharply from earlier accounts of beauty, which had generally located aesthetic pleasure in proportion, fitness, divine order or some other rational or theological principle, is his insistence on grounding both the sublime and the beautiful in direct physiological effect on the body's nerves and fibers. Sublime objects, on his account, produce a kind of beneficial tension or exercise of the nerves, analogous to the way physical exercise strengthens the body, which is why the controlled terror of the sublime is experienced as pleasurable rather than simply unpleasant. Beautiful objects, by contrast, produce a relaxation of the nerves, consistent with the languor and softness Burke associates with love and social affection. This blunt physiological account was genuinely novel and struck many contemporaries as reductive, explaining aesthetic response through bodily mechanism rather than through reason, divine proportion, or moral sentiment, the more usual eighteenth-century explanations.

**Words and the passions.** The treatise's final section turns to a related but distinct question: why do words, which present no direct sensory image to the mind at all, often move the passions more powerfully than paintings or sculptures, which present a precise, fully realized visual likeness of their subject. Burke's answer is that a clear, complete visual image is, in an important sense, a limited image, fixed and fully comprehended, while words work through habitual, associated feeling built up through a lifetime of use, allowing a description to summon powerful emotion without ever forcing the mind into the fixed clarity a picture requires, which is part of why, on his account, a well-chosen description of a terrible or sublime scene can move a reader more powerfully than an accurate painting of the same scene might.

## How to read it

Read the definitions of the sublime and the beautiful in Parts I and III as the treatise's core, and test Burke's specific catalogue of qualities, obscurity, vastness, power for the sublime, smallness, smoothness, gradual variation for the beautiful, against your own experience of landscapes, buildings or works of art that strike you as sublime or beautiful respectively.

Take the physiological grounding seriously as Burke's genuinely distinctive contribution, rather than skimming past it as an odd period curiosity. His insistence that aesthetic response is rooted in bodily mechanism, nerves relaxing or tensing, rather than in reason or divine order, was a significant departure from earlier aesthetic theory and shaped how later thinkers, including Kant, felt obliged to address the physiological or psychological, as opposed to purely rational, dimension of aesthetic experience.

Read the final section on words and images as an early and still interesting contribution to the comparative theory of the arts, asking what a specific medium, language, painting, sculpture, can and cannot do, and why, rather than assuming all the arts move an audience through essentially the same mechanism.

Notice how directly this treatise anticipates and helped shape Romantic taste for mountains, ruins, storms and vast, obscure landscapes, subjects earlier aesthetic theory, more oriented toward classical proportion and harmony, had generally treated as unpleasant or merely sublime in a looser, less theorized sense. Reading Burke alongside Romantic poetry or landscape painting from a generation later shows a vocabulary being put directly into artistic practice.

Read this treatise alongside Kant's later Critique of Judgment if you want to see the same basic distinction between the beautiful and the sublime developed within a far more systematic and demanding philosophical framework, and consider what Kant's more purely rational, non-physiological account gains and loses compared to Burke's blunter, bodily grounded one.

## Questions it raises

- Does grounding aesthetic response in physiological mechanism, the tensing or relaxing of nerves, actually explain the sublime and the beautiful, or does it merely relocate the question of why certain bodily states feel pleasurable in the first place?
- Is the distinction between the sublime and the beautiful as clean as Burke presents it, two genuinely opposite kinds of pleasure, or do many objects and experiences combine elements of both in ways his framework struggles to accommodate?
- Burke argues the sublime requires safety from actual danger to be pleasurable rather than merely terrifying. Where exactly does the boundary lie between exhilarating aesthetic terror and genuine, unpleasant fear?
- Does Burke's claim that words move the passions more powerfully than visual images hold up against your own experience of literature versus painting, film or photography?
- What does treating beauty as fundamentally a social quality, tied to love and affection, add to or leave out of a fuller account of why we find certain objects, landscapes or forms beautiful?
- How much of Burke's specific catalogue of sublime and beautiful qualities reflects genuine, universal features of human aesthetic response, and how much reflects the particular tastes and assumptions of an educated eighteenth-century English observer?
