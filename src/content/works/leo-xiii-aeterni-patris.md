## Overview

About thirty pages, an encyclical letter issued by Pope Leo XIII in August 1879, only his second year as pope, on the restoration of Christian philosophy. Its actual consequences would extend across the following century in ways few individual papal documents manage, launching the Thomist revival that reshaped Catholic philosophical education from seminaries down through undergraduate curricula that institutions like Thomas Aquinas College still teach today.

The letter argues that faith and reason cannot conflict, surveys the Fathers and the scholastics, and singles out Thomas Aquinas as the master to be followed in Catholic schools and seminaries. Writing at a moment when modern philosophy had grown increasingly estranged from scholastic method, Leo's directive reasserted an entire vision of how philosophy and revealed religion should actually relate.

## What happens

**The compatibility of faith and reason.** Leo opens by establishing the encyclical's central theological premise, that faith and reason cannot genuinely conflict since both ultimately derive from the same divine source, refusing the assumption that rigorous philosophical inquiry and Catholic doctrine exist in permanent tension.

**Surveying the Fathers and scholastics.** Leo surveys the Church Fathers and the broader medieval scholastic tradition, situating this historical review within his larger argument about which approaches to integrating philosophy and theology had actually proven successful across Christian intellectual history.

**Singling out Thomas Aquinas.** From this broader survey, Leo singles out Thomas Aquinas specifically as the master to be followed in Catholic schools and seminaries, treating this particular historical judgment as a considered verdict about Aquinas's systematic achievement rather than arbitrary institutional preference.

**A directive for Catholic education.** The encyclical functions as a direct institutional directive, instructing Catholic schools and seminaries to adopt Aquinas's philosophical method and content as the foundation for training students and future clergy going forward.

**Launching the Thomist revival.** The letter directly launched what became known as the Thomist revival, a sustained movement within Catholic intellectual life recovering and systematically teaching Aquinas's philosophy as the preferred framework for Catholic engagement with philosophical questions.

**Shaping education for a century.** This directive's influence extended across the following century, shaping Catholic educational institutions worldwide and establishing the specific curricular foundation that schools explicitly committed to Thomist education, including Thomas Aquinas College, continue to teach today.

## How to read it

Read the encyclical's foundational claim about faith and reason's compatibility as the premise underlying everything that follows; Leo's subsequent historical survey and specific recommendation of Aquinas only make sense given this prior theological commitment that philosophy and revealed doctrine can genuinely reinforce rather than undermine one another.

Pay close attention to how Leo frames his recommendation of Aquinas as historical judgment rather than simple institutional preference; the encyclical's survey of the Fathers and scholastics positions this choice as the outcome of considered evaluation across Christian intellectual history rather than arbitrary selection.

Notice how directly this encyclical functions as practical institutional directive rather than purely abstract theological argument; Leo explicitly instructs Catholic schools and seminaries to adopt specific curricular content, giving the letter genuine binding consequence beyond mere theoretical recommendation.

Consider the encyclical's considerable subsequent historical influence, launching a revival that shaped Catholic education for an entire following century, as itself significant evidence for how effectively this particular papal directive actually achieved its stated institutional purpose.

Read this encyclical alongside Aquinas's own Summa Theologica to see directly what specific philosophical and theological achievement Leo is recommending; encountering Aquinas's actual systematic work illuminates exactly what qualities Leo identified as making this particular medieval synthesis worth reviving for contemporary Catholic education.
