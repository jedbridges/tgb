## Overview

About a hundred and fifty pages, written by Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi, known in the Latin West as Rhazes, sometime in the late ninth or early tenth century. Al-Razi spent his working life as a practicing physician in Rayy, near modern Tehran, and later in Baghdad, running hospitals, teaching, and writing extensively on medicine, alchemy and philosophy, and he is generally regarded as the greatest clinical physician of the medieval Islamic world, with a reputation for careful, patient-centered observation that distinguished him even among a tradition full of accomplished doctors.

Doubts About Galen, in Arabic al-Shukuk ala Jalinus, addresses a specific problem in the intellectual culture of al-Razi's time. Galen, the second-century Greek physician whose vast body of medical writing had been systematically translated into Arabic in the preceding century, had achieved a level of authority in medicine that al-Razi believed was actively harming the discipline. Physicians treated Galen's conclusions not as a body of work to be tested and refined but as settled truth, and al-Razi, over decades of clinical practice, had accumulated a long list of specific instances where what he observed in actual patients did not match what Galenic theory predicted.

The book is organized in two distinguishable movements: an opening section, usually printed as the Introduction, that mounts a general argument for the legitimacy of disagreeing with a revered founder of a discipline, followed by the body of the work, which moves through specific Galenic texts and specific claims, challenging them one after another on both theoretical and empirical grounds. The version most commonly assigned in programs is the Introduction alone, which is where the treatise's most quoted and most philosophically significant material appears.

## The argument

**The case for criticizing a founder.** Al-Razi opens by confronting directly the objection he expects: who is a later physician to correct Galen, whose authority and achievement in medicine were unmatched? His answer proceeds on several fronts at once.

First, he appeals to Galen's own practice. Galen, in his surviving works, frequently corrects Hippocrates, the earlier Greek physician Galen himself revered as the founder of the discipline, identifying specific errors in Hippocratic doctrine even while praising Hippocrates extravagantly elsewhere. If Galen's reverence for Hippocrates did not stop him from correcting Hippocrates where the evidence required it, al-Razi argues, then reverence for Galen cannot be a reason to refrain from correcting Galen under the same circumstances.

Second, he makes a general argument about how disciplines progress. The arts and sciences, medicine included, advance cumulatively: each generation inherits the observations and conclusions of those before it, and precisely because later practitioners stand on that accumulated foundation, they are positioned to see errors the founders could not have seen, working with less accumulated experience and observation than later physicians eventually acquire. Treating a founder's conclusions as final and beyond revision, on this argument, does not honor the discipline; it arrests its development at whatever point the founder happened to reach, guaranteeing that all subsequent practitioners remain permanently as ignorant as the founder was, without what al-Razi calls even a small addition to genuine knowledge.

Third, and more personally, al-Razi insists that his motive is not the desire to diminish Galen's genuine and considerable achievement but loyalty to what he calls the truth itself, and to the patients whose treatment depends on physicians working from accurate rather than merely traditional accounts of how the body actually functions.

**The specific criticisms.** The body of the treatise, following this opening defense, works systematically through a substantial number of specific Galenic claims, drawn from across Galen's writings on anatomy, physiology and disease, and challenges them one by one. Al-Razi's method combines close textual engagement with Galen's own arguments, pointing out internal inconsistencies or places where Galen's reasoning does not actually establish the conclusion Galen draws from it, with appeals to his own direct clinical observation, describing specific patients and specific cases of fever, of particular symptoms, of particular anatomical questions, where what he had personally observed at the bedside or in dissection did not match Galenic prediction.

Topics covered across the full treatise, beyond what survives or is typically excerpted in the Introduction alone, include disputes over Galen's account of vision and the mechanism of sight, over his theory of fevers and their proper classification and treatment, over specific claims in Galenic anatomy, and over broader questions where Galen's medical writing shaded into philosophical claims about the eternity of the world and the nature of the soul, areas where al-Razi, who held independent and sometimes unorthodox philosophical views of his own, had particular reason to disagree.

Not every one of al-Razi's specific criticisms has held up under later scientific scrutiny; on some points modern physiology has vindicated Galen over al-Razi, and on others it has vindicated al-Razi over Galen, and on still others both were working with concepts later medicine abandoned altogether. The lasting significance of the work lies less in any single verdict on any single anatomical or physiological point than in the method and the stance it models: sustained, respectful, evidence-based disagreement with an authority whose word had effectively become unchallengeable.

## How to read it

Read the Introduction closely and completely; it is the section that survives most fully, that is most commonly assigned, and that contains al-Razi's general argument about intellectual authority, which is more durable and more widely applicable than any of his specific anatomical disagreements.

Do not read the specific medical criticisms expecting a modern medical textbook. Al-Razi and Galen are both working within a broadly humoral framework of medicine, and many of the specific terms in dispute, and much of the underlying physiology both men assume, will look unfamiliar or simply mistaken by contemporary medical standards. Read these sections as a case study in how an early scientific dispute was actually conducted, with what kinds of evidence and what kinds of argument, rather than as a source of medically reliable content.

Notice how carefully al-Razi frames his motive. He is not staging a takedown of Galen's reputation; he explicitly and repeatedly affirms Galen's greatness while insisting that greatness is compatible with, and indeed on al-Razi's account requires, being open to correction. That combination of genuine respect and genuine independence is harder to sustain than either respect alone or dismissal alone, and it is worth noticing exactly how al-Razi manages it.

Think about where else in intellectual history you have seen the same basic move: a later thinker arguing that respecting a founder means testing and, where necessary, correcting the founder's specific claims, rather than treating those claims as permanently settled. The pattern recurs constantly, in science, in religious and legal interpretation, in any tradition organized around founding authorities, and al-Razi's opening argument is one of the clearest early statements of the case for it.

Read this alongside something by Galen himself if you can find an excerpt, even a short one, to get a sense of the authority al-Razi is actually pushing against, and to judge for yourself whether specific criticisms land.

## Questions it raises

- Is al-Razi's argument, that Galen's own willingness to correct Hippocrates licenses correcting Galen, a strong argument, or does it prove too much, licensing endless revision with no stable authority ever surviving?
- What distinguishes legitimate scientific disagreement with a founder from mere iconoclasm for its own sake, and does al-Razi's treatise fall clearly on one side of that line?
- Al-Razi grounds many specific criticisms in his own clinical observation. How should a reader weigh one physician's accumulated bedside experience against an inherited theoretical framework?
- Some of al-Razi's specific criticisms have been vindicated by later medicine and some have not. Does that mixed record support or undermine his general argument about how disciplines should treat their founders?
- If every generation is licensed to correct the last on the grounds of accumulated observation, what protects a discipline from simply discarding genuinely correct earlier insights along with genuine errors?
- What would it mean to apply al-Razi's argument to a tradition you personally hold in high regard? Whose authority would you find hardest to treat the way he treats Galen's?
