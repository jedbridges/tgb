## Overview

About nine hundred pages, Thomas Malory's compilation of the Arthurian legend, finished in prison in 1469 or 1470 and printed by William Caxton in 1485 as twenty-one books. Malory condensed and reconciled centuries of scattered French and English Arthurian material into a single coherent English narrative that would become the actual source most later English-language versions of the legend would draw on directly or indirectly.

It follows Arthur from his birth and the sword in the stone through the founding of the Round Table, the adventures of Lancelot, Gareth and Tristram, the quest of the Holy Grail achieved by Galahad, the adultery of Lancelot and Guinevere, the war it causes, Mordred's treason and the last battle in which Arthur is carried away to Avalon.

## What happens

**Arthur's birth and the sword in the stone.** The narrative opens with Arthur's birth and his eventual proof of rightful kingship by drawing the sword from the stone, establishing his legitimacy before the subsequent founding of the Round Table and its fellowship of knights.

**The founding of the Round Table.** Arthur founds the Round Table, establishing the fellowship of knights whose adventures, alliances and eventual internal betrayals will structure the entire remainder of the narrative across its considerable length.

**The adventures of Lancelot, Gareth and Tristram.** The narrative's central books trace the individual adventures of major knights, Lancelot's exceptional chivalric prowess, Gareth's proving himself despite initial disguise and low apparent status, and Tristram's own extended romantic and chivalric story.

**The quest of the Holy Grail.** Galahad alone achieves the quest of the Holy Grail, his exceptional purity succeeding where Lancelot's otherwise considerable chivalric excellence cannot, a pointed distinction between worldly knightly virtue and the specifically spiritual worthiness the Grail quest actually requires.

**The adultery of Lancelot and Guinevere.** Lancelot and Guinevere's adultery, treated with genuine sympathy rather than simple condemnation, structures the narrative's later tragic turn, an intimate betrayal within the fellowship that will eventually precipitate its complete destruction.

**Mordred's treason and the last battle.** The adultery's exposure precipitates war between Arthur and Lancelot's supporters, Mordred's treason against his own father, and the final catastrophic battle in which Arthur is mortally wounded.

**Arthur's departure to Avalon.** Arthur is carried away to Avalon following the last battle, his actual fate left deliberately unresolved, neither confirmed dead nor confirmed to return, one of English literature's most productive and enduring ambiguities.

## How to read it

Read the narrative's considerable structural achievement, holding together the Round Table's idealistic founding promise with the specific human failures that will eventually destroy it, as its genuine organizing logic rather than simply a loose sequence of individual knightly adventures.

Pay close attention to how sympathetically Malory treats Lancelot and Guinevere's adultery even while never letting readers forget this specific betrayal is what ultimately brings the entire fellowship down; this combination of compassion and consequence gives the tragedy real emotional complexity.

Notice how the Grail quest introduces a genuinely different register from the earlier books' more secular chivalric adventure; spiritual achievement here belongs specifically to Galahad's exceptional purity rather than to Lancelot's otherwise considerable worldly excellence, a pointed judgment worth tracking carefully.

Consider Arthur's final departure to Avalon as deliberately, productively ambiguous rather than a narrative loose end; this refusal to confirm his death or his return gave English literature one of its most enduring and generative uncertainties, still shaping how later writers treat the Arthurian legend centuries later.

Read this work alongside Tennyson's own later poetic treatment of Arthurian material to see how two English writers, separated by roughly four centuries, each approached the same core legendary material with distinctly different emphases and poetic sensibilities.
