## Overview

About sixty pages, an account of Francis Drake's voyage of 1572 and 1573 to the Spanish Main, written around 1593 and published in 1626. It was compiled by the preacher Philip Nichols from the reports of Drake and his companions and revised by Drake himself before his death.

It describes the attack on Nombre de Dios, where Drake was wounded, the alliance with the Cimaroons, escaped African slaves who guided him across the isthmus of Panama, his first sight of the Pacific from a tree, and the ambush of a Spanish mule train carrying silver and gold. It was published by Drake's nephew in 1626.

## What happens

**The attack on Nombre de Dios.** Drake leads an attack on the Spanish colonial town of Nombre de Dios, where he is wounded, an early and costly raid establishing the pattern of daring assault on Spanish American possessions he would pursue throughout the voyage.

**Alliance with the Cimaroons.** Drake allies with the Cimaroons, escaped African slaves living in the isthmus region, who guide him across Panama in a genuine partnership against their shared Spanish colonial enemy.

**Crossing the isthmus.** Guided by the Cimaroons, Drake crosses the isthmus of Panama, an arduous overland journey through territory the escaped slaves knew intimately and the English could not have navigated alone.

**First sight of the Pacific.** From a tree during the crossing, Drake gains his first sight of the Pacific Ocean, a moment of geographical revelation the account records with particular vividness and significance for his later career.

**The ambush of the mule train.** Drake and his allies ambush a Spanish mule train carrying silver and gold across the isthmus, converting months of difficult alliance-building and travel into a successful raid on Spanish colonial wealth.

**Publication by Drake's nephew.** The account, compiled and revised decades earlier, is finally published in 1626 by Drake's own nephew, preserving this formative early voyage for readers long after the events and Drake's own death.

## How to read it

Read Drake's alliance with the Cimaroons as complicating any simple reading of Elizabethan exploration as purely English enterprise; his raid on Spanish colonial wealth depended directly on cooperation with people Spain's own empire had enslaved and who had their own stake in weakening it.

Pay close attention to Drake's first sight of the Pacific from a tree; this specific, almost mythic moment of geographical revelation helped motivate his own later circumnavigation, connecting this earlier, less famous voyage directly to his greater subsequent achievement.

Notice the account's unusual compilation history, gathered by Philip Nichols from participant reports and revised by Drake himself before his death; this gives the text unusual authority as something close to firsthand testimony rather than later secondhand reconstruction.

Consider the account's long gap between composition around 1593 and publication in 1626 by Drake's own nephew; this delayed publication preserved an Elizabethan voyage combining privateering raid with genuine international alliance in one compact narrative.

Read this account alongside Raleigh's Discovery of Guiana to compare two Elizabethan voyage narratives, one centered on alliance and raid against Spanish colonial power, the other on exploration and the search for a legendary golden kingdom in South America.

## Questions it raises

- Does Drake's alliance with the Cimaroons represent genuine mutual partnership against shared Spanish colonial power, or does the account, compiled and revised by English participants, inevitably frame this cooperation primarily in terms of its usefulness to Drake's own raiding purposes?
- How should a reader weigh the account's compilation history, gathered from participant reports and revised by Drake himself, against its claims to accuracy? Does this unusual provenance make the text more reliable than later secondhand accounts, or does it also risk self-serving bias?
- What does Drake's first sight of the Pacific from a tree accomplish narratively beyond marking a geographical milestone? Does this moment function as genuine motivation for his later circumnavigation, or does the account retrospectively inflate its significance?
- How does the ambush of the Spanish mule train complicate any straightforward celebration of Drake as heroic explorer? Does this raid on colonial wealth extraction reveal privateering's essentially predatory character beneath its adventurous narrative framing?
- Given the long gap between the voyage's occurrence, its composition around 1593, and its eventual publication in 1626, what does this delayed publication history suggest about how and why Drake's family chose to preserve and eventually share this particular account?
- What does this account's combination of alliance, raid and geographical discovery suggest about the varied motivations driving Elizabethan overseas ventures? Does treating these elements together complicate any single narrative of purely commercial, purely exploratory, or purely military purpose?
