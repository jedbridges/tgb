## Overview

About one hundred and ten pages, an Icelandic prose saga written around 1250 to 1300, retelling in order the poems of the heroic Edda. It follows the Volsung family from Odin's descendant Volsung through Sigmund, who draws the god's sword from a tree, to his son Sigurd, who kills the dragon Fafnir, wins its cursed gold, wakes the valkyrie Brynhild from her enchanted sleep, and then, drugged into forgetting her, marries Gudrun and wins Brynhild for Gudrun's brother Gunnar.

Brynhild has Sigurd murdered and kills herself; Gudrun's later marriage to Atli ends with the slaughter of her brothers and her revenge on her husband.

## What happens

**The Volsungs.** Odin's descendant Volsung builds a hall around a tree, into which a one-eyed stranger thrusts a sword that only Sigmund can draw. Volsung is killed by his son-in-law Siggeir; Sigmund survives in the forest, fathers Sinfjotli on his own sister Signy, and takes revenge by burning Siggeir's hall, Signy choosing to die in it.

**Sigmund's death and Sigurd's birth.** Sigmund's sword breaks against Odin's spear in his last battle. His widow Hjordis bears Sigurd, who is fostered by the smith Regin and learns from him the story of the otter's ransom, the cursed gold that Regin's brother Fafnir took and became a dragon to guard.

**Fafnir and the gold.** With the reforged sword Gram, Sigurd kills Fafnir from a pit, roasts his heart, tastes the blood and understands the birds, who tell him Regin means to kill him. He kills Regin, takes the gold, and rides to a mountain ringed with fire.

**Brynhild.** He wakes the valkyrie Brynhild, whom Odin had put to sleep, and they swear to marry. At the court of the Gjukungs, Grimhild gives Sigurd a drink of forgetfulness; he marries Gudrun, and rides through the fire in Gunnar's shape to win Brynhild for him.

**The murder.** Quarrelling at the river over whose husband is greater, Gudrun tells Brynhild the truth. Brynhild demands Sigurd's death; Gunnar's brother Guttorm kills him in bed, and Brynhild stabs herself and is burned beside him.

**Gudrun and Atli.** Gudrun is married to Brynhild's brother Atli, who invites her brothers and kills them for the gold, Gunnar dying in the snake pit with a harp. Gudrun kills her sons by Atli, feeds him their hearts, stabs him and burns his hall, and the saga follows her children into the last of the killings.

## How to read it

Read the saga as the form in which the scattered poems of the Edda became one continuous story, and the form through which the legend reached Wagner, Morris and Tolkien.

Pay close attention to the passage of the curse and the sword down five generations, each inheriting a debt it did not contract, and notice that the heroes act in full knowledge of their doom.

Notice Brynhild, given the will to have Sigurd killed and then die beside him, and compare her with the grieving heroines of romance who could not have done either.

Consider the ending, halls burned and brothers slaughtered and no absolution offered, and ask what the northern imagination meant by setting glory and doom so close.

Read this saga alongside the Nibelungenlied to see the same story told in the courtly German manner, with Brynhild diminished, Kriemhild's revenge enlarged and the gods gone.

## Questions it raises

- Is the gold's curse the cause of the saga's catastrophes, or an explanation laid over choices the characters make freely and would make again?
- What does the saga think of Sigurd's forgetting of Brynhild, a drink he did not choose to take: is he innocent, and does innocence matter?
- Why is Brynhild's rage at being deceived greater than Gudrun's grief at being widowed, and what does the saga admire in her?
- What is Odin doing in the story, giving the sword and breaking it, and does the god want the Volsungs to prosper or to fall?
- How should a modern reader take Gudrun's revenge on Atli, killing her own sons: as heroism, madness, or the logic of the feud carried to its end?
- What did Wagner and Tolkien each take from the saga, and what did each have to leave out to make their own stories work?
