## Overview

About thirty pages, Percy Shelley's pastoral elegy of fifty-five Spenserian stanzas, written at Pisa within weeks of Keats's death in Rome in 1821, which Shelley blamed on a hostile review. Adonais, the dead poet, is mourned by Urania and by the personified dreams and desires that were his poems.

A procession of fellow poets mourns him too, including a self-portrait of Shelley. The poem then turns from grief to consolation: Adonais is not dead but has become part of the one Spirit that animates nature, and the poet's own bark is driven out toward him.

## What happens

**Keats's death and Shelley's response.** Shelley writes this elegy at Pisa within weeks of learning of Keats's death in Rome, a death Shelley blamed, inaccurately but with genuine conviction, on a hostile published review of Keats's poetry.

**Urania mourns Adonais.** Urania, the muse figure, mourns Adonais alongside the personified dreams and desires that constituted his poems, gathering an entire imaginative apparatus of loss around the dead poet's absence.

**The procession of fellow poets.** A procession of fellow poets joins the mourning, including an explicit self-portrait of Shelley among them, making the elegy about poets as a class as much as about Keats specifically.

**Grief building through the stanzas.** The poem's fifty-five Spenserian stanzas build an extended, escalating expression of grief before the structural turn that will eventually transform this sustained mourning into consolation.

**The turn to consolation.** The poem pivots from grief toward consolation, declaring that Adonais is not dead but has become part of the one Spirit that animates nature, a specifically Romantic and pantheist answer to mortality.

**The poet's bark driven toward Adonais.** In the poem's closing lines, Shelley's own poetic bark is driven out toward Adonais, an image of imagined merger that would gain tragic resonance after Shelley's own death by drowning less than a year later.

## How to read it

Read Shelley's decision to blame Keats's death on a hostile review, however medically inaccurate, as transforming private grief into public indictment of criticism itself, positioning the elegy as an act of poetic solidarity against a literary establishment he believed had genuinely killed one of its own.

Pay close attention to how populating the poem with a procession of fellow poets mourning Adonais, including Shelley's own self-portrait among them, makes the elegy simultaneously about Keats specifically and about poets as a class facing shared vulnerability.

Notice how the poem's structural turn from grief to consolation offers a specifically Romantic and pantheist answer to mortality, relocating immortality within nature's ongoing processes rather than any conventional Christian afterlife the elegiac tradition might otherwise invoke.

Consider Shelley's own closing image, his bark driven out toward the dead poet, with the retrospective poignancy of knowing his own death by drowning came less than a year later, making the elegy's imagined merger eerily anticipate the author's actual fate.

Read this elegy alongside Shelley's own Defence of Poetry to see how the elevated claims made there for poetry's civilizing power inform the specific grief and consolation this poem offers for a fellow poet's premature death.

## Questions it raises

- Does blaming Keats's death on a hostile review, however factually questionable, strengthen the elegy's emotional force by giving grief a specific target, or does this inaccuracy undermine the poem's credibility as genuine tribute to its subject?
- How does including a self-portrait of Shelley among the procession of mourning poets shape your understanding of the elegy's actual subject? Is this poem primarily about Keats, or does Shelley's own presence make it equally about poetic vulnerability generally?
- What does the poem's pantheist consolation, Adonais absorbed into nature's animating Spirit, accomplish that a more conventional religious consolation could not? Does this Romantic framework offer genuine comfort, or does it substitute abstraction for the specific loss being mourned?
- How should a reader now approach the poem's closing image, Shelley's bark driven toward Adonais, given the knowledge of his own subsequent death by drowning? Does this retrospective knowledge enrich the poem's meaning, or does it impose significance Shelley himself could not have intended?
- Given that this poem was written specifically in response to a perceived injustice against Keats, how does its function as elegy relate to its function as polemic against hostile criticism? Do these two purposes reinforce or complicate each other?
- What does the elegy's use of fifty-five Spenserian stanzas, a demanding and archaic form, suggest about Shelley's understanding of how formal tradition should relate to genuinely personal, contemporary grief for a specific individual's death?
