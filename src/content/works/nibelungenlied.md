## Overview

About four hundred pages, a Middle High German epic composed around 1200, in thirty-nine adventures. The hero Siegfried, killer of a dragon and owner of the Nibelung treasure, comes to Worms to win Kriemhild, sister of the Burgundian king Gunther, and helps Gunther by trickery to win the Icelandic queen Brünhild.

A quarrel between the two queens exposes the deceit, and Gunther's vassal Hagen murders Siegfried and sinks the treasure in the Rhine. Years later Kriemhild marries Etzel, king of the Huns, invites her brothers to his court, and destroys them and herself in a massacre.

## What happens

**Siegfried arrives at Worms.** Siegfried, already famous as killer of a dragon and owner of the Nibelung treasure, arrives at Worms hoping to win Kriemhild, sister of the Burgundian king Gunther, as his wife.

**Winning Brünhild by trickery.** Siegfried helps Gunther win the Icelandic queen Brünhild through deceit, using his own strength and a cloak of invisibility to accomplish feats Gunther could not have managed alone, a deception both men conceal from her.

**The queens' quarrel.** A quarrel between Kriemhild and Brünhild, each claiming precedence over the other, exposes the deceit by which Brünhild was actually won, revealing the concealed trickery to the entire Burgundian court.

**Hagen murders Siegfried.** Gunther's vassal Hagen, acting to protect Brünhild's honor and Burgundian interests, murders Siegfried, converting the exposed deception into fatal violence against the poem's greatest hero.

**The treasure sunk in the Rhine.** Hagen sinks the Nibelung treasure in the Rhine after Siegfried's death, transforming the material wealth into a buried, unresolved grievance that will eventually justify Kriemhild's much later revenge.

**Kriemhild's revenge at Etzel's court.** Years later, Kriemhild marries Etzel, king of the Huns, invites her Burgundian brothers to his court, and destroys them and herself in a massacre completing her decades-long pursuit of revenge for Siegfried's murder.

## How to read it

Read Siegfried's murder by Hagen, engineered through the queens' quarrel exposing a deceit they had no part in creating, as betrayal growing directly from concealed trickery rather than open conflict; the epic's central catastrophe emerges from a lie's discovery, not any battlefield defeat.

Pay close attention to how sinking the treasure in the Rhine transforms material wealth into a buried, unresolved grievance; this act gives the poem's first half a consequence that patiently waits through years of apparent peace before erupting into the final massacre.

Notice how Kriemhild's transformation from wronged widow into an agent of total destruction demonstrates how grief compounded by decades of patient waiting can convert a victim into someone as destructively violent as those who originally wronged her.

Consider how the poem's court culture of hospitality and public honor gets repeatedly weaponized to draw victims into fatal traps, first at Worms and later at Etzel's court, revealing how customs meant to bind noble society together also supply its instruments of destruction.

Read this epic alongside Beowulf and the Song of Roland to compare how three major medieval epics treat heroic death, betrayal and the relationship between personal honor and catastrophic collective violence within their own distinct cultural and narrative traditions.

## Questions it raises

- Does Hagen's murder of Siegfried represent a justified defense of Burgundian and Brünhild's honor against genuine deception, or does the poem ultimately condemn this act as treacherous violence disproportionate to the original trickery it answers?
- How does Kriemhild's decades-long patience before finally taking revenge shape your understanding of her eventual massacre? Does this extended waiting make her actions more sympathetic as sustained grief, or more disturbing as calculated, cold-blooded vengeance?
- What does sinking the Nibelung treasure in the Rhine accomplish narratively beyond removing it from immediate circulation? Does the treasure's burial suggest something about how unresolved grievances persist beneath a surface of apparent peace and reconciliation?
- How should a reader weigh the poem's court culture of hospitality against its repeated use as an instrument of betrayal? Does this tension suggest genuine criticism of courtly custom, or does the poem treat such betrayal as tragic rather than systemic failure?
- Given that Kriemhild destroys her own brothers in the final massacre, how does this specifically familial violence complicate any straightforward reading of her as simply avenging her husband? What does destroying her own family suggest about the scope of what Siegfried's murder cost her?
- What does the epic's continued fascination for later readers and adapters suggest about its treatment of heroism, betrayal and revenge? Do these medieval concerns still illuminate how honor, grievance and violence interact in more contemporary contexts?
