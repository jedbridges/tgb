## Overview

About ten pages, a Sumerian hymn of 153 lines attributed to Enheduanna, daughter of the empire-building king Sargon of Akkad and high priestess of the moon god Nanna at the city of Ur, composed sometime around 2300 BCE, making Enheduanna, on current evidence, the earliest named author in the entire history of world literature, predating by many centuries the various anonymous or later-attributed authors of comparably ancient texts. Enheduanna's appointment as high priestess was itself a deliberate political arrangement, part of Sargon's broader strategy for consolidating religious as well as military and administrative authority over the Sumerian cities his empire had recently conquered, placing his own daughter in direct control of one of Sumerian religion's most significant priestly offices.

The hymn's occasion is a genuine political and personal crisis rather than purely abstract devotional composition: Enheduanna was driven from her temple and stripped of her office by a rebel named Lugal-Ane, and the poem records both her appeal for help and, in its unusual closing lines, her eventual actual restoration to office, giving the hymn a documentary as well as devotional character genuinely unusual for surviving religious poetry from this period.

## What happens

**Praise of Inanna.** The hymn opens with extended, elevated praise of the goddess Inanna, establishing her as mistress of all the divine powers, a goddess whose dominion extends over war and destruction as completely as over any other domain typically associated with divine authority, praise that functions both as conventional religious hymn and as careful preparation for the personal appeal that follows, establishing Inanna's supremacy and her capacity to act before Enheduanna turns to her own specific crisis.

**Expulsion and Nanna's silence.** The poem turns personal as Enheduanna describes being driven from her temple and stripped of her priestly office and regalia by the rebel Lugal-Ane, and records that she called out for help to her own patron god Nanna, the moon god whose priestess she formally was, and received no response, a silence the poem presents as a genuine and troubling failure of the deity most directly obligated to protect and support her.

**Appeal to Inanna.** Having been failed by her own patron god, Enheduanna turns instead to Inanna, offering renewed praise alongside a direct, personal plea for restoration to her rightful office, a rhetorical and theological pivot that implicitly argues for Inanna's superior power and willingness to act relative to Nanna's documented silence in the face of his own priestess's genuine distress.

**Restoration.** The poem records that Inanna heard and answered this appeal where Nanna had not, restoring Enheduanna to her position, a resolution the hymn presents not as vague devotional hope but as an actual, specific outcome the poem itself documents having occurred.

**Recording its own composition.** In the poem's unusual closing lines, Enheduanna records the hymn's own act of composition, describing it as made during the night, and notes her actual return to her office, a self-referential closing that gives the poem a documentary quality genuinely rare among surviving religious compositions from this early period, closing the circle the poem opened with her original expulsion and directly linking the poem's own existence to the real political and personal crisis it addresses.

## How to read it

Read the hymn's opening praise of Inanna as doing genuine rhetorical and theological work rather than functioning as mere conventional preamble; establishing Inanna's supremacy over all divine powers, including war and destruction, prepares directly for the poem's later implicit argument that Inanna, rather than Enheduanna's own silent patron Nanna, is the deity actually capable of and willing to act on her behalf.

Pay close attention to the pointed theological contrast the poem draws between Nanna's silence and Inanna's responsiveness; this contrast is not incidental but central to the hymn's actual argument, implicitly reordering divine authority based on which deity proves capable of decisive action when genuinely appealed to.

Notice how unusual and how significant the poem's closing self-reference actually is, recording its own composition and Enheduanna's actual restoration to office; this documentary quality distinguishes the hymn from more purely devotional or mythological religious poetry, giving it real historical as well as literary and religious significance.

Consider Enheduanna's specific historical position, daughter of an empire-building king placed in a major religious office as a deliberate instrument of imperial consolidation, as relevant context for understanding both the genuine political stakes of Lugal-Ane's rebellion against her and the poem's own function as more than purely private devotional expression.

Read this hymn with appropriate awareness of its considerable historical and cultural distance, composed in Sumerian more than four thousand years ago and surviving through a considerably later scribal tradition of copying and transmission, while still recognizing the poem's genuine, remarkably direct personal voice across this immense temporal gap.

## Questions it raises

- What does the poem's pointed contrast between Nanna's silence and Inanna's responsiveness suggest about how ancient Mesopotamian religious thought understood the relationship between formal religious office, personal appeal and divine obligation to act?
- How should a reader weigh Enheduanna's position as both a genuine religious and literary figure and a deliberate instrument of her father Sargon's imperial political consolidation? Does recognizing this political dimension change how the hymn's devotional content should be read?
- What is the significance of the poem's unusual closing self-reference, recording its own composition and Enheduanna's actual restoration to office? Does this documentary quality change how the hymn should be classified relative to more purely mythological or devotional ancient religious poetry?
- Given Enheduanna's position as the earliest named author currently known in world literature, what does her survival and eventual rediscovery suggest about how historical literary credit and recognition have been preserved, lost and recovered across thousands of years of transmission?
- How does the hymn's genuine, direct personal voice, a specific individual's crisis, appeal and eventual restoration, compare to more anonymous or purely mythological religious literature from comparable periods? What does this personal specificity add to or change about how the poem functions religiously and politically?
- What responsibility does a modern reader have in approaching a text this ancient and this culturally distant, composed in a religious and political context radically different from contemporary experience, while still taking seriously its genuine, specific human voice and the real crisis it records?
