## Overview

About fifteen pages, the first constitution of independent Haiti, issued in 1805 under Jean-Jacques Dessalines, who had defeated Napoleon's invasion force the previous year and been proclaimed emperor of the newly sovereign nation. Where Louverture's 1801 constitution had maintained a careful, strategic ambiguity about Saint-Domingue's formal relationship to France, this 1805 document answers that ambiguity unambiguously, founding an independent state explicitly on the permanent abolition of slavery, making it the earliest modern constitution built on outlawing slavery as a foundational national principle.

The document's most striking provisions go beyond simple abolition, forbidding any white man from owning property within Haitian territory and declaring that all Haitians, regardless of their actual skin color, shall be officially known as black, redefining national citizenship and belonging around a shared history of enslavement and liberation rather than any inherited category of race.

## What happens

**Permanent abolition of slavery.** The constitution's foundational provisions declare slavery abolished forever throughout Haitian territory, formally and permanently ending the institution that colonial Saint-Domingue's entire plantation economy had depended on, a declaration made with none of the studied ambiguity Louverture's earlier 1801 constitution had shown regarding France.

**Redefining Haitian identity as black.** In one of its most striking provisions, the constitution declares that all Haitians, whatever their actual skin color, shall be known officially and collectively as black, a redefinition that uses national identity itself to erase the racial hierarchy colonial slavery had depended on and to unite the new nation's citizens under a shared history of liberation.

**Forbidding white property ownership.** The constitution forbids any white man from owning property within Haiti, a direct constitutional measure intended to prevent the return of the plantation economy and racial hierarchy that slavery had required, closing off the specific legal mechanism, land ownership, through which colonial power had previously operated.

**Establishing freedom of worship.** Alongside its sweeping abolitionist and anti-colonial provisions, the constitution establishes freedom of worship throughout the new nation, extending the document's broader commitment to liberty into the domain of religious practice as well as legal and racial status.

**Concentrating power in Dessalines.** The constitution grants Dessalines, proclaimed emperor of the new nation, sweeping executive authority, including the explicit right to name his own successor, a concentration of personal power its framers considered necessary to defend a fragile, newly independent Black nation surrounded by hostile slaveholding colonial powers.

## How to read it

Read the constitution's declaration that all Haitians shall be known officially as black not as a simple description of the population's actual composition but as a deliberate political redefinition of national identity, one that uses citizenship itself to erase the racial categories colonial slavery had depended on.

Pay close attention to how directly the prohibition on white property ownership functions as an anti-colonial legal mechanism rather than a merely symbolic gesture, closing off the specific economic structure, land held by a colonial planter class, through which the old racial and economic order had operated.

Notice how this constitution's unambiguous declaration of full independence contrasts with Louverture's earlier 1801 constitution's careful strategic ambiguity about Haiti's relationship to France; consider what had changed, militarily and politically, between 1801 and 1805 to make this more explicit declaration possible and necessary.

Consider Dessalines's concentration of sweeping imperial authority, including the right to name his own successor, alongside the constitution's genuinely radical abolitionist and anti-colonial achievements; the document combines both in ways worth weighing together rather than treating as separate, unrelated features.

Read this constitution alongside the Haitian Declaration of Independence, proclaimed the previous year, and Louverture's 1801 constitution, to trace the escalating political settlement of the Haitian Revolution from careful strategic ambiguity through outright declared independence to this fuller constitutional refounding of the state.

## Questions it raises

- Does redefining Haitian national identity so that all citizens are officially known as black represent an effective and defensible response to colonial racial hierarchy, or does this redefinition raise its own questions about how a nation should constitutionally define citizenship and belonging?
- How should a reader weigh the constitution's prohibition on white property ownership against its own subsequent history? Does using constitutional law to bar an entire racial group from property ownership complicate the document's broader claims to founding a just society, even given the specific colonial context that provoked it?
- What does the shift from Louverture's careful ambiguity in 1801 to Dessalines's unambiguous declared independence and sweeping imperial authority in 1805 suggest about the different political calculations facing Haitian leadership at these two distinct moments in the revolution?
- Given that this constitution is the earliest modern document founding a state explicitly on the permanent abolition of slavery, why do you think it receives comparatively little attention in standard accounts of modern constitutional history compared to documents like the American or French constitutions?
- How does Dessalines's concentration of imperial power, including the right to name his own successor, compare to Louverture's similar concentration of authority in the 1801 constitution? Does this continuity across both documents suggest something necessary about founding and defending a newly independent state in this specific historical situation?
- What does this constitution's combination of genuinely radical emancipatory achievement with authoritarian centralization suggest more broadly about the political forms available to a revolutionary movement attempting to secure lasting freedom against continued external threat from hostile, more powerful slaveholding nations?
