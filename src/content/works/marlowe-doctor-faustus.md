## Overview

About a hundred pages, Christopher Marlowe's tragedy, composed around 1592. A scholar at Wittenberg who has exhausted logic, medicine, law and divinity turns to magic, conjures the devil Mephistopheles and signs away his soul in his own blood for twenty-four years of knowledge and power. What he does with them is mostly trivial: he plays tricks on the Pope, fetches grapes for a duchess, is shown a pageant of the seven deadly sins, and calls up the shade of Helen of Troy.

Throughout, a good and an evil angel argue over him and he is repeatedly told that he could still repent. In the last scene the hour strikes and the devils come for him. The play survives in two quite different texts.

## What happens

**Faustus's exhausted learning.** The play opens with Faustus surveying and rejecting conventional academic disciplines, logic, medicine, law and divinity, each found inadequate to satisfy his genuinely unlimited ambition for knowledge and power beyond what ordinary scholarship could provide.

**The bargain with Mephistopheles.** Faustus turns to magic, conjures the devil Mephistopheles, and signs away his soul in his own blood for twenty-four years of unlimited knowledge and power, establishing the play's founding bargain and the mismatch between his ambition and what will follow.

**Trivial uses of unlimited power.** Despite his purchased unlimited power, Faustus spends his twenty-four years on largely trivial pursuits, playing tricks on the Pope, fetching grapes for a duchess, exposing a genuine gap between his world-transforming original ambition and his actual petty accomplishments.

**The pageant of the seven deadly sins.** Faustus is shown a pageant of the seven deadly sins, a spectacle offered as entertainment that ironically displays exactly the moral corruption his own bargain has already committed him to more fundamentally.

**Summoning Helen of Troy.** Faustus calls up the shade of Helen of Troy, using his magical power for purely private aesthetic pleasure rather than any more substantial or world-transforming purpose, extending the pattern of trivial application his considerable power has consistently followed.

**The angels and repeated opportunities to repent.** Throughout the play, a good angel and an evil angel argue over Faustus, and he is repeatedly told he could still repent and be saved, genuine opportunities he consistently fails to take across the entire narrative.

**The final scene.** In the last scene, the hour strikes and the devils come for Faustus, a climax the entire preceding play has been building toward through his repeatedly squandered opportunities for repentance rather than arriving as sudden, unprepared catastrophe.

## How to read it

Read the gap between Faustus's infinite original ambition and his actual trivial accomplishments as the play's genuine central subject; this mismatch suggests that even limitless power, once actually possessed, cannot escape a fundamentally limited human imagination for how to use it meaningfully.

Pay close attention to how the good and evil angels' sustained argument, and Faustus's repeated genuine opportunities to repent, refuse to let his eventual damnation read as simple predetermined fate; the play insists throughout on his ongoing freedom to choose otherwise rather than treating his destruction as unavoidable from the outset.

Notice how specifically trivial Faustus's actual magical accomplishments prove to be, practical jokes and private aesthetic pleasures rather than any genuinely world-transforming achievement; this pettiness is worth weighing directly against the vast ambition his original bargain expressed.

Consider the play's survival in two considerably different texts, the shorter A-text and the more elaborate B-text, as itself significant; choosing which version to read shapes your actual encounter with the play in ways worth being explicitly aware of rather than treating either text as simply the authoritative version.

Read this play alongside Goethe's own later Faust to compare how two major writers, separated by roughly two centuries, treated the same core legend of a scholar bargaining with the devil, Marlowe's more compressed Renaissance tragedy against Goethe's considerably more expansive philosophical treatment.

- How does the sustained presence of the good and evil angels, along with Faustus's repeated opportunities to repent, shape your understanding of his ultimate damnation? Does this structure successfully establish genuine ongoing freedom, or does the play's eventual outcome feel predetermined despite this apparent structural openness?
- What does Faustus's specific choice to summon Helen of Troy for private aesthetic pleasure, rather than pursuing any more substantial goal, suggest about the play's broader treatment of beauty and desire? Does this scene complicate or simply extend the pattern of trivial magical application established earlier?
- How significant is the choice between the A-text and B-text for actually understanding this play? Does this textual instability meaningfully affect interpretation, or do the two versions ultimately convey substantially the same core dramatic and thematic content despite their specific differences?
- Given that Faustus explicitly rejects conventional academic disciplines as inadequate before turning to magic, what does the play suggest about the actual limits and possibilities of legitimate human learning? Does the tragedy indict intellectual ambition itself, or specifically its pursuit through illegitimate, damnable means?
- How does this play's treatment of a scholar's bargain with the devil compare to other versions of the Faust legend across literary history? Does Marlowe's specific Renaissance moment and dramatic form shape this particular treatment in ways worth distinguishing from later versions of the same underlying story?
