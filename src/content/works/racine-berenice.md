## Overview

About seventy pages, Jean Racine's tragedy, first performed in 1670. Titus has just become emperor of Rome. For five years he has loved Berenice, queen of Judaea, and promised to marry her, but Roman law and opinion will not accept a foreign queen as empress.

His friend Antiochus, king of Commagene, also loves her and has kept silent. The whole play is Titus finding the will to tell Berenice, and Berenice finding the will to accept it. No one dies. Racine's preface argues that majestic sadness is enough for tragedy.

## What happens

**Titus becomes emperor.** Titus has just become emperor of Rome, a new political reality that will force him to confront the incompatibility between his five-year love for Berenice and his newly assumed public duty.

**Five years of promised love.** For five years, Titus has loved Berenice and promised to marry her, a commitment now genuinely threatened by his changed political circumstances and Rome's institutional constraints on imperial marriage.

**Roman law's refusal.** Roman law and public opinion refuse to accept a foreign queen as empress, staging political and cultural prejudice as a force capable of overriding even the emperor's own considerable personal will and years of devotion.

**Antiochus's silent love.** Titus's friend Antiochus, king of Commagene, also loves Berenice and has kept this feeling silent throughout, maintaining loyal friendship with Titus even while privately harboring his own unfulfillable devotion.

**Titus finding the will to speak.** The central dramatic action follows Titus finding the will to actually tell Berenice that Roman law forbids their marriage, a difficult confrontation between his private desire and his newly assumed public obligation.

**Berenice finding the will to accept.** Berenice, in turn, finds the will to accept this painful separation, completing a tragedy carried entirely through internal moral struggle rather than any external violence or death.

## How to read it

Read Racine's preface arguing that majestic sadness is enough for tragedy as staking out a genuinely radical dramatic theory, insisting neither death nor violent action is required for genuine tragic effect, that sustained noble suffering alone can carry a play's full weight.

Pay close attention to how building an entire tragedy around Titus finding the will to speak and Berenice finding the will to accept, with no one dying and no external villain, demonstrates how internal moral struggle can generate genuine dramatic tension without conventional tragic machinery.

Notice how Antiochus's silent, years-long love for Berenice, maintained even while loyally serving his friend Titus, adds a genuinely poignant third perspective on unfulfillable desire that deepens the play's meditation on love thwarted by circumstance.

Consider how Roman law's refusal to accept a foreign queen as empress stages political and cultural prejudice as a force genuinely capable of overriding even an emperor's personal will, institutional constraint proving more powerful than individual authority or love alone.

For a starker contrast, turn to Racine's own Andromache: where that earlier tragedy destroys its characters through a chain of mutually consuming passions and outright violence, this play achieves comparable tragic weight through restraint, separation and nobody dying at all.

## Questions it raises

- Does Racine's claim that majestic sadness alone suffices for genuine tragedy hold up against this play's actual dramatic execution? Does the absence of death and violence genuinely produce tragic effect, or does the play's restraint risk feeling emotionally insufficient by comparison to more conventional tragedy?
- How does Antiochus's silent, sustained love for Berenice, maintained throughout his loyal friendship with Titus, shape your understanding of the play's central romantic conflict? Does his unfulfilled devotion offer a meaningful alternative perspective on love's demands?
- What does Roman law and opinion's refusal to accept a foreign queen as empress suggest about the relationship between personal authority and institutional constraint? Does even an emperor's considerable power prove genuinely limited by inherited political and cultural prejudice?
- How persuasive is the play's central dramatic achievement, generating tragic weight through Titus finding the will to speak and Berenice finding the will to accept, rather than through external action or violent consequence? Does internal struggle alone sustain genuine dramatic tension?
- Given that no one dies in this tragedy, how should a reader classify its genre relative to more conventional tragic drama? Does Racine's preface successfully defend this play's status as genuine tragedy, or does its unusual restraint place it closer to another dramatic category entirely?
- What does this play's focus on public duty overriding private desire suggest about Racine's broader view of political power's relationship to personal happiness? Does the play ultimately endorse Titus's painful choice, or does it leave this judgment genuinely open to the audience?
