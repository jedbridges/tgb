## Overview

About forty pages, Percy Shelley's essay, written in 1821 in reply to Thomas Love Peacock's essay The Four Ages of Poetry, and published after Shelley's death in 1840. Peacock had argued that poetry was obsolete in an age of science.

Shelley argues that poetry in the broad sense, the imaginative making of order, underlies language, law, religion and science, and that poets have been the founders of civil society. Poetry enlarges the sympathetic imagination, which is the instrument of moral good. It closes by calling poets the unacknowledged legislators of the world.

## What happens

**Responding to Peacock's challenge.** Shelley writes this essay directly in reply to Peacock's argument that poetry was obsolete in an age of science, making the defence genuinely occasional, a direct response to a specific contemporary challenge to poetry's value.

**Poetry as the making of order.** Shelley argues that poetry in the broad sense, the imaginative making of order, underlies language itself, treating imaginative creation as prior to and generative of the structures through which humans communicate and organize.

**Poetry underlying law and religion.** Shelley extends his claim to argue that poetry underlies law and religion as well, positioning imaginative creation as the source from which these seemingly separate civil and spiritual institutions actually emerge.

**Poets as founders of civil society.** Shelley argues that poets have been the founders of civil society, a considerable claim asserting imaginative creation's foundational role in establishing the basic structures through which human communities actually govern themselves.

**Enlarging the sympathetic imagination.** Shelley argues that poetry enlarges the sympathetic imagination, which is the instrument of moral good, connecting aesthetic experience directly to ethical development rather than treating imaginative pleasure as separate from genuine morality.

**Poets as unacknowledged legislators.** The essay closes by calling poets the unacknowledged legislators of the world, crystallizing Shelley's argument into a single memorable formulation about poetry's genuine, if unrecognized, social and political influence.

## How to read it

Approach this essay as genuinely occasional rather than abstract theory, since Shelley composed it directly against Peacock's specific claim that poetry had become obsolete; the argument gains force from answering a live contemporary challenge rather than defending poetry in a vacuum.

Weigh how far Shelley expands poetry's scope beyond verse alone, treating the imaginative making of order as the generative source behind language, law, religion and science; this ambitious claim asks readers to reconsider what counts as poetry's actual domain.

Trace the connection Shelley draws between enlarged sympathetic imagination and moral good; the essay gives aesthetic pleasure direct ethical consequence, asking whether imaginative engagement with literature genuinely cultivates the capacity for moral feeling toward others.

Sit with the closing claim that poets are the unacknowledged legislators of the world as the essay's most quoted line, then test it against the specific argument that built toward it, rather than treating it as a free-standing slogan detachable from what precedes it.

This essay pairs naturally with Shelley's own poetic practice: turning from the Defence to Adonais shows the same writer applying, in an actual elegy for Keats, the very claims about imagination and moral sympathy he argues for here in the abstract.

## Questions it raises

- How persuasive is Shelley's expansion of poetry to mean the imaginative making of order generally, rather than verse specifically? Does this broad definition strengthen his defence, or does it risk making the term so expansive it loses useful meaning?
- Does the claim that poetry enlarges the sympathetic imagination, which is the instrument of moral good, hold up under scrutiny? Can imaginative engagement with literature be shown to actually produce moral improvement, or does this connection remain an assertion rather than a demonstration?
- What does writing this essay specifically against Peacock's claim that poetry was obsolete in an age of science suggest about the relationship Shelley assumes between poetry and scientific or technical progress? Does he adequately answer Peacock's actual argument, or does he change the terms of the debate?
- How should a reader weigh the famous closing claim, poets as unacknowledged legislators, against actual historical evidence for poetry's political influence? Does this formulation hold as literal claim, or does it work better as a deliberately provocative overstatement?
- Given that Shelley claims poets founded civil society itself, how does this claim relate to more conventional accounts crediting lawgivers, philosophers or religious founders with this role? Does Shelley's argument compete with or complement these more standard explanations?
- What does this essay's continued relevance to debates about the arts and humanities' value suggest about the durability of Shelley's specific arguments? Do his claims about imagination and sympathy still offer useful resources for defending literature's importance today?
