## Overview

About thirty pages, a short story by Flannery O'Connor, first published in 1953 and the title piece of her first collection, gathered in 1955. A Georgia family sets out on a car trip to Florida, over the objections of the grandmother, who would rather visit relatives in Tennessee and who has read that an escaped killer called The Misfit is loose.

Her insistence on seeing a plantation house she remembers leads them down a dirt road, into a ditch, and into the hands of The Misfit and his men. As her family is taken into the woods and shot, the grandmother talks with the killer about Jesus and, at the last moment, reaches out to him.

## What happens

**The family's departure for Florida.** A Georgia family sets out on a car trip to Florida, the grandmother objecting because she would rather visit relatives in Tennessee, mentioning an escaped killer called The Misfit more to get her way than from genuine concern.

**The remembered plantation house.** The grandmother's insistence on seeing a plantation house she remembers from her youth, a minor vanity rooted in nostalgic self-regard, persuades the family to take a detour that will prove fatal.

**Into the ditch.** The detour leads the family down a dirt road and into a ditch, stranding them exactly where The Misfit and his men happen to be, converting the grandmother's small self-indulgence into catastrophic consequence.

**The family taken into the woods.** The Misfit's men take the family members into the woods one by one and shoot them, while the grandmother remains behind, increasingly desperate as she realizes what is happening to those she loves.

**The Misfit's theological clarity.** The grandmother talks with The Misfit about Jesus, and he articulates his own stark theological position, that Jesus's resurrection either happened and demands total transformation or did not and nothing ultimately matters.

**The grandmother's final reach.** At the last moment, the grandmother reaches out to The Misfit, a gesture of sudden grace breaking through her complacent selfishness only in the instant before he kills her.

## How to read it

Read the grandmother's insistence on seeing the remembered plantation house as demonstrating how genuinely small moral failures, a minor vanity rooted in nostalgic self-regard, can produce catastrophic consequences entirely disproportionate to their apparent triviality.

Pay close attention to The Misfit's own theological clarity, his insistence that Jesus's resurrection either happened and demands total transformation or did not and nothing ultimately matters; this forces the story's central question into stark relief rather than leaving it comfortably ambiguous.

Notice how the grandmother's last-moment reach toward The Misfit, offering genuine recognition of shared humanity precisely as he prepares to kill her, stages grace arriving not as reward for prior virtue but as sudden gift breaking through complacency.

Consider O'Connor's own insistence that this violence serves specifically theological rather than merely sensational purpose; the story challenges readers to consider whether shocking brutality can function as legitimate vehicle for genuine spiritual insight rather than gratuitous shock alone.

Read this story alongside Faulkner's A Rose for Emily to compare two Southern Gothic treatments of ordinary domestic surfaces concealing sudden, disturbing violence, each writer using regional specificity to stage genuinely universal moral and spiritual questions.

## Questions it raises

- Does the grandmother's last-moment gesture toward The Misfit represent genuine spiritual transformation, or does the story leave open whether this reach constitutes authentic grace or merely a final, desperate act of self-preservation misread as something more?
- How persuasive is The Misfit's theological framework, that everything hinges on whether Jesus's resurrection actually happened? Does the story ultimately endorse this stark either-or framing, or does it present The Misfit's clarity as itself a kind of moral failure?
- What does the grandmother's manipulative mention of The Misfit's escape, offered to get her way rather than from genuine concern, suggest about how the story frames the relationship between selfishness and eventual catastrophe throughout its unfolding action?
- How should a reader weigh the story's violence against its claimed theological purpose? Does the brutal killing of the family serve genuine spiritual insight, or does the shock risk overwhelming whatever deeper meaning O'Connor intended it to convey?
- Given that The Misfit himself seems to recognize something significant in the grandmother's final gesture, what does his own reaction suggest about how the story understands the relationship between violence, recognition and whatever grace might mean within its world?
- What does this story's continued prominence in discussions of Southern Gothic fiction and Catholic literary tradition suggest about how O'Connor's specific combination of regional detail, dark humor and theological seriousness has proven durable for readers across different backgrounds?
