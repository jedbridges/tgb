## Overview

About three hundred and fifty pages in two volumes, composed largely by dictation at considerable speed in 1815 and published two years later in 1817, written by Samuel Taylor Coleridge during a period of genuine personal difficulty, burdened by debt, damaged health and a longstanding opium dependency that shadows the book's composition even though it is rarely addressed directly within it. Coleridge subtitled the work Biographical Sketches of My Literary Life and Opinions, a description that undersells how genuinely strange and hybrid the resulting book actually is, part memoir, part sustained engagement with German philosophy, and part extended, often pointed literary criticism, held together less by systematic organization than by Coleridge's own restless, digressive intelligence moving between registers as the argument demands.

The book's two volumes divide, roughly, between autobiography and theory in the first and practical criticism in the second. The first volume traces Coleridge's early education, his formative reading, and above all his friendship and creative partnership with William Wordsworth, before turning to an extended, philosophically demanding attempt to ground a theory of poetic imagination in German idealist philosophy, particularly the work of Kant and Schelling. The second volume applies the theoretical apparatus built in the first directly to Wordsworth's own poetry and to Wordsworth's stated theory of poetic diction, producing one of English criticism's most famous instances of serious, sustained critical disagreement conducted between genuine friends and collaborators.

## The argument

**Early education and the making of a poet.** The book opens autobiographically, with Coleridge recounting his own schooling, his early and formative reading, particularly in Renaissance and metaphysical poetry, and the development of his critical sensibilities well before he had begun writing the poetry on which his later reputation would rest. These early chapters establish Coleridge's lifelong habit of finding theoretical and philosophical significance in what might otherwise read as ordinary autobiographical anecdote.

**The Wordsworth partnership and Lyrical Ballads.** Coleridge devotes considerable space to recounting his collaboration with Wordsworth on the 1798 volume Lyrical Ballads, a landmark of English Romanticism to which Coleridge contributed The Rime of the Ancient Mariner among other poems, describing the division of labor the two poets settled on, broadly, Wordsworth taking ordinary subjects and treating them with the strangeness of genuine perception, Coleridge taking supernatural or strange subjects and grounding them in enough psychological truth to earn what he famously calls the willing suspension of disbelief that constitutes poetic faith.

**Imagination and fancy.** The book's most philosophically demanding and most historically influential section works through German idealist philosophy, above all Kant and Schelling, in pursuit of a rigorous distinction between two mental faculties Coleridge believed earlier English criticism had carelessly conflated. Fancy, in Coleridge's account, is a merely mechanical faculty, combining and recombining images and ideas that are themselves already fixed, complete and given by the ordinary laws of association. Imagination, by contrast, and above all what Coleridge calls the primary imagination, is an active, unifying, almost creative power, one he describes, in the book's most quoted single passage, as a finite echo within the human mind of the infinite, eternal act of creation itself, capable of genuinely dissolving, diffusing and recreating its materials into something new rather than merely rearranging what already exists.

**Critiquing Wordsworth's theory of poetic diction.** The second volume turns this theoretical apparatus directly on Wordsworth's own famous preface to Lyrical Ballads, in which Wordsworth had argued that poetry should employ the real language of ordinary men, particularly rural men, speaking under genuine emotional pressure, rather than the artificial, elevated diction conventional poetic theory demanded. Coleridge, while praising Wordsworth's actual poetic genius without qualification throughout, argues at considerable length and with close textual analysis that this stated theory does not accurately describe what Wordsworth's own best poems actually do, that Wordsworth's greatest writing depends on exactly the kind of shaping, selecting, elevating imaginative power the preface's theory seems to disclaim.

**A discursive, digressive method.** Running throughout both volumes is Coleridge's characteristic digressive habit, extended philosophical tangents, personal anecdotes, sudden shifts of subject and register, a method that has frustrated many readers since the book's first publication but that also reflects, quite directly, the very theory of imaginative, associative mental activity the book is simultaneously trying to describe and analyze.

## How to read it

Read the autobiographical first chapters and the philosophical middle chapters as building toward the imagination and fancy distinction in Chapter XIII, which functions as the book's genuine theoretical center even though it arrives roughly halfway through the whole work; earlier material makes considerably more sense once that distinction is in view.

Approach the German philosophy sections with patience rather than expecting full systematic clarity; Coleridge's engagement with Kant and Schelling was genuine but also somewhat improvisational, assembled at speed and under real personal pressure, and scholars have long debated exactly how much of the philosophical apparatus is fully original to Coleridge versus adapted, sometimes very closely, from his German sources.

Read the second volume's critique of Wordsworth as a genuine test case for the theoretical claims of the first: Coleridge is not simply praising or attacking a friend's work but using close, specific textual analysis to show where he believes Wordsworth's own stated theory and his own actual poetic practice diverge.

Notice how the book's digressive, associative structure itself performs, whether deliberately or not, something like the difference between fancy and imagination that Coleridge is arguing for theoretically, a text that keeps dissolving and recombining its own materials rather than proceeding in a fixed, linear, purely expository way.

Read this book alongside Wordsworth's own preface to Lyrical Ballads directly, since Coleridge's second volume argument depends entirely on specific claims in that preface, and the critical disagreement is considerably sharper and more legible when both texts are read side by side.

## Questions it raises

- Does Coleridge's distinction between imagination and fancy hold up as a rigorous theoretical claim, or does it function more as a persuasive redescription that classifies whatever poetry Coleridge already admires as imaginative and whatever he does not as merely fanciful?
- What does it mean that Coleridge could praise Wordsworth's poetic genius without reservation while arguing at length that Wordsworth's own theory of that genius was substantially mistaken? Is this a model for how serious criticism should handle friendship, or does the friendship compromise the criticism's honesty?
- How much does the book's philosophical authority depend on Coleridge's engagement with Kant and Schelling being genuinely original, and how should a reader's assessment change if some of that material turns out to be more closely adapted from German sources than the text itself acknowledges?
- Is the book's notoriously digressive, associative structure a genuine weakness, or does it enact, formally, the very theory of imaginative mental activity the book argues for philosophically?
- Coleridge's willing suspension of disbelief has become one of the most widely used phrases in discussions of fiction and drama, often stripped of its original context. How does the phrase's meaning change when returned to Coleridge's specific argument about supernatural poetry in Lyrical Ballads?
- What responsibility does a reader have to account for the circumstances of the book's composition, dictated rapidly under real financial and physical distress, when evaluating its philosophical rigor and its occasional inconsistencies?
