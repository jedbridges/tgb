## Overview

About a hundred and fifty pages, William Harrison's survey of England under Elizabeth I, first published in 1577 and revised in 1587 as the introduction to Holinshed's Chronicles, the sprawling history of Britain that Shakespeare himself read and drew on repeatedly for his history plays and tragedies. Harrison, a country clergyman rather than a professional historian, wrote his description not as formal political or military narrative but as a wide-ranging survey of Elizabethan society and daily life, covering the Church, the universities, the precise ranks of society from gentlemen down to laborers, food and diet, clothing, houses and their recently spread chimneys and glass windows, the law and its punishments, fairs, markets, inns, dogs, gardens, the navy and the treatment of the poor.

The result has considerably outlasted its original modest purpose as mere introductory material, standing today as the fullest surviving firsthand picture of ordinary daily life in Shakespeare's England, a level of concrete social and material detail that the Chronicles' own grander historical narrative, and most formal histories of the period, rarely bother to record.

## What happens

**The Church and universities.** Harrison opens his survey with the Church and England's universities, describing their organization, their role in Elizabethan education and religious life, and giving a firsthand contemporary account of institutions later readers would otherwise know only through later historical reconstruction.

**The ranks of society.** Harrison catalogues English society rank by rank, from gentlemen and nobility down through yeomen, artificers and day laborers, describing precisely what distinguished each rank's manner of living, dress and social standing, giving readers a firsthand sense of how Elizabethans themselves understood their own social hierarchy.

**Food, clothing and housing.** Harrison devotes considerable attention to the material texture of Elizabethan daily life, describing typical diet and food preparation, clothing customs across social ranks, and housing construction, including his notable observation that chimneys and glass windows had spread through English houses only recently, a change within living memory that older residents could still recall predating.

**Law, punishment and daily institutions.** Harrison surveys the English legal system and its punishments in direct, unsparing detail, and extends his account to the practical institutions of everyday life, fairs, markets and inns, along with more unexpected subjects like notable breeds of English dogs and the state of the navy and the treatment of the poor.

**A firsthand picture for later readers.** Taken together, Harrison's wide-ranging survey builds an unusually complete contemporary picture of Elizabethan daily life across virtually every social and material dimension, a resource historians and readers of Shakespeare's own period have relied on precisely because so few other contemporary sources attempted anything comparably comprehensive.

## How to read it

Read Harrison's survey as a genuine primary source for the physical and social world Shakespeare's plays assume rather than directly describe; where the plays give you kings, battles and grand rhetoric, Harrison gives you the houses, food, clothing and social hierarchy the plays' original audiences actually lived within.

Pay close attention to Harrison's observation about the recent spread of chimneys and glass windows as a small but genuinely significant piece of social history, a concrete material change Harrison treats as meaningful evidence of broader transformation in English daily life within a single generation.

Notice how directly Harrison's catalogue of social ranks reflects contemporary Elizabethan self-understanding rather than any later historian's retrospective category, offering a firsthand sense of how the period's own social hierarchy was actually described and understood by someone living within it.

Consider Harrison's inclusion of seemingly minor subjects, dogs, gardens, inns, alongside weightier matters like the Church, universities and the law, as itself meaningful; this range suggests Harrison understood comprehensive daily life, not only formal institutions, as worth recording for posterity.

Read this description alongside any of Shakespeare's history plays or Hamlet to see how directly the social world Harrison catalogues, its ranks, its material culture, its legal punishments, underlies the assumptions Shakespeare's original audiences would have brought to the theater without needing any of it explained.
