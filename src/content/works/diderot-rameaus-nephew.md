## Overview

About a hundred pages, a philosophical dialogue written by Denis Diderot over an extended and genuinely uncertain period, likely composed and revised sometime between roughly 1761 and 1774, and never published during Diderot's own lifetime, surviving only in manuscript until Goethe encountered a copy and translated it into German, publishing that translation in 1805, well before a reliable French original text was established and published. The dialogue takes the form of a wide-ranging conversation in a Paris café between two figures, Moi, a philosopher clearly modeled closely on Diderot's own voice and values, and Lui, the nephew of the celebrated composer Jean-Philippe Rameau, a failed musician who has settled into a precarious existence as a parasite, flatterer and entertainer among Paris's wealthy patrons.

The dialogue's genuine strangeness, and likely part of why Diderot never published it, lies in how thoroughly Lui's arguments in defense of his own parasitic, self-interested way of life resist easy philosophical defeat; rather than staging a simple contest Moi's reasoned virtue clearly wins, Diderot gives Lui genuine brilliance, candor and rhetorical force, producing a work that unsettles Enlightenment confidence in virtue's ultimate reward without ever fully collapsing that confidence either.

## The argument

**Lui's defense of a parasitic life.** At the dialogue's center is Lui's sustained, remarkably candid defense of his own existence, surviving through flattery, mimicry and calculated self-interest rather than through honest labor or genuine artistic achievement of his own. Rather than expressing shame or offering excuses, Lui argues directly and with evident intellectual pleasure that his way of life represents a rational, even admirable adaptation to how Parisian high society actually operates, a system that rewards entertaining flattery considerably better than it rewards honest, unglamorous merit.

**Genius against virtue.** Lui advances a genuinely provocative claim that runs through much of the dialogue: that genius and conventional virtue rarely travel together, and that demanding moral respectability from genuinely talented or brilliant people may actually work against the production of real artistic or intellectual achievement. Moi resists this claim without ever fully refuting it, and the dialogue leaves the tension between genius and virtue considerably less resolved than a more conventional philosophical treatise defending virtue might have allowed.

**The solo performances.** In the dialogue's most theatrically vivid sequences, Lui performs, entirely alone at the café table, extended imitations of complete operas and imagined society dinners, switching voices, postures and even imagined instruments with total physical commitment, drawing onlookers and producing a spectacle Moi records with genuine, largely unresolved admiration despite his continuing philosophical disapproval of the man performing it. These performances make the dialogue function as much as theatrical writing as philosophical argument, and complicate any easy separation between Lui's parasitic cunning and what might be genuine, if badly channeled, artistic talent.

**The social critique.** Lui's sharpest and most genuinely difficult argument redirects moral responsibility away from his own individual choices and onto the broader social system that produces and rewards them: a society that pays flatterers better than it pays honest, hardworking people, he argues, has no legitimate grounds for moral shock when clever, ambitious people become flatterers rather than honest laborers, making his own corruption less a personal moral failing than a rational, almost inevitable adaptation to real incentives Parisian society itself has established.

**An unresolved ending.** The dialogue closes without any clean philosophical resolution, Moi neither fully converted to Lui's cynical realism nor able to claim decisive victory for his own position, an ending that reflects the dialogue's larger refusal to stage a simple contest between clearly right and clearly wrong positions, leaving readers considerably less settled than a more conventional philosophical dialogue in the Socratic tradition typically would.

## How to read it

Read Lui's arguments with genuine openness to their actual force rather than assuming Moi's philosophical position must automatically win the exchange; Diderot's real achievement lies precisely in making Lui's defense of a parasitic, self-interested life genuinely difficult to dismiss, however morally troubling its conclusions remain.

Pay close attention to the solo performance sequences as doing real argumentative work rather than functioning as mere comic or theatrical interlude; Lui's evident talent in these performances complicates any simple judgment that he possesses no genuine gift at all, raising real questions about the relationship between his parasitic social role and whatever authentic artistic capacity he might actually possess.

Notice how the dialogue's genuine formal instability, no clear victor, no tidy moral resolution, itself constitutes part of its philosophical achievement, resisting the more conventional Enlightenment confidence that reasoned argument, properly conducted, should straightforwardly vindicate virtue over vice.

Consider the dialogue's unusual publication history, unpublished by Diderot himself and reaching print first in German translation decades before an established French text existed, as relevant context for understanding why this particular work remained so genuinely unsettled and unsettling relative to Diderot's other, more confidently Enlightenment-aligned writing.

Read this dialogue alongside Hegel's later treatment of Lui as a philosophically significant figure of fractured, self-alienated modern consciousness in the Phenomenology of Spirit, to see how a work Diderot himself apparently could not settle on how to present became, for a later and very different philosophical tradition, a genuinely important and productive image of divided selfhood.

## Questions it raises

- Does Lui's argument that his parasitic way of life represents a rational adaptation to Parisian society's actual incentives successfully shift moral responsibility from himself to the broader social system, or does this argument function more as clever self-justification than genuine ethical analysis?
- What is the effect of Diderot giving Lui's position such genuine rhetorical brilliance and candor rather than making him an easily dismissed villain? Does this choice strengthen the dialogue's philosophical seriousness, or does it risk making vice too attractive relative to Moi's comparatively duller virtue?
- How should a reader interpret Lui's extraordinary solo performances, as evidence of genuine artistic talent wasted on parasitic performance, as further evidence of his skill at manipulation and flattery, or as something the dialogue deliberately leaves ambiguous between these two readings?
- Why might Diderot have chosen never to publish this dialogue during his own lifetime? What does this choice suggest about how comfortable he actually was with the unresolved tension between Lui's and Moi's positions, compared to his more confidently argued published philosophical work?
- Does the dialogue's genuine claim that genius and virtue rarely coincide hold up as a general observation, or does it depend too heavily on Lui's own particular, self-interested framing of the question to constitute a genuinely persuasive philosophical position?
- What does Hegel's later use of Lui as a figure of divided, self-alienated modern consciousness reveal about the dialogue's philosophical afterlife? Does this later philosophical appropriation illuminate something genuinely present in Diderot's original text, or does it impose a considerably later philosophical framework onto material that was not originally designed to support it?
