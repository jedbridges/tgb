## Overview

A twelve-page pamphlet, published in 1729, in the voice of a reasonable public-spirited projector who calculates that Ireland's poverty could be relieved if the children of the poor were fattened and sold at one year old as food for the rich. He works through the economics with care, recommends recipes, and lists the advantages to landlords, parents and the nation, dismissing gentler remedies as impractical.

The essay is Swift's most savage attack on English policy in Ireland and on the habit of treating human beings as figures in a calculation.

## What happens

**Calculating Ireland's poverty as a solvable problem.** The projector opens by calculating that Ireland's poverty could be relieved through a single decisive intervention, framing extreme human suffering from the outset as a problem awaiting the correct policy solution.

**Proposing that children be sold as food.** He proposes, as that solution, that the children of the poor be fattened and sold at one year old as food for the rich, the essay's central and unrelenting monstrous conceit.

**Working through the economics.** The projector works through the economics of his proposal with evident care, treating the sale and consumption of children as a matter of ordinary calculation rather than horror.

**Recommending recipes.** He recommends actual recipes for preparing the children, extending the proposal's calm reasonableness into a register of domestic practical advice.

**Listing advantages to landlords, parents and the nation.** The projector lists the advantages his scheme offers landlords, parents and the nation collectively, presenting mass infanticide as a rational public good distributed across every interested party.

**Dismissing gentler remedies.** He dismisses gentler remedies as impractical beside his own proposal, refusing every humane alternative in the same reasonable tone he uses to defend his monstrous solution.

## How to read it

Read the projector's calm, reasonable voice as itself the essay's central satirical instrument, its horror depending entirely on a narrator who never once breaks character to signal what he is actually proposing.

Pay close attention to how calculating that Ireland's poverty could be relieved by selling poor children as food exposes the actual logic of policies that treat human suffering as a problem in arithmetic rather than a moral emergency.

Notice how working through the economics with genuine apparent care, and recommending actual recipes, commits fully to the reasonable voice, letting the proposal's monstrousness emerge entirely from its own careful, uninterrupted logic.

Consider how listing the advantages to landlords, parents and the nation, while dismissing gentler remedies as impractical, mimics precisely the rhetorical structure of genuine contemporary policy proposals rather than any obviously exaggerated fantasy.

Read this pamphlet alongside Gulliver's Travels to compare two different satirical strategies, the fantastic displacement of the Houyhnhnms and Yahoos against this essay's unbroken commitment to a single reasonable, monstrous voice speaking directly about real contemporary Ireland.

## Questions it raises

- Does the projector's unbroken reasonable voice make the essay's satire more effective than direct moral outrage would be, or does sustaining the horror without comment risk being misread as genuine advocacy?
- How does calculating extreme human suffering as a problem in arithmetic, rather than treating it as a moral emergency, expose something true about how real policy actually gets discussed and justified?
- What does the essay's willingness to work through recipes and practical details, rather than stopping at the general proposal, contribute to its satirical force? Does specificity intensify the horror or risk becoming grotesque for its own sake?
- Given that the essay targets English policy in Ireland specifically, how much of its force depends on the reader knowing that historical context, and how much survives as a more general indictment of calculating approaches to poverty?
- Does dismissing gentler remedies as impractical, within the same reasonable tone used for the monstrous central proposal, tell readers something about how real policy debates dismiss humane alternatives as insufficiently rigorous?
- What responsibility does satire bear for readers who might, even briefly, fail to recognize the irony? Does the essay's total commitment to its premise create genuine risk of being taken at face value?
