## Overview

About fifteen pages, John Milton's short open letter to the reformer Samuel Hartlib, published in 1644, setting out a plan for an academy for boys from twelve to twenty-one. Milton defines the end of learning as repairing the ruins of our first parents by regaining a right knowledge of God.

He proposes a curriculum that moves from languages through agriculture, mathematics, natural philosophy, ethics, politics and theology to poetry and rhetoric, with music, fencing and military exercise in the afternoons, an integrated program forming the whole person rather than only the intellect.

## What happens

**The theological end of learning.** Milton opens by defining the end of all learning as repairing the ruins of our first parents by regaining a right knowledge of God, grounding his entire proposed program in this specifically religious purpose.

**The academy's intended students.** Milton proposes the academy for boys from twelve to twenty-one, a considerably longer and more comprehensive span than either grammar school or university alone typically covered in his own period.

**Languages and practical subjects.** The curriculum begins with languages before moving into agriculture and other practical subjects, establishing linguistic competence as the necessary foundation for everything the student will study afterward.

**Mathematics and natural philosophy.** Students proceed to mathematics and natural philosophy, building genuine scientific understanding of the physical world before advancing to the more directly human and civic subjects that follow.

**Ethics, politics and theology.** The curriculum moves through ethics, politics and theology, preparing students for both private moral life and public civic responsibility before culminating in the highest theological knowledge.

**Poetry, rhetoric and physical exercise.** The program concludes with poetry and rhetoric, and includes music, fencing and military exercise in the afternoons, forming eloquence and physical capacity alongside the intellectual and moral training.

## How to read it

Read Milton's definition of learning's end as repairing the ruins of our first parents as the interpretive key to everything that follows; the specific curriculum only makes sense as an attempt to realize this theological goal through carefully ordered practical instruction.

Pay close attention to the curriculum's deliberate sequence, languages, then practical subjects, then mathematics and natural philosophy, then ethics, politics and theology, then poetry and rhetoric; Milton insists each stage genuinely prepares for the next rather than standing independently.

Notice how including music, fencing and military exercise alongside intellectual study reveals Milton's conviction that education should form the whole person, anticipating later humanist arguments against purely bookish learning that neglects body and civic capacity.

Consider the tract's practical, occasional character, addressed to an actual reformer proposing an actual academy, as distinct from more abstract treatises on education's general value; Milton is thinking concretely about institutional design and specific course sequencing.

Read this tract alongside Milton's own Areopagitica to see how his convictions about intellectual formation and free inquiry, developed at length there regarding publication and censorship, connect to the more practical curricular proposals he offers here for how students should actually be taught.

## Questions it raises

- Does grounding education's end in repairing the ruins of our first parents through right knowledge of God limit the tract's relevance for readers outside Milton's specific theological framework, or does the underlying educational philosophy transfer to more secular contexts?
- How persuasive is Milton's proposed curricular sequence, moving from languages through practical subjects to mathematics, ethics and finally poetry? Does this order reflect a genuinely defensible theory of how learning should build, or does it simply reflect his own particular intellectual formation?
- What does including music, fencing and military exercise alongside intellectual study suggest about Milton's broader understanding of education's proper scope? Does this integration of physical and civic training with intellectual formation offer a genuine alternative to purely academic models?
- How does the tract's practical, occasional framing, an open letter to an actual reformer, shape its argument compared to a more abstract philosophical treatise on education? Does this specificity strengthen or limit its persuasive force?
- Given that Milton proposes education spanning ages twelve to twenty-one within a single academy, how does this compare to the separate grammar school and university structure of his own period? What does this consolidation suggest about his view of institutional reform?
- What does this short tract's continued relevance to discussions of liberal education and curricular design suggest about Milton's underlying educational insights? Do his specific proposals still offer useful resources for evaluating contemporary educational structures?
