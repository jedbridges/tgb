## Overview

About a hundred and ten pages, Percy Shelley's verse tragedy, published in 1819, based on a Roman case of 1599. Count Francesco Cenci, a nobleman whose crimes the Pope repeatedly pardons for money, celebrates the deaths of two of his sons and then rapes his daughter Beatrice.

With her stepmother and brother she hires assassins to kill him. The murder is discovered, and Beatrice, who refuses to confess or repent under torture, is condemned to death with her family. Shelley wrote it for the stage, but its subject kept it from being performed in Britain until 1886.

## What happens

**Cenci's crimes and papal pardons.** Count Francesco Cenci commits repeated crimes that the Pope pardons for money, an arrangement establishing from the play's opening how institutional authority has been purchased into complicity with his ongoing abuse.

**Celebrating his sons' deaths.** Cenci celebrates the deaths of two of his own sons, displaying a monstrous cruelty toward his own family that the play presents as consistent with, rather than separate from, his broader pattern of abuse.

**The rape of Beatrice.** Cenci rapes his daughter Beatrice, the play's central atrocity, an act of violation within the family that institutional authority has already shown itself unwilling or unable to prevent or punish.

**Beatrice hires assassins.** With her stepmother and brother, Beatrice hires assassins to kill her father, choosing private violence as the only available response after institutional authority has repeatedly failed to check his crimes.

**The murder discovered.** The murder of Count Cenci is discovered, bringing official investigation to bear on Beatrice and her co-conspirators despite the institutional failures that had made their private action seem necessary in the first place.

**Beatrice's refusal and condemnation.** Beatrice refuses to confess or repent under torture, holding firm even as this refusal guarantees her execution, and she is condemned to death alongside her family for the murder.

## How to read it

Read Count Cenci's crimes repeatedly pardoned by the Pope for money as exposing how easily institutional authority, even religious authority claiming moral oversight, could be purchased into complicity with sustained abuse, implicating structure rather than isolating evil in one monstrous individual.

Pay close attention to how Beatrice's rape and her subsequent choice to hire assassins rather than seek official recourse dramatizes how thoroughly corrupted institutions can leave victims no legitimate path to justice, forcing private violence as the only available response.

Notice how Beatrice's refusal to confess or repent under torture, holding firm even as this refusal guarantees her execution alongside her family, stages a genuinely difficult question about whether resistance to unjust legal process constitutes moral integrity or simply compounds catastrophe.

Consider how the play, written explicitly for the stage yet kept from British performance until 1886 by its subject matter, has a suppressed performance history that mirrors its central theme: institutions refusing to confront uncomfortable truths about power and complicity.

Read this tragedy alongside Hawthorne's Scarlet Letter to compare two very different treatments of how institutions, religious and legal, respond to sexual transgression and its victims, one exposing outright corruption, the other examining more ambiguous social hypocrisy.

## Questions it raises

- Does the play's depiction of the Pope's repeated pardons for money successfully implicate institutional religious authority in Cenci's crimes, or does this specific detail risk reducing a complex historical and religious institution to simple corruption for dramatic convenience?
- How should a reader judge Beatrice's decision to hire assassins rather than seek any official recourse? Does the play present this choice as morally justified given institutional failure, or does it complicate her sympathetic status by making her complicit in premeditated murder?
- What does Beatrice's refusal to confess or repent under torture, despite this refusal guaranteeing her execution, suggest about the play's understanding of integrity under unjust legal process? Does her steadfastness represent genuine virtue or a tragic miscalculation?
- How does the play's suppressed performance history in Britain until 1886 relate to its actual content? Does this censorship suggest audiences and authorities recognized something genuinely dangerous in the play's exposure of institutional complicity in abuse?
- Given that Shelley based this tragedy on an actual historical case from 1599, how does dramatizing real events shape the play's moral claims differently than a purely fictional tragedy might? Does historical basis add or complicate the work's persuasive force?
- What does the play's title, naming the family rather than any individual character, suggest about where Shelley locates the tragedy's ultimate source? Does this framing implicate the family as institution alongside the specific individuals who commit and suffer its crimes?
