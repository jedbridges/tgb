## Overview

About six hundred pages, George Santayana's autobiography, written in Rome in his old age and published in three parts between 1944 and 1953, the last after his death. It recounts his Spanish childhood, his boyhood in Boston, and his years as a student and professor at Harvard, with portraits of William James and Josiah Royce.

The memoir continues with his friendships in England and his resignation from teaching in 1912 to live in Europe as a writer. It is as much a book of character sketches and reflections on being a foreigner everywhere as it is a life story.

## What happens

**Spanish childhood.** Santayana recounts his Spanish childhood, establishing the early formative experiences that would remain in genuine tension with his subsequent American education and career throughout the rest of his life.

**Boston boyhood.** Santayana narrates his boyhood in Boston, describing his transition into American life and the specific cultural and social adjustments this move required of him as a young immigrant.

**Student and professor at Harvard.** Santayana traces his years as a student and eventually professor at Harvard, including detailed portraits of William James and Josiah Royce, colleagues he observed closely during this extended academic period.

**Friendships in England.** Santayana describes his friendships in England, extending the memoir's geographical and social range beyond his American academic career into a broader transatlantic intellectual and personal life.

**Resigning from teaching.** Santayana resigns from teaching in 1912 to live in Europe as a writer, a considerable personal and professional gamble abandoning Harvard's institutional security for independent literary and philosophical work.

**Reflections on being a foreigner everywhere.** Throughout, the memoir develops sustained reflections on being a foreigner everywhere, Santayana's Spanish origins and American formation never fully reconciled into a single settled national identity.

## How to read it

Read Santayana's decision to write this autobiography in Rome during his own old age as giving the work genuine retrospective distance from the childhood, education and career it describes, memory considerably filtered through decades of subsequent reflection.

Pay close attention to the portraits of William James and Josiah Royce; these give readers direct firsthand access to two major American philosophers through a colleague's own intimate professional and personal observation rather than only through their own published writing.

Notice how the resignation from teaching in 1912 to live in Europe as a writer represents a considerable personal and professional gamble, abandoning Harvard's institutional security for independent literary work the autobiography retrospectively frames as necessary rather than merely convenient.

Consider how reading this as much a book of character sketches and reflections on being a foreigner everywhere as a conventional life story demonstrates how autobiographical form can accommodate genuine philosophical reflection on identity alongside straightforward biography.

For the philosophical system this life prepared him to write, Santayana's own Life of Reason is the natural next stop: that earlier systematic work gains considerable biographical context once read alongside the formative years and departures this memoir traces in such close detail.
