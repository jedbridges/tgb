## Overview

About three hundred pages, Shakespeare's drama, written around 1597 to 1598. The rebellion continues after Shrewsbury but is ended by a trick, when Prince John promises the rebel lords redress and then arrests them once their army disperses. Falstaff, now old and ill, recruits soldiers in Gloucestershire and dines with Justice Shallow, while the sick king broods on the crown he took.

Hal takes the crown from his sleeping father's pillow, is reconciled with him before he dies, and is crowned Henry V. In the last scene he publicly rejects Falstaff.

## What happens

**The rebellion continues.** The rebellion against King Henry continues after the earlier battle at Shrewsbury, establishing the ongoing political instability that will drive the play's central action toward eventual resolution.

**Prince John's trick.** Prince John promises the rebel lords redress for their grievances, then arrests them once their army has dispersed on the strength of that promise, ending the rebellion through calculated deception.

**Falstaff's decline.** Falstaff, now visibly old and ill, recruits soldiers in Gloucestershire, his characteristic wit and appetite for life increasingly shadowed by genuine physical decline and approaching mortality.

**Dining with Justice Shallow.** Falstaff dines with the comic Justice Shallow, a sequence combining continued comic vitality with an underlying melancholy about time's passage and diminishment.

**The sick king's brooding.** The dying king broods on the crown he took, reflecting on the burdens and moral costs of the power he seized, a meditation running parallel to Falstaff's own physical decline.

**Hal's succession and rejection of Falstaff.** Hal takes the crown from his sleeping father's pillow, is reconciled with him before his death, and is crowned Henry V, before publicly rejecting Falstaff in the play's final scene.

## How to read it

Read Prince John's ending of the rebellion by trick as staging political victory through betrayal of given word rather than honorable combat, a considerably darker resolution than the earlier play's more straightforwardly heroic single combat at Shrewsbury.

Pay close attention to Falstaff's decline into old age and illness; this gives the play's comic relief genuine melancholy undertone, mortality shadowing even his characteristic wit and appetite for life throughout the Gloucestershire scenes.

Notice how Hal taking the crown from his sleeping father's pillow, believing him already dead, stages the son's genuine ambivalence about succession with unusual psychological directness, desire for power surfacing even amid apparent filial grief.

Consider how Hal's public rejection of Falstaff in the final scene completes the political education begun across both parts, the newly crowned king decisively sacrificing personal affection for the disciplined public role kingship now requires.

Read this play as the direct continuation of Henry IV, Part 1: the earlier play's Shrewsbury victory only sets up this darker second half, where Hal's transformation into a king finally completes with Falstaff's public rejection.

## Questions it raises

- Does Prince John's trick ending the rebellion through betrayed promise represent necessary political pragmatism, or does the play ultimately condemn this deception as a genuine moral failure compromising the legitimacy of the crown's eventual victory?
- How does Falstaff's visible physical decline shape your understanding of his comic function throughout this second part? Does approaching mortality deepen the poignancy of his characteristic humor, or does it simply confirm his diminishing relevance to the play's central political concerns?
- What does Hal taking the crown while believing his father already dead reveal about his genuine feelings toward succession and power? Does this moment complicate the more straightforward political education the play otherwise traces?
- How should a reader weigh Hal's public rejection of Falstaff against their considerable earlier friendship? Does this final scene represent necessary maturation into responsible kingship, or a troubling capacity for calculated emotional abandonment?
- Given that this play continues directly from Henry IV, Part 1, how does its considerably darker tone, political betrayal, physical decline, difficult succession, relate to that earlier play's more energetic and comically buoyant treatment of similar political material?
- What does the parallel between Falstaff's physical decline and the king's own mortality suggest about the play's broader treatment of aging, power and the body? Does Shakespeare draw a deliberate connection between these two very different figures approaching death?
