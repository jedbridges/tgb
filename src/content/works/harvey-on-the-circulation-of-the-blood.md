## Overview

About fifty pages, two open letters by the English physician William Harvey, published in Cambridge in 1649, addressed to the Paris anatomist Jean Riolan, who had accepted a limited circulation of the blood while continuing to deny that it actually ran through the whole body. Twenty years after Harvey's original De Motu Cordis had first proposed continuous circulation, these letters show him still defending the claim against serious professional resistance, restating and considerably extending his original experimental evidence in direct response to Riolan's specific objections.

Harvey treats Riolan's partial concession, accepting some circulation while denying its full extent, as a more serious obstacle to genuine understanding than outright rejection would be, since a half-acceptance can let a reader believe the underlying question has already been settled without actually following the physical evidence to its complete conclusion.

## What happens

**Restating the original case.** Harvey opens by restating the core experimental evidence from his 1628 De Motu Cordis, the original work proposing that blood circulates continuously through the body rather than being consumed and regenerated as earlier physicians had believed, establishing the foundation his new letters will extend and defend.

**Answering Riolan's objections.** Harvey addresses Riolan's specific position directly, noting that Riolan has accepted a limited circulation while still denying that it runs through the entire body, and argues point by point that this partial acceptance represents a genuine intellectual error rather than a reasonable, cautious middle position between full acceptance and outright rejection.

**New experiments on the pulse and veins.** Harvey adds fresh experimental evidence specifically responsive to Riolan's stated objections, new observations on the pulse's behavior, on how blood actually moves through the venous system, and on what happens physically when an artery is deliberately severed, extending his original demonstrations with additional repeatable physical evidence.

**Defending observation against authority.** The second letter turns explicitly methodological, defending direct observation and experiment as the proper foundation for anatomical knowledge, arguing against anatomists, Riolan among them, who continue to weigh ancient textual authority, particularly Galen's writings, more heavily than the evidence available through their own dissections.

**Answering two decades of criticism.** Beyond Riolan specifically, Harvey's letters respond more broadly to the accumulated criticism his circulation theory had faced across the twenty years since its original publication, treating this sustained defense as an opportunity to consolidate and strengthen his original argument with additional evidence and clearer methodological justification.

## How to read it

Read Harvey's treatment of Riolan's partial acceptance as more dangerous than outright rejection as a genuinely important methodological point extending well beyond this specific anatomical dispute; a half-concession that lets readers believe a question is settled without following the full evidence represents a distinct kind of intellectual danger worth recognizing in other contexts.

Pay close attention to how directly Harvey grounds his response in repeatable physical demonstration, new experiments on the pulse, veins and cut arteries, rather than in further textual or rhetorical argument alone, a methodological commitment the second letter makes explicit as its own genuine subject.

Notice how the second letter's defense of observation and experiment against ancient authority functions as a manifesto for the experimental method generally, not merely a specific defense of blood circulation, aimed at anatomists still more persuaded by Galen's centuries-old texts than by the evidence available through their own direct dissection.

Consider these letters alongside Harvey's original De Motu Cordis to see how a significant scientific claim actually gets defended and refined over decades of professional resistance, extended through new evidence and clarified methodological argument rather than simply restated unchanged.

Read this work alongside Descartes's Discourse on Method and Galileo's Two New Sciences to compare how differently three roughly contemporary figures defended the priority of direct observation and experiment over inherited textual authority across medicine, philosophy and physics.
