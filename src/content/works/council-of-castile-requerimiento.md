## Overview

About three pages, a legal proclamation drafted in 1513 by the Spanish jurist Juan López de Palacios Rubios, working under commission from the Council of Castile, and intended to be read aloud, in Spanish, to indigenous peoples in the Americas before Spanish forces attacked or subjugated them. The document, known as the Requerimiento, meaning the requirement or the demand, emerged from genuine legal and theological anxiety within the Spanish crown and church about whether conquest of the Americas could be considered just without first offering its inhabitants some formal opportunity to submit peacefully, an anxiety Palacios Rubios's text was designed to resolve on paper regardless of how it functioned, or failed to function, in actual practice.

The document's short length belies its considerable historical significance, both as a rare surviving instance of a colonial power's attempt to construct explicit legal justification for conquest in advance, and as one of history's starkest illustrations of formal procedure satisfied while substantively defeating its own stated purpose, since the Requerimiento was, by numerous documented historical accounts, frequently read in Spanish to people who spoke no Spanish, from the decks of ships anchored offshore, or to villages emptied by the population's flight, or before dawn raids in which the reading itself functioned as nothing more than a legal formality preceding violence already decided upon.

## The argument

**The chain of authority.** The document opens by reciting a theological and legal genealogy intended to establish Spanish sovereignty's legitimacy: the creation of the world, the descent of all humanity from a single common ancestry, the establishment of the papacy as the supreme spiritual authority over all peoples regardless of whether they had ever encountered Christianity, and finally the pope's donation of the Indies, the islands and mainland of the Americas, to the Spanish crown, an act of papal authority the document treats as automatically and self-evidently binding on peoples who had, of course, never heard of the papacy, Christianity, or Spain before the moment of the document's reading.

**The demand.** Having established this chain of claimed authority, the document formally requires its hearers to acknowledge the Church as ruler of the world, to acknowledge the Spanish crown as their rightful sovereign by virtue of the papal donation just described, and to permit Christian religious instruction among their people, presenting compliance as a genuine choice that, if made promptly, will result in the hearers being received in love and friendship by the Spanish crown, their existing customs and property left largely undisturbed.

**The threat.** The document's closing section spells out, in considerable and deliberate detail, the consequences of refusal or delay: war will be waged with fire and sword, the hearers and their families will be subjected to conquest and enslavement, their property will be seized, and, in the document's most striking single formal move, it explicitly declares that any resulting deaths, suffering and loss will be the fault of the hearers themselves for having refused a reasonable demand, rather than the fault of the Spanish forces carrying out the violence that the document itself is simultaneously threatening.

## How to read it

Read the Requerimiento together with an understanding of its actual documented historical use, since the gap between the document's formal claim to offer genuine choice and its frequent practical deployment, read in an incomprehensible language to absent or fleeing populations, is where the document's real historical significance lies, considerably more than in its formal legal or theological content taken alone.

Notice how carefully the document constructs a chain of authority, creation, papacy, papal donation, that requires the hearer's prior acceptance of an entire theological and political framework, Christianity, the papacy's supreme authority, the pope's right to donate entire continents, before its central demand can even be coherently understood, let alone freely accepted.

Pay particular attention to the document's closing assignment of moral responsibility for future violence onto its own intended victims, a rhetorical move that allowed Spanish conquest to proceed while maintaining, at least on paper, a formal claim to having offered a just alternative that was refused rather than one that was never genuinely available.

Read this document as arising from a real and, by the legal and theological standards of early sixteenth-century Spain, not entirely cynical anxiety about the justice of conquest; the very fact that Spanish jurists felt the need to produce a document like this at all reveals a genuine contemporary unease about unjustified conquest, even as the document's actual use largely defeated the purpose that unease had originally prompted.

Read the Requerimiento alongside later Spanish debates about the treatment of indigenous peoples in the Americas, particularly the arguments advanced by critics of Spanish colonial practice, to see how quickly and how thoroughly the document's claimed procedural justice was challenged even within Spain's own legal and theological establishment.

## Questions it raises

- What does it mean for a legal or moral requirement to be satisfied procedurally, the document was read aloud, while being completely defeated substantively, the hearers could not understand it or were not even present? Does formal compliance under these conditions carry any genuine moral or legal weight?
- The document explicitly assigns responsibility for future violence to its own hearers rather than to the forces threatening that violence. What does this rhetorical move reveal about how systems of power construct formal justifications that shift moral responsibility onto those with the least actual power in the situation?
- Given that Spanish jurists and theologians felt genuine anxiety about the justice of conquest, anxiety serious enough to produce this document, why did that anxiety fail to produce any more substantive check on how conquest was actually carried out?
- How should a document this short and this narrowly legal be read as a historical source? What does its very existence reveal about the concerns of the colonial power that produced it, independent of how the document actually functioned in practice?
- Does the Requerimiento's theological chain of authority, creation, papacy, papal donation, represent genuine reasoning its authors believed in, or was it always understood, even by its drafters, as a formal pretext constructed to satisfy legal requirements rather than to persuade anyone of anything?
- What responsibility do modern readers have in encountering a document like this, understanding it as a historical artifact of genuine contemporary belief and anxiety, versus understanding it primarily through the lens of how catastrophically it failed the people it was nominally addressed to?
