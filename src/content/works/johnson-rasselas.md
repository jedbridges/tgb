## Overview

About a hundred and twenty pages, Samuel Johnson's tale of Rasselas, prince of Abyssinia, written in a single week in 1759 specifically to raise money for his mother's funeral, a genuine urgency that gives the tale's central philosophical question unusual weight despite its brief composition period. Rasselas grows restless in the Happy Valley, where the royal children are kept in engineered luxury, and escapes with his sister Nekayah, her maid Pekuah and the poet Imlac to make the choice of life.

In Cairo and beyond, the travelers examine the lives of the rich, the pastoral, the hermit, the philosopher and the sage astronomer who believes he controls the weather, and find that none is happy. Pekuah is kidnapped and ransomed. In a conclusion in which nothing is concluded, the travelers decide to return to Abyssinia, the tale refusing to resolve its systematic survey of ways of living into any single satisfactory recommendation.

## What happens

**Restlessness in the Happy Valley.** Rasselas grows genuinely restless within the Happy Valley, an enclosed paradise where Abyssinia's royal children are kept in engineered luxury specifically designed to prevent restlessness, discovering that this manufactured contentment produces not happiness but a persistent, gnawing dissatisfaction he cannot suppress.

**Escape and the choice of life.** Rasselas escapes the Happy Valley together with his sister Nekayah, her maid Pekuah, and the poet Imlac, setting out specifically to make what the tale calls the choice of life, a systematic investigation into how a person should actually choose to live.

**Examining the rich and the pastoral life.** In Cairo and the surrounding region, the travelers examine several candidate ways of living in turn, beginning with the lives of the wealthy and the simple pastoral existence, finding in each case genuine dissatisfaction beneath surface appearance of contentment.

**The hermit and the philosopher.** The travelers continue their survey by examining the hermit's withdrawn solitary life and the philosopher's reasoned, detached approach to living, finding these approaches similarly wanting despite their surface appeal to reason and spiritual discipline.

**The astronomer who controls the weather.** The travelers encounter a sage astronomer who has convinced himself, through intense solitary study, that he personally controls the weather, a striking episode examining how learned isolation from ordinary social correction can produce genuine delusion even in an accomplished, rational mind.

**Pekuah's kidnapping and ransom.** Amid the travelers' broader philosophical survey, Pekuah is kidnapped and subsequently ransomed, an episode of genuine danger and practical crisis that interrupts the tale's more abstract investigation with concrete narrative incident.

**The conclusion in which nothing is concluded.** The tale closes with the travelers deciding simply to return to Abyssinia, having examined numerous ways of living without settling on any single satisfactory answer, a deliberately unresolved ending that refuses the positive philosophical recommendation readers might otherwise expect.

## How to read it

Read the tale's famous unresolved conclusion as its genuine philosophical achievement rather than a narrative failure to reach proper resolution; Johnson's refusal to recommend any single way of living represents an honest report that the underlying question may not have a discoverable settled answer, delivered with real narrative charm rather than despair.

Pay close attention to how systematically the travelers' survey proceeds, examining the rich, the pastoral, the hermit, the philosopher and the astronomer in sequence, treating this structure as something like an empirical investigation testing each candidate way of living directly rather than settling the question through abstract argument alone.

Notice how the astronomer's specific delusion, believing he personally controls the weather, functions as more than mere comic episode; this case study examines how intense private intellectual pursuit, divorced from ordinary social correction, can produce genuine derangement even in an otherwise learned and rational mind.

Consider the genuine urgency behind the tale's composition, written in a single week to fund Johnson's mother's funeral, as relevant to how you read its treatment of happiness and loss; this was not abstract philosophical speculation composed at leisure but writing produced under real, pressing personal circumstance.

Read this tale alongside Voltaire's Candide, composed the same year and addressing remarkably similar questions about happiness, suffering and philosophical optimism, to compare how two major eighteenth-century writers, working independently within roughly the same period, arrived at related skeptical conclusions about whether any single philosophical system can fully account for human experience.
