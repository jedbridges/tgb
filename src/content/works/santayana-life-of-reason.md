## Overview

About eight hundred pages, George Santayana's five-volume account, published between 1905 and 1906, of how human impulses, once shaped by reflection, become a life worth living. Reason in Common Sense lays out the naturalist basis; the four following volumes trace reason in society, from love and the family to patriotism.

The remaining volumes address reason in religion, which he treats as poetry mistaken for science; in art; and in science, which he defends as the only knowledge we have. The sentence that those who cannot remember the past are condemned to repeat it comes from the first volume.

## What happens

**Reason in Common Sense.** The first volume, Reason in Common Sense, lays out Santayana's naturalist basis, establishing reason as developing from impulse through reflection rather than existing as a separate, fixed faculty.

**The famous sentence on memory.** This first volume contains Santayana's famous sentence, that those who cannot remember the past are condemned to repeat it, distilling the entire project's historical understanding of reason into one memorable line.

**Reason in society.** The second volume traces reason in society, examining love and the family through to patriotism, tracing how reflective reason gradually civilizes and organizes humanity's basic social impulses and attachments.

**Reason in religion.** The third volume addresses reason in religion, which Santayana treats as poetry mistaken for science, preserving religion's genuine imaginative and moral value while denying its literal cognitive claims.

**Reason in art.** The fourth volume examines reason in art, extending Santayana's naturalist framework into aesthetic experience and creative production as further expressions of reflectively organized human impulse.

**Reason in science.** The fifth and final volume addresses reason in science, which Santayana defends as the only knowledge we actually have, a conclusion reached only after seriously engaging society, religion and art's own distinct contributions.

## How to read it

Read Santayana's famous sentence, that those who cannot remember the past are condemned to repeat it, as distilling the entire five-volume project's naturalist foundation, insisting reason itself is fundamentally historical, developing through accumulated memory rather than fixed abstraction.

Pay close attention to how treating religion as poetry mistaken for science offers a genuinely distinctive middle position between crude debunking dismissal and uncritical theological acceptance, preserving religion's imaginative value while denying its literal cognitive claims.

Notice how defending science as the only knowledge we have, after volumes exploring society, religion and art's own genuine contributions, gives this epistemic claim considerable weight, arrived at only after serious prior engagement with alternative sources of understanding.

Consider how tracing reason's progressive development across society, religion, art and science within a single systematic architecture demonstrates naturalism's genuine explanatory ambition, a comprehensive account of how impulse becomes civilized, reflective life.

Read this work alongside Santayana's own later Skepticism and Animal Faith to see how the confident naturalist synthesis built here across five systematic volumes gives way to that later book's more explicitly epistemological reckoning with knowledge's genuine foundations and limits.

## Questions it raises

- Does Santayana's famous claim about memory and repetition successfully distill his broader naturalist project, or does this memorable line risk oversimplifying a considerably more nuanced argument about reason's historical, developmental character across the full five volumes?
- How persuasive is treating religion as poetry mistaken for science? Does this framework successfully preserve religion's genuine value while avoiding both crude dismissal and uncritical acceptance, or does it satisfy neither committed believers nor committed skeptics?
- What does defending science as the only knowledge we have, after extensive engagement with society, religion and art, accomplish that asserting this claim from the outset could not? Does this earned conclusion carry more weight than a purely programmatic epistemological commitment?
- How does tracing reason's development from society through religion, art and finally science reflect Santayana's own view of civilization's proper historical and conceptual order? Does this progression suggest a genuine developmental hierarchy among these domains?
- Given that this ambitious five-volume project addresses nearly every major domain of human value and activity, how should a contemporary reader approach a work of this considerable scope? Does comprehensive systematic treatment still offer genuine philosophical advantages over more focused, specialized inquiry?
- What does this work's continued influence on American philosophical naturalism suggest about the durability of Santayana's specific synthesis? Do his particular treatments of religion, art and science still offer useful resources for contemporary naturalist philosophy?
