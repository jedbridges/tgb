## Overview

About fifteen pages, a poem of three hundred and sixty-eight lines in heroic couplets by Samuel Johnson, published in 1749, freely imitating rather than translating Juvenal's tenth satire. Johnson surveys mankind's actual desires across a sweeping geographic range from China to Peru, wealth, political power, learning, military glory, long life and beauty, updating Juvenal's ancient Roman examples into figures his own eighteenth-century English readers would recognize directly.

Cardinal Wolsey, the scholar, Charles XII of Sweden, and the aged and the beautiful serve as the poem's central examples, each demonstrating how a specific object of desire ultimately betrays those who actually succeed in obtaining it. The poem closes by asking whether no wish should then be made at all, and answers that we should pray instead for a healthy mind, patience and faith, leaving the ultimate choice to heaven.

## What happens

**The geographic and thematic survey.** The poem opens by surveying mankind's actual desires across a sweeping geographic range from China to Peru, cataloguing wealth, political power, learning, military glory, long life and beauty as the specific objects of ambition the poem will subsequently examine and ultimately undermine.

**Cardinal Wolsey's rise and fall.** Johnson presents Cardinal Wolsey as the poem's central English example of political ambition, tracing his rise to the height of power before his catastrophic fall, demonstrating how even fully achieved political success carries the mechanism of its own eventual betrayal.

**The scholar's pursuit of learning.** The poem examines the scholar's pursuit of learning as another specific object of human desire, finding that intellectual achievement, like political power, ultimately fails to deliver the lasting satisfaction its pursuit seemed to promise.

**Charles XII's military glory.** Charles XII of Sweden serves as the poem's central example of military ambition, his campaigns achieving considerable glory before collapsing into defeat and an obscure death, demonstrating that even successfully achieved military ambition offers no genuine protection against fate's ultimate reversal.

**The aged and the beautiful.** The poem turns to the aged and the beautiful, examining how these qualities, long life and physical beauty, erode through time's ordinary operation rather than sudden catastrophe, extending its argument about desire's betrayal into the universal process of aging itself.

**The closing turn to faith.** The poem's final movement asks directly whether no wish should then be made at all, given how thoroughly the preceding survey has undermined every specific object of human desire, and answers that a healthy mind, patience and faith remain worth praying for, leaving the ultimate choice to heaven.

## How to read it

Read the poem's relationship to Juvenal's original tenth satire as genuine creative imitation rather than simple translation; Johnson updates Juvenal's ancient Roman examples into figures his own contemporary readers would recognize directly, a technique that lets the poem's ancient satirical framework speak with immediate eighteenth-century relevance.

Pay close attention to how consistently the poem's examples, Wolsey, the scholar, Charles XII, the aged and beautiful, demonstrate not simple bad luck but a more unsettling pattern, that the very success of obtaining a desired object contains within it the specific mechanism of that object's eventual betrayal.

Notice how the poem's tightly controlled heroic couplets give even its bleakest observations a formal composure and balance; this technical control matters directly to how the poem manages to survey such comprehensive human disappointment without collapsing into simple despair.

Consider the poem's final turn toward a healthy mind, patience and faith as its genuine resolution rather than a merely conventional pious addition; these qualities specifically cannot betray their possessor the way external objects of desire inevitably do, making the poem's closing prayer a considered answer rather than empty consolation.

Read this poem alongside Johnson's own Rasselas, composed a decade later, to see how the same underlying skepticism about worldly satisfaction, expressed here in tightly controlled satirical verse, finds a considerably different narrative and prose treatment in the later prose tale's systematic survey of ways of living.
