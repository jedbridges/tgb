## Overview

About a hundred and twenty pages, Richard Brinsley Sheridan's comedy of manners, first performed at Drury Lane in 1777. Set among the gossips of London society, Lady Sneerwell's circle manufactures scandal for sport. The brothers Surface are its subjects: Joseph, a hypocrite of fine sentiments, and Charles, an open-handed spendthrift who is the better man.

Their rich uncle Sir Oliver returns from India in disguise to test them. In the screen scene, Joseph is found hiding Lady Teazle, the young wife of old Sir Peter, and his reputation collapses. Charles gets the girl and the fortune.

## What happens

**Lady Sneerwell's circle.** Lady Sneerwell's circle manufactures scandal purely for sport, establishing the gossip-driven social world within which the Surface brothers' contrasting reputations will be tested and ultimately revealed.

**Joseph and Charles Surface.** The brothers Surface become the circle's central subjects, Joseph cultivating a reputation as a man of fine sentiments while Charles remains an open-handed, genuinely generous spendthrift.

**Sir Oliver's disguised return.** Their rich uncle Sir Oliver returns from India in disguise specifically to test his nephews, hiding his own identity and judgment so both brothers will reveal their genuine characters unknowingly.

**Testing Charles.** Sir Oliver, disguised, tests Charles directly, discovering genuine warmth and honest generosity beneath his nephew's reckless spending, qualities the play will ultimately value over calculated propriety.

**The screen scene.** In the play's central scene, Joseph is found hiding Lady Teazle, the young wife of old Sir Peter, behind a screen, a sudden physical revelation that instantly collapses his carefully cultivated reputation.

**Charles rewarded.** Following Joseph's exposed hypocrisy, Charles gets both the girl and the fortune, the play's resolution rewarding his honest, if reckless, character over his brother's calculated and ultimately hollow propriety.

## How to read it

Read Sir Oliver returning from India in disguise to test his nephews as staging character assessment through deliberate deception, the brothers revealing their genuine natures without knowing they are actually being evaluated for the inheritance at stake.

Pay close attention to how Joseph's carefully cultivated reputation as a man of fine sentiments collapsing the moment Lady Teazle is discovered hiding behind his screen exposes hypocrisy's characteristic vulnerability to sudden physical revelation.

Notice how Charles's open-handed spendthrift generosity proving genuinely more admirable than Joseph's calculated propriety inverts conventional moral hierarchy, reckless honesty valued over careful hypocrisy throughout the play's resolution.

Consider how Lady Sneerwell's circle manufacturing scandal purely for sport satirizes gossip as a genuine social institution with its own considerable destructive power, malicious entertainment inflicting real reputational damage regardless of casual attitude.

Read this comedy alongside Molière's own Tartuffe to compare two studies of hypocrisy exposed within a household, one through an uncle's disguised testing and a literal screen, the other through a father's own eventual, hard-won recognition of a false devotee's true character.

## Questions it raises

- Does Sir Oliver's disguised testing of his nephews represent a fair method for assessing genuine character, or does this elaborate deception itself raise questions about whether such manufactured circumstances can reliably reveal how people actually behave under ordinary conditions?
- How persuasive is the play's ultimate judgment favoring Charles's reckless generosity over Joseph's calculated propriety? Does genuine virtue necessarily require the kind of financial imprudence Charles displays, or does the play oversimplify this moral contrast for comic effect?
- What does the screen scene's sudden physical revelation accomplish that gradual exposure through accumulated evidence could not? Does this theatrical device successfully capture something true about hypocrisy's actual vulnerability to unexpected circumstance?
- How should a reader weigh Lady Sneerwell's circle's gossip against its genuine social function within the play's world? Does the satire suggest gossip serves any legitimate purpose, or does the play present it as purely destructive entertainment lacking any redeeming value?
- Given that Charles ultimately receives both romantic and financial reward despite his spendthrift habits, what does this resolution suggest about the play's actual relationship between virtue and practical prudence? Does honest recklessness genuinely deserve this comic vindication?
- What does this play's continued status as a classic comedy of manners suggest about the enduring appeal of gossip, hypocrisy and eventual exposure as comic material? Does eighteenth-century London society's specific concerns translate to contemporary understanding of similar social dynamics?
