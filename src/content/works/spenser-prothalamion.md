## Overview

About ten pages, Edmund Spenser's betrothal poem of ten stanzas, written for the double engagement of the Earl of Worcester's daughters in 1596. The poet walks by the Thames and sees two swans, white as the brides, gliding down to Essex House while nymphs strew flowers, and follows them to the wedding.

Between the pageant Spenser laments his own disappointed hopes at court and praises the Earl of Essex. The refrain asking the Thames to run softly was borrowed by T. S. Eliot in The Waste Land.

## What happens

**Walking by the Thames.** The poet walks along the Thames, establishing the riverine setting through which the poem's central allegorical procession toward the betrothal celebration will unfold.

**Sighting the two swans.** The poet sees two swans, white as the brides, gliding down the river toward Essex House, an allegorical vision representing the Earl of Worcester's two betrothed daughters.

**Nymphs strewing flowers.** Nymphs strew flowers as the swans glide past, extending the poem's mythological pageant into an elaborate natural celebration accompanying the swans' symbolic procession.

**Following to the wedding.** The poet follows the swans to the wedding itself, tracing the allegorical procession's movement from the open river toward the actual celebratory destination at Essex House.

**Lamenting disappointed hopes.** Between the pageant's celebratory movements, Spenser laments his own disappointed hopes at court, personal grievance surfacing within a poem ostensibly focused entirely on others' happier circumstance.

**Praising the Earl of Essex.** Spenser praises the Earl of Essex within this same poem, deploying his poetic craft toward maintaining his own precarious court position alongside the daughters' celebration.

## How to read it

Read Spenser composing this poem for a double engagement of patrons' daughters, rather than for his own marriage as in the Epithalamion, as showing his considerable skill operating in service of external occasion and courtly obligation.

Pay close attention to how lamenting his own disappointed hopes at court within a poem ostensibly celebrating others' betrothal reveals genuine personal grievance surfacing through occasional verse's conventional public form.

Notice how praising the Earl of Essex within this same poem stages patronage's practical operation directly, poetic craft deployed toward maintaining Spenser's own court position even while ostensibly focused on the betrothed daughters.

Consider how T. S. Eliot's borrowing of the refrain asking the Thames to run softly in The Waste Land traces a genuinely direct line of literary influence across more than three centuries into modernism's radically transformed poetic context.

This shorter betrothal poem shows Spenser working the same formal territory as the Epithalamion at smaller scale and for someone else's occasion; setting them side by side reveals how differently personal stake shapes an otherwise similar celebratory structure.

## Questions it raises

- Does composing this poem for others' betrothal rather than his own marriage produce a genuinely different emotional register than the Epithalamion, or does Spenser's formal skill successfully generate comparable celebratory power regardless of his personal distance from the actual occasion?
- How does the swan allegory, representing the two betrothed daughters as white birds gliding down the Thames, function as poetic device? Does this natural imagery successfully elevate the human betrothal, or does it risk obscuring the actual people being celebrated?
- What does Spenser's lament about disappointed court hopes accomplish within a poem ostensibly dedicated entirely to celebrating someone else's happiness? Does this personal interpolation complicate or simply coexist alongside the poem's primary celebratory purpose?
- How should a reader weigh the poem's explicit patronage function, praising the Earl of Essex, against its more purely aesthetic and celebratory achievements? Does acknowledging this practical motivation change how the poem's beauty should be evaluated?
- Given T. S. Eliot's direct borrowing of the poem's refrain in The Waste Land, what does this specific literary transmission suggest about how particular poetic phrases can carry meaning across radically different historical and aesthetic contexts?
- What does this poem's relatively modest ten-stanza scale, compared to the Epithalamion's more extensive structure, suggest about how occasion and purpose shaped Spenser's own formal choices across his various occasional celebratory poems?
