## Overview

About a hundred pages, the text of a presidential address B. R. Ambedkar was invited to deliver at the annual conference of the Jat-Pat Todak Mandal, a Hindu reform society in Lahore, in 1936. He submitted the text in advance, as was customary. The organisers read it, objected strenuously to its most radical conclusions, asked him to remove the passages calling for the rejection of the shastras' authority, and when he refused, cancelled the conference rather than let him speak. Ambedkar printed the undelivered speech himself, at his own expense, and it became one of the most consequential texts of twentieth-century Indian political thought.

Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar was born into a Dalit, then called untouchable, family, and experienced caste discrimination directly and painfully throughout his education, despite eventually earning doctorates from Columbia and the London School of Economics and becoming the principal architect of the Indian Constitution after independence. The speech draws on that lived experience but is argued as political philosophy and social theory rather than as memoir, addressed to an audience of Hindu reformers who wanted caste improved rather than abolished, which is precisely the distinction Ambedkar's argument refuses to accept.

The most widely read modern edition includes Gandhi's published response to the speech and Ambedkar's rejoinder to that response, an exchange that sharpened the disagreement between the two men over whether caste could be reformed from within Hinduism or had to be rejected along with it.

## The argument

**Reform is not enough.** Ambedkar begins by dismissing the reform society's own program as fundamentally inadequate to the problem it claims to address. Efforts at intercaste dining, at opening temples to previously excluded castes, at softening the harshest practical disabilities imposed on those at the bottom of the hierarchy, he treats as gestures that leave the actual mechanism of caste completely intact.

That mechanism, he argues, is endogamy, marriage restricted within the caste. As long as marriage across caste lines remains forbidden or socially catastrophic, caste will continue to reproduce itself generation after generation regardless of how much intercaste dining or temple access is achieved, because caste identity is transmitted through birth and marriage, not through where one eats. Reformers who focus on dining and temple entry while leaving endogamy untouched are, on this argument, treating symptoms while leaving the disease to propagate itself indefinitely.

**Caste is not a division of labour.** Ambedkar directly confronts the most common defence of caste offered by its apologists: that it represents a rational, efficient division of labour, each group performing the occupation suited to its nature, the whole arrangement benefiting social cooperation. He rejects this on two grounds.

First, empirically: caste assigns occupation by birth, not by aptitude, so a person born into a caste associated with one trade is required to pursue that trade regardless of natural talent or inclination, and forbidden from pursuing another regardless of gift for it. This is not efficient allocation of labour to ability; it is the opposite, a systematic mismatch enforced by birth rather than corrected by choice.

Second, conceptually: even granting, for argument's sake, that some division of labour serves society, Ambedkar insists that caste divides not labour but labourers, fixing human beings themselves into a graded hierarchy of worth that has nothing to do with the efficient organisation of work and everything to do with rank, purity and the permanent subordination of some groups to others.

**Graded inequality, not simple oppression.** One of Ambedkar's sharpest analytical moves is his account of what makes caste especially durable and especially destructive compared to a simple two-tier system of oppressor and oppressed. Caste is graded: nearly every caste has some group above it and some group beneath it, which means nearly everyone in the system has both a grievance against those above and a stake in maintaining their own position of superiority over those below. This graded structure, Ambedkar argues, fractures the solidarity that might otherwise unite the oppressed against the system as a whole, because the caste immediately above the lowest has as much interest in preserving the hierarchy, to maintain its position over that lowest caste, as any caste higher up does.

**Fellow feeling stops at the caste line.** Ambedkar extends this into a broader social diagnosis. Caste, he argues, confines genuine fellow feeling, the sympathy and sense of shared belonging that makes a society more than a collection of mutually indifferent or hostile groups, almost entirely within caste boundaries. A Hindu society organised this way is not merely unjust to those at the bottom; it lacks the basic social cohesion a functioning nation requires, since solidarity that stops at caste lines cannot sustain genuinely shared national purpose. This is the argument behind his striking claim that caste makes Hindu society not merely unjust but antisocial and, in the context of a nation seeking independence and self-government, anti-national.

**Why the sanction must be rejected, not just the practice.** The speech's most radical and most controversial section addresses where caste's authority actually comes from. Ambedkar argues that caste persists not merely as custom or habit, which might be gradually reformed through education and changed practice, but because it carries the sanction of religious scripture, specifically the shastras, which most Hindus regard as divinely authoritative.

If the shastras themselves command or sanctify caste distinctions, then asking Hindus to abandon caste while continuing to accept the shastras as authoritative is, Ambedkar argues, asking for an incoherent position: obedience to an authority whose content one simultaneously refuses to follow on this specific point. Genuine reform, on this account, cannot stop at criticising caste practice; it must go further and deny the authority of the texts that sanctify the practice, following the historical example of Buddha and of Guru Nanak, both of whom Ambedkar credits with rejecting Brahminical scriptural authority rather than merely reforming particular customs within it.

This is the section the Jat-Pat Todak Mandal found unacceptable, and it is the section that most clearly anticipates Ambedkar's own eventual trajectory: two decades later, in 1956, shortly before his death, he led a mass public conversion of hundreds of thousands of followers from Hinduism to Buddhism, an act that put into practice the logic this speech had already worked out.

## How to read it

Read the speech as a whole rather than mining it for the famous line about labour and labourers, memorable as that line is. The argument builds cumulatively, from the inadequacy of piecemeal reform, through the analysis of graded inequality, to the conclusion about scriptural authority, and each step depends on the ones before it.

Pay particular attention to the distinction between reforming a practice and rejecting the authority that sanctions it. This distinction is the speech's central intellectual contribution and the reason it remains disturbing to readers who would prefer caste addressed through incremental social change rather than through a direct confrontation with religious authority.

Read Gandhi's response and Ambedkar's rejoinder if your edition includes them. Gandhi, while opposing untouchability, defended a reformed version of caste organised around varna, occupational categories he distinguished from the practice of untouchability, and his exchange with Ambedkar over whether that distinction can be sustained is one of the sharpest disagreements in modern Indian political thought.

Notice how much of the argument depends on Ambedkar's analysis of endogamy as the actual reproductive mechanism of caste. This is a specific, falsifiable social-scientific claim as much as a moral one, and it is worth testing against what you know of how caste, or comparable systems of hereditary hierarchy elsewhere, actually persist or dissolve over time.

Read it alongside other texts of comprehensive social critique that locate the sanction for an unjust institution in a sacred or foundational text, and that therefore conclude the institution cannot be reformed without confronting the text itself. The structure of Ambedkar's argument, that piecemeal reform leaves the root cause untouched and only rejecting the foundational sanction will work, recurs across very different traditions and contexts.

## Questions it raises

- Is Ambedkar right that reforms leaving endogamy untouched cannot meaningfully weaken caste, or can cultural change occur gradually through other channels first?
- Does his distinction between division of labour and division of labourers hold up, or could a defender of caste construct a version that survives the objection?
- Is his argument that caste is uniquely durable because it is graded, rather than a simple two-tier hierarchy, borne out by comparison with other systems of hereditary inequality?
- If a reform effort accepts the authority of a tradition's foundational texts, is Ambedkar right that it cannot coherently ask the tradition's adherents to abandon a practice those texts sanction?
- What is gained and lost when the response to an unjust institution is to reject the authority that sanctions it altogether, rather than to reinterpret that authority?
- Ambedkar eventually left Hinduism for Buddhism rather than continuing to argue from within. Does his own trajectory vindicate the logic of this speech, or does it show the limits of trying to reform a tradition from inside?
