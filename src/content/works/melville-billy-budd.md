## Overview

About a hundred pages, a novella by Herman Melville, written between 1886 and 1891 and left unfinished at his death, published only posthumously in 1924. In 1797, the year of the great mutinies in the British navy, the handsome and innocent young sailor Billy Budd is pressed from a merchant ship onto the warship Bellipotent.

The master-at-arms John Claggart conceives an unexplained hatred for him and accuses him of plotting mutiny before Captain Vere. Billy, who stammers when agitated, cannot answer and strikes Claggart dead with a single blow. Vere, convinced of Billy's moral innocence but bound by the Articles of War, convenes a drumhead court that condemns him, and Billy is hanged at dawn blessing the captain.

## What happens

**Billy's impressment.** Billy Budd, a handsome and innocent young sailor, is pressed from a merchant ship onto the warship Bellipotent, an involuntary conscription into naval service that places him within the ship's rigid military hierarchy and law.

**Claggart's unexplained hatred.** The master-at-arms John Claggart conceives a genuine, deliberately unexplained hatred for Billy, a mystery the narrative never resolves into any specific comprehensible grievance or personal motive, reading instead as something closer to evil's own inexplicable reaction to innocence.

**The false accusation.** Claggart falsely accuses Billy of plotting mutiny before Captain Vere, bringing the ship's rigid military law directly to bear on an accusation Billy has no actual grounds to answer, since the charge itself is entirely fabricated.

**Billy's fatal blow.** Billy, who stammers when agitated and finds himself unable to answer the false accusation verbally, strikes Claggart dead with a single blow, his own physical speech limitation directly producing the fatal violent act that will condemn him.

**Vere's drumhead court.** Captain Vere, though personally convinced of Billy's genuine moral innocence, insists that the Articles of War require conviction and execution regardless of this personal conviction, and convenes a drumhead court that formally condemns Billy to death.

**Billy's execution.** Billy is hanged at dawn, blessing the captain in his final moments, a conclusion that refuses to let legal process and genuine moral truth simply align, staging one of literature's starkest confrontations between natural justice and positive law.

## How to read it

Read Claggart's unexplained hatred as deliberately unresolved mystery rather than a gap Melville simply failed to fill; this refusal to provide comprehensible motive treats evil's attraction to innocence it cannot tolerate as itself the point, resisting easy psychological explanation.

Pay close attention to how directly Billy's stammer causes his own destruction; a man capable of verbal self-defense might have survived the false accusation, making his specific physical limitation, rather than any genuine guilt, the actual mechanism producing his fatal violent response.

Notice how Vere's simultaneous conviction of Billy's innocence and insistence on legal condemnation refuses easy resolution; the novella does not suggest legal process was simply wrong, but stages a genuine tragic conflict between what justice requires and what the law, once invoked, actually demands.

Consider the novella's unfinished state and posthumous publication as relevant to how you weigh its genuine ambiguities; Melville never provided whatever final authorial resolution he might eventually have imposed, leaving readers to confront these tensions without a settled final verdict.

Read this novella alongside Melville's own earlier Moby-Dick to see how his treatment of obsession, moral ambiguity and confrontation with forces beyond simple comprehension develops from that considerably more expansive earlier novel into this later, more compressed and formally different final work.
