## Overview

About a hundred and eighty pages, published by F. Scott Fitzgerald in 1925, narrated by Nick Carraway, a young Midwesterner who has moved to New York in the summer of 1922 to work in the bond business and rents a modest cottage on Long Island directly beside the enormous mansion of Jay Gatsby, a mysterious, immensely wealthy neighbor known for throwing lavish, extravagant parties that Gatsby himself, Nick gradually notices, rarely seems to fully enjoy or even properly attend. The novel unfolds during the Jazz Age's peak years of Prohibition-era excess, and Fitzgerald renders this specific historical moment, its music, its fashion, its casual, often reckless wealth, with considerable period precision even as the novel's actual concerns extend well beyond simple period portraiture.

Nick gradually learns that Gatsby's entire elaborate project, the fortune of uncertain and likely illegal origin, the enormous mansion, the legendary parties drawing hundreds of guests Gatsby barely knows, has been constructed for a single specific purpose: to win back Daisy Buchanan, Nick's own cousin, whom Gatsby had loved and lost five years earlier before he left for war, and who has since married Tom Buchanan, a brutal, unfaithful man of genuinely old, inherited wealth that Gatsby's own newer fortune, however large, can never fully match in social standing.

## What happens

**Gatsby's mysterious parties.** The novel's early chapters establish Gatsby's legendary status through his lavish, constantly rumored-about parties, drawing crowds of guests who speculate wildly and often inaccurately about their host's actual background and the true source of his evident wealth, while Gatsby himself remains a genuinely enigmatic figure Nick only gradually comes to know directly.

**Nick learns the truth.** Through his own connection to Daisy and through Gatsby's neighbor Jordan Baker, Nick learns the actual history behind Gatsby's elaborate project: Gatsby, born James Gatz to a poor Midwestern family, had reinvented himself entirely, changing his name and manufacturing a wealthy background, initially to win Daisy's love before the war separated them, and has since built his considerable fortune, through means the novel leaves deliberately ambiguous but clearly implies were illegal, specifically to become the kind of man he believes could finally reclaim her.

**Reunion and affair.** Nick arranges a reunion between Gatsby and Daisy, and the two begin an affair, with Gatsby genuinely believing, despite Daisy's marriage to Tom and the five years that have passed, that he can simply resume the relationship exactly where it had been interrupted, treating the intervening years and Daisy's marriage as obstacles to be overcome rather than as genuine changes to the situation he is trying to recover.

**Confrontation.** Tensions come to a direct confrontation in a New York hotel room, where Gatsby insists directly, to Daisy and to Tom both, that Daisy has never actually loved Tom and has always loved only Gatsby himself, a claim Daisy cannot ultimately bring herself to fully confirm even as her own marriage's genuine unhappiness becomes increasingly clear, leaving the confrontation unresolved and the underlying tensions considerably heightened rather than settled.

**Tragedy.** Driving back from the city in Gatsby's car, Daisy strikes and kills Myrtle Wilson, Tom's own mistress, in a hit-and-run accident, and Gatsby, in a final act of devotion, takes responsibility for the death himself rather than let Daisy face the consequences, a sacrifice that proves fatal when Myrtle's husband George, misled by Tom into believing Gatsby was both Myrtle's lover and her killer, shoots and kills Gatsby before taking his own life.

**Aftermath and retreat.** Following Gatsby's death, Nick discovers that almost none of the hundreds of people who had attended Gatsby's parties bother to attend his funeral, while Tom and Daisy, whose combined carelessness has produced the entire chain of tragedy, simply retreat back into their money and into each other, leaving Nick, and the reader, to absorb the full weight of consequences neither of them will actually face.

## How to read it

Read the green light at the end of Daisy's dock, introduced early and returned to explicitly only in the novel's closing pages, as the novel's central organizing image, a longing whose object recedes the closer it seems to come, and track how this image accumulates meaning across the narrative before Nick's closing meditation makes its fullest significance explicit.

Pay close attention to Gatsby's direct insistence that he can simply repeat the past, a claim Nick and the novel treat with genuine, complicated sympathy rather than simple mockery, since Gatsby's confidence in recovering an idealized past moment, rather than building any genuine new future, is presented as both his defining tragic flaw and, in its own strange way, a form of genuine romantic conviction.

Notice how carefully the novel distinguishes Gatsby's newer, likely illegally acquired wealth from Tom and Daisy's older, entirely legitimate inherited fortune, and consider how this distinction shapes the novel's actual critique, since it is the Buchanans' careless, socially protected privilege, not Gatsby's more questionable but genuinely aspirational money, that ultimately proves destructive.

Consider Nick's own position as narrator carefully, both genuinely sympathetic to Gatsby and clearly aware of his illusions, a double perspective that shapes how the entire story is filtered and that becomes explicit in Nick's own eventual, considered judgment of both Gatsby and the Buchanans by the novel's close.

Read the closing meditation extending Gatsby's individual longing into a broader claim about the American promise, comparing Gatsby's wonder at the green light to Dutch sailors' original wonder at the American continent, as the novel's deliberate move from personal tragedy toward a larger claim about American aspiration and its inherent impossibility.

## Questions it raises

- Does Gatsby's confidence that he can simply repeat the past represent a genuinely tragic flaw, or does the novel's evident sympathy for his romantic conviction suggest something more admirable, even if ultimately doomed, in his refusal to accept that the past cannot actually be recovered?
- How does the novel's distinction between Gatsby's newer, questionably acquired wealth and the Buchanans' older, legitimately inherited fortune shape its actual critique of American wealth and class? Is the novel's sympathy for Gatsby's aspiration complicated by the illegal means through which he actually acquired his money?
- What does the almost total absence of mourners at Gatsby's funeral, contrasted with the hundreds who attended his parties, suggest about the actual nature of the relationships his wealth and hospitality had purchased throughout the novel?
- How should a reader weigh Tom and Daisy's complete escape from consequence against Gatsby's own death and Myrtle's death, both of which stem directly from the Buchanans' carelessness? Does the novel offer any form of justice, or does it deliberately withhold one?
- What does Nick's own position as both sympathetic participant and clear-eyed judge of the events he narrates contribute to how the novel's central tragedy is ultimately understood? Would a more purely objective or more purely sympathetic narrator change how persuasive the novel's judgments feel?
- How does the closing meditation's extension of Gatsby's individual longing into a broader claim about American aspiration and the impossibility of recovering the past change the novel's scope? Does this closing move earn its broader historical and national claim, or does it overreach beyond what the preceding personal tragedy has actually established?
