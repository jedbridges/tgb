## Overview

About one hundred and ninety pages, an essay published in 1920 by an English economic historian and Christian socialist, expanded from a Fabian pamphlet. Tawney argues that modern industrial society is acquisitive, organised around rights to property and income without regard to the functions they serve, and that this makes it both unjust and unstable.

He proposes instead a functional society in which industry is treated as a profession, ownership is tied to service, and the standard of conduct is the purpose an activity serves rather than the wealth it yields. Chapters treat property, the nostrum of productivity, the vice of the industrial system and the position of the brain worker.

## What happens

**Diagnosing the acquisitive society.** Tawney opens by naming the condition of modern industrial society as acquisitive, organised around rights to property and income held without regard to any function those rights serve.

**Arguing that it is unjust and unstable.** He argues that such an arrangement is both unjust, because it rewards ownership rather than service, and unstable, because a society organised around competing claims to wealth has no principle that can hold it together.

**Examining property.** The chapter on property distinguishes ownership that serves a function, the tools of a craftsman or the land a farmer works, from ownership that merely extracts income, and argues that only the first deserves the protection the second currently enjoys.

**Rejecting the nostrum of productivity.** Tawney rejects the claim that the cure for industrial discontent is simply greater output, arguing that producing more within an unjust arrangement does not make the arrangement just.

**Naming the vice of the industrial system.** He identifies the system's vice as the severance of work from purpose, so that industry is conducted for the wealth it yields rather than the service it renders.

**Proposing a functional society.** Tawney proposes that industry be treated as a profession, ownership tied to service, and conduct measured by the purpose an activity serves, and considers the position of the brain worker within such an order.

## How to read it

Read the word acquisitive as the book's central instrument, a single diagnostic term that reframes the whole economic order as a moral condition rather than a natural fact to be accepted.

Pay close attention to how the argument that acquisitive society is both unjust and unstable ties the ethical complaint to a practical one, so that the case does not rest on sentiment alone.

Notice how the proposal of a functional society, with industry as a profession and ownership tied to service, offers a positive alternative rather than mere denunciation, and ask whether the professional analogy holds.

Consider how the insistence that conduct be judged by the purpose an activity serves rather than the wealth it yields carries a Christian socialist conviction into economic argument without ever becoming a sermon.

Read this essay alongside The Wealth of Nations to set Tawney's test of function against Smith's account of self-interest coordinated by the market, the two books disagreeing about what an economy is for.

## Questions it raises

- Does the distinction between property that serves a function and property that merely extracts income hold up in practice, or does every form of ownership shade into both at once?
- Is Tawney right that an acquisitive society is unstable as well as unjust, or has the century since 1920 shown that such a society can hold together indefinitely?
- Can industry really be treated as a profession, answerable to purpose in the way medicine or law claim to be, or does the analogy break down once production is organised on a large scale?
- How much of the argument depends on Tawney's Christian socialism, and how much survives for a reader who does not share his faith?
- What does the book's rejection of productivity as a nostrum say to later economies that have measured their success chiefly by growth?
- Where does the brain worker, the manager or professional, stand in a functional society, and does Tawney's treatment of that position anticipate or evade the rise of a managerial class?
