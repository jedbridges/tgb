## Overview

About two hundred and eighty pages in three books, written in French prose by Christine de Pizan and completed in 1405, roughly a decade into a career that had already made her one of the very few women in medieval Europe to earn her living as a professional writer, supporting herself and her children after being widowed young by producing poetry and prose for aristocratic patrons at the French court. The book was composed in direct response to what Christine describes, in its opening pages, as a genuine personal crisis of confidence brought on by immersion in the misogynist tradition running through medieval literature, most pointedly Jean de Meun's widely read continuation of the Romance of the Rose, which portrayed women as fundamentally deceitful and morally weak, a portrayal Christine had absorbed to the point of briefly doubting her own worth and that of every woman she knew, before deciding to answer it directly and systematically.

The book arrives in the middle of what later scholars have named the querelle des femmes, the quarrel over women, an extended literary debate running through late medieval and early modern French letters over women's moral and intellectual capacities, and Christine's contribution is distinctive for answering her opponents on their own scholarly terms, citing named authorities, marshaling historical precedent, and building a sustained counter-argument rather than simply asserting an alternative view.

## The argument

**The crisis and the three ladies' arrival.** The book opens with Christine, alone in her study, having just set aside a misogynist text and fallen into genuine despair at the seeming unanimity of learned male opinion against women, wondering whether the moral failings these authors attribute to women might simply be true, since so many respected writers agree on them. Three crowned allegorical women then appear to her: Reason, Rectitude and Justice, who identify the despair itself as evidence of exactly the kind of unexamined deference to authority Christine should be questioning rather than accepting, and who propose to help her build a fortified city, populated entirely by worthy women drawn from history, as both a literal architectural allegory and a systematic rebuttal.

**Reason and the city's foundations, Book I.** Lady Reason takes the lead in the book's first section, laying the city's foundations with women demonstrating intellectual and practical capability: learned women, skilled artisans, and above all women warriors and rulers, including the Amazons, whom Christine presents not as mythical curiosities but as historically credible evidence that women are capable of the courage and strategic capacity misogynist writers insist belongs to men alone. Reason's consistent method, which governs the entire book, is to answer each general slander against women with specific, named historical counter-examples, treating the accumulation of documented exceptions as itself a refutation of the supposed rule.

**Rectitude and the city's walls, Book II.** Lady Rectitude continues the construction with women demonstrating moral rectitude specifically, good wives, faithful daughters, and rulers whose governance is presented as evidence that women in positions of real power have historically governed as well as or better than their male counterparts, citing Semiramis, who is credited with ruling and expanding ancient Babylon after her husband's death, among other historical and legendary queens. Christine uses these accounts to press a further, pointed argument: that the specific vices misogynist writers attribute to women, deceitfulness, inconstancy, moral weakness, are frequently vices Christine finds at least as prevalent, if not more so, among the men who wrote the accusations.

**Justice and the highest towers, Book III.** Lady Justice completes the city in its final section, populating its highest and most honored towers with female saints and martyrs, women whose faith and constancy under persecution Christine presents as the culmination of the qualities Reason and Rectitude had already demonstrated in more worldly form. The book closes with Justice leading the Virgin Mary herself into the completed city as its queen, and with Christine stepping briefly outside the allegorical frame to address her women readers directly, urging them to defend the city, meaning to live by the virtues it embodies, against the ongoing attacks she expects the misogynist literary tradition to continue making.

## How to read it

Read the book as a direct, point-by-point rebuttal rather than as free-standing celebration of admirable women; Christine is consistently answering specific claims from specific, named authors, and the force of her argument depends on recognizing what particular accusation each historical example is being marshaled against.

Notice how carefully Christine distinguishes her method from mere assertion. Reason's stated approach, proving claims by manifest deduction and demonstrable historical example, mimics the scholastic argumentative techniques used by the male authors Christine is answering, a deliberate choice to defeat the misogynist tradition using its own accepted intellectual tools rather than simply rejecting its methods along with its conclusions.

Pay attention to the allegory's literal architecture. Christine is genuinely building something, laying foundations, raising walls, adding towers, and the sustained, structural metaphor of a fortified city, defensible and permanent, is doing real argumentative work: women's worth, once properly documented, becomes a fortress rather than a claim that has to be defended anew in every generation.

Read the book with some awareness of its immediate context in the querelle des femmes, a genuine, ongoing literary debate rather than a settled historical question Christine was addressing from a position of security; she was writing as an active participant in a live and, to her contemporaries, genuinely contested argument.

Consider reading this alongside a misogynist text Christine is directly answering, such as sections of Jean de Meun's continuation of the Romance of the Rose, to see her specific counter-arguments land against the claims that actually provoked them, rather than encountering her defense in isolation from what it was defending against.

## Questions it raises

- Does answering misogynist claims with historical counter-examples, Semiramis, the Amazons, learned women who excelled once educated, ultimately concede too much ground by accepting the terms of the original debate, or is meeting opponents on their own evidentiary standards the only effective strategy available to Christine?
- What is the effect of building the defense as a literal city, with foundations, walls and towers, rather than as a straightforward treatise? Does the architectural allegory add genuine argumentative force, or is it primarily a memorable organizing device?
- Christine argues repeatedly that women's apparent intellectual limitations reflect exclusion from education rather than natural incapacity. How does this argument compare to later, more developed versions of the same claim in writers like Wollstonecraft, writing roughly four centuries later?
- Is there a tension between Christine's individual examples, largely exceptional women, queens, warriors, saints, and a broader claim about women's capacities in general? Does citing exceptional cases risk implying that ordinary women remain as the misogynist tradition describes them?
- What does it mean that Christine frames her project explicitly as a matter of justice, personified as one of the three ladies who help her build the city, rather than simply as historical correction or moral argument?
- How does Christine's position as a professional woman writer, supporting herself and her children through her work, shape the urgency and the specific arguments of a book defending women's capacity for serious intellectual and practical achievement?
