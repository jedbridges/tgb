## Overview

About ten pages, the manifesto of the peasants of Upper Swabia, printed at Memmingen in March 1525 during the German Peasants' War. Drafted by the furrier Sebastian Lotzer with the preacher Christoph Schappeler, it demands the right of each community to choose its pastor, the abolition of serfdom and the small tithe.

The manifesto also demands free access to game, fish and forest, an end to arbitrary rents and punishments, and the return of common lands. Every article is justified from Scripture, with an offer to withdraw any shown to be against God's Word. Luther answered it in his Admonition to Peace.

## What happens

**Demanding pastor selection.** The manifesto opens by demanding the right of each community to choose its own pastor, giving ordinary peasants genuine institutional voice within religious governance previously reserved for ecclesiastical hierarchy.

**Abolishing serfdom and the small tithe.** The peasants demand the abolition of serfdom and the small tithe, targeting two of feudal obligation's most burdensome specific institutional mechanisms.

**Free access to game, fish and forest.** The manifesto demands free access to game, fish and forest, resources feudal law had restricted to lords despite their practical importance to ordinary peasant subsistence.

**Ending arbitrary rents and punishments.** The peasants demand an end to arbitrary rents and punishments, targeting the unpredictable exercise of lordly power that left them without genuine legal security or protection.

**Returning common lands.** The manifesto demands the return of common lands, addressing enclosure's specific economic harm to peasant communities that had previously relied on shared access to these resources.

**Scriptural justification throughout.** Every article is justified from Scripture, with an explicit offer to withdraw any article shown to be against God's Word, grounding these material demands in specifically religious authority.

## How to read it

Read the demand for the right of each community to choose its own pastor as giving ordinary peasants genuine institutional voice within religious governance, a considerable claim that local congregational authority should override established ecclesiastical hierarchy.

Pay close attention to how justifying every article from Scripture, with an explicit offer to withdraw any shown to be against God's Word, grounds economic and social grievance in specifically religious authority rather than leaving demands as purely secular claim.

Notice how the specific demands, abolishing serfdom and the small tithe, securing free access to game, fish and forest, ending arbitrary rents, catalogue feudal obligation's actual daily burden with unusual concrete specificity.

Consider how Luther's own direct answer in his Admonition to Peace demonstrates how seriously contemporary religious authority took this peasant manifesto, Reformation-era controversy proving directly entangled with genuine economic and social upheaval.

Read this manifesto alongside Luther's own Admonition to Peace to see directly how the Reformation's most prominent theological voice responded to peasant demands grounded explicitly in the same scriptural authority his own broader movement had helped unleash.

- How does the demand for community pastor selection relate to the manifesto's other, more directly economic demands? Does this religious governance claim reflect genuine spiritual concern, or does it function primarily as leverage for the peasants' broader material grievances?
- What does the specific catalogue of grievances, serfdom, tithes, restricted access to game and forest, arbitrary rents, reveal about feudal obligation's actual daily texture for ordinary peasants? Does this concrete specificity make the manifesto more persuasive than abstract complaint would be?
- How should a reader weigh the manifesto's explicit offer to withdraw any article shown to be against God's Word? Does this concession represent genuine openness to correction, or strategic rhetorical positioning designed to appear reasonable to potential opponents?
- Given that Luther felt compelled to answer this manifesto directly, what does his response suggest about the relationship between his own Reformation theology and the broader social and economic upheaval it helped inspire among ordinary peasants?
- What does this manifesto's continued historical significance suggest about the relationship between religious reform movements and social and economic grievance more broadly?
