## Overview

About three hundred pages, a collection of Old Norse poems preserved mainly in the Icelandic Codex Regius, written down around 1270 but containing verse composed centuries earlier. The mythological poems include the Völuspá, in which a seeress recounts the creation of the world and foretells its destruction at Ragnarök.

The Hávamál collects sayings attributed to Odin, alongside comic and grim tales of Thor, Loki and the giants. The heroic poems tell the story of Sigurd, the dragon Fafnir, Brynhild and Gudrun, the same legend told in prose in the Völsunga saga and in German in the Nibelungenlied.

## What happens

**The Völuspá's vision.** A seeress delivers the Völuspá, recounting the creation of the world from its earliest primordial state and foretelling its eventual destruction at Ragnarök within a single sweeping mythological vision.

**The Hávamál's practical wisdom.** The Hávamál collects sayings attributed to Odin himself, blending everyday practical wisdom about hospitality, friendship and caution with the god's own mythic experience and self-sacrifice.

**Tales of Thor, Loki and the giants.** The mythological section continues with comic and grim tales of Thor's exploits, Loki's trickery, and various encounters with giants, ranging in tone from broadly humorous to genuinely ominous.

**Sigurd and the dragon Fafnir.** The heroic poems begin the story of Sigurd, who kills the dragon Fafnir and gains access to a hoard of treasure, launching the legendary hero on the path that will eventually lead to his own tragic fate.

**Brynhild and the broken vow.** The heroic poems continue with Brynhild, whose relationship with Sigurd becomes entangled in deception and broken vows, setting in motion the tragic events that will consume the legend's central figures.

**Gudrun's grief and vengeance.** The heroic poems conclude by following Gudrun through grief and eventual vengeance, completing a legendary cycle preserved here in compressed Old Norse verse distinct from its prose and German verse counterparts.

## How to read it

Read the Völuspá's structure, a seeress recounting both creation and Ragnarök within a single continuous vision, as giving Norse mythology a genuinely comprehensive cosmic frame running from absolute beginning through prophesied end that shapes the collection's other poems.

Pay close attention to how the Hávamál blends everyday worldly wisdom directly alongside Odin's own mythic self-sacrifice, refusing any clean separation between ordinary practical advice and the highest cosmic religious knowledge within a single collected poem.

Notice how the heroic poems telling Sigurd, Fafnir, Brynhild and Gudrun's story preserve, in compressed and often fragmentary verse, the same legend told at greater prose length in the Völsunga saga and transformed considerably in the German Nibelungenlied.

Consider the collection's written form, fixed around 1270 but preserving verse composed centuries earlier in oral tradition, as offering rare textual access to pre-Christian Norse belief systems that would otherwise have been lost to later conversion and cultural transformation.

Read this collection alongside the Nibelungenlied to compare how the same underlying heroic legend, Sigurd or Siegfried, the dragon's treasure, Brynhild or Brünhild's betrayal, developed into genuinely distinct narrative traditions across Norse and German literary cultures.

## Questions it raises

- Does the Völuspá's comprehensive vision, spanning creation through Ragnarök, successfully unify the collection's more varied and fragmentary individual poems, or does this cosmic frame sit somewhat separately from the more localized concerns of poems like the Hávamál?
- How does the Hávamál's blending of practical everyday wisdom with mythic religious content shape your understanding of how Norse culture related ordinary conduct to cosmic and divine concerns? Does this combination suggest a worldview without sharp separation between sacred and mundane?
- What does comparing the heroic poems here to their prose retelling in the Völsunga saga and their transformation in the German Nibelungenlied reveal about how medieval literary traditions reshaped shared narrative material for different audiences and purposes?
- How should a reader weigh this collection's written form, fixed around 1270, against the centuries of oral transmission that preceded it? Does written fixation preserve or inevitably alter material originally composed and transmitted through purely oral tradition?
- Given that Ragnarök foretells the gods' own destruction, what does this specific mythological detail suggest about Norse religious thought's relationship to fate and mortality, even for divine beings who might otherwise seem exempt from such cosmic limitation?
- What does this collection's survival, offering rare access to pre-Christian Norse belief that might otherwise have been lost entirely, suggest about the fragility and contingency of how any culture's mythological and religious heritage actually gets preserved across historical transformation?
