## Overview

A forty-page ethnographic essay, written in 98 AD the same year as the Agricola, on the peoples beyond the Rhine and Danube. The first half describes the Germans in general, their land, warfare, kings and assemblies, religion, marriage customs and love of drinking and gambling; the second surveys the individual tribes from the Rhine to the Baltic.

Tacitus uses German simplicity and chastity as a mirror for Roman decadence, and the work has had a long and troubling afterlife in German nationalism.

## What happens

**Describing the German land.** Tacitus opens by describing the land the Germans inhabit, setting the physical and climatic terms for the ethnographic account that follows.

**Detailing warfare and the comitatus.** He details German warfare and the bond between warriors and their chief, the martial customs that most struck Roman observers as distinct from their own military organization.

**Surveying kings and assemblies.** The essay surveys German kings and tribal assemblies, documenting forms of authority and collective decision that Tacitus implicitly compares with Rome's own imperial arrangements.

**Recording religion and marriage customs.** Tacitus records German religious practice and marriage customs, presenting the tribes' domestic and sacred life as evidence of a simplicity Rome had lost.

**Noting drinking and gambling.** He notes the Germans' love of drinking and gambling, complicating the portrait of noble simplicity with recognizably human vice.

**Surveying individual tribes to the Baltic.** The second half surveys the individual tribes from the Rhine to the Baltic, naming and locating peoples whose own accounts of themselves do not otherwise survive.

## How to read it

Read the essay's general description of German land, warfare, kings and assemblies, religion and marriage customs as assembling the fullest surviving ancient ethnographic portrait of the peoples beyond the Rhine and Danube.

Pay close attention to how Tacitus uses German simplicity and chastity as a mirror for Roman decadence, turning what looks like objective ethnography into moral commentary directed at Rome itself.

Notice how the survey of individual tribes from the Rhine to the Baltic extends the general portrait into a documented gazetteer, naming peoples whose own voices do not otherwise reach us.

Consider how the work's long and troubling afterlife in German nationalism shows an ancient ethnographic text, written for specifically Roman purposes, conscripted centuries later into ideological projects its author never anticipated.

Read this essay alongside the Annals and Histories to compare Tacitus's ethnographic mirror for Roman decadence against his direct narrative history of the empire the Germans stood implicitly against.

## Questions it raises

- Does using German simplicity and chastity as a mirror for Roman decadence tell readers more about the actual Germans or about the moral anxieties of the Roman audience Tacitus was writing for?
- How reliable is an ethnographic account written by an author who never visited the region he describes, and who had clear rhetorical purposes beyond simple documentation? What should a reader weigh against Tacitus's testimony?
- What does the essay's survey of German kings and assemblies suggest about the range of political organization Romans recognized among peoples they considered barbarian? Does this complicate a simple civilized-versus-barbarian framework?
- Given the essay's long and troubling afterlife in German nationalism, how should a modern reader hold both the text's ancient context and its later, unintended ideological uses in mind simultaneously?
- Does noting the Germans' love of drinking and gambling alongside their supposed simplicity and chastity produce a coherent portrait, or does it reveal the essay pulling in two different rhetorical directions at once?
- How does surveying individual tribes from the Rhine to the Baltic, naming peoples whose own accounts do not survive, shape later readers' access to a history otherwise almost entirely unrecorded from any but the Roman side?
