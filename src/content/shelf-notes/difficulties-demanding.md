Two hundred and fifty-two works, the largest shelf on the site, and the level at which the great books earn their reputation. The Iliad, the Odyssey, the Aeneid, the Confessions, Leviathan, Herodotus, Thucydides, the Meditations of Descartes, the Wealth of Nations, Tocqueville: these are books that a careful reader can finish alone, and that a seminar, a lecture or a good introduction will make twice as good.

Demanding usually means one of two things. The book is long and its shape only appears at the end, as the Iliad's does when Priam comes to Achilles's tent, or as Middlemarch's does when the town's stories finally cross. Or the book is arguing at a level where each step is clear and the whole is hard to hold, as Descartes and Hume and Smith are. The tragedies of Shakespeare are here for a third reason: the language is four hundred years old and the plays were meant to be heard.

The programs spend most of their second and third years at this level. Read these with a plan: a book a week for the plays and short treatises, a month for the epics and the long histories, and a companion, a friend or a group or the guide on each page, for the ones that fight back.
