The Greeks had the word but not quite the idea: for them freedom was the condition of a citizen as against a slave, and it meant taking part in ruling, not being left alone. The Christian tradition turned it inward, so that a slave could be free in Christ and a king could be a slave to sin. Milton's Areopagitica, in 1644, is the first great argument for a freedom the state must not touch, the freedom to publish.

Hobbes then defined liberty as nothing but the absence of external impediments and gave almost all of it up for safety. Locke kept it and made it the reason governments exist. The Declaration of Independence and the Constitution are Locke turned into law. Rousseau said that a man could be forced to be free, and the Revolution tried it. Mill drew the line where most people still draw it: over yourself, sovereign; over others, answerable.

The shelf's last books ask who was left out. Frederick Douglass wrote what liberty looked like from inside slavery. Mill's Subjection of Women and Wollstonecraft's Vindication applied the argument to half the species. Marx said that political liberty without economic liberty was a fraud, and the twentieth century spent itself testing the claim.
