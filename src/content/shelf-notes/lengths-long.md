A hundred and twelve works, and among them the books the programs devote whole units to: the Iliad and the Odyssey, the Aeneid, Herodotus and Thucydides, the Republic, the Divine Comedy, the Canterbury Tales, Paradise Lost, Faust, Leviathan, the Origin of Species, and most of the great nineteenth-century novels short of the epics. These are the reading that a great books education is measured by, and a student who finishes the first year has read four or five of them.

The trick with a long book is pace. The epics were sung in sessions and divide naturally into books of a few hundred lines; two a night finishes the Odyssey in twelve. The Republic has ten books and each is a conversation. Dante wrote in cantos of about a hundred and forty lines, three or four to an evening. The novels were serialised, and their chapters are the instalments. Leviathan is the exception, a treatise with no natural pauses, and it is the hardest long book on the shelf for that reason.

Read one a month alongside shorter things, as the programs do. The long books are where the tradition builds its worlds, and a world takes a month to live in.
