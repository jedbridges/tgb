These are the sixty-six works on the lists that ask for attention and nothing else: no prior reading, no special vocabulary, no argument that has to be held across a hundred pages. Many of them are the books the programs assign in the first week for exactly that reason. The Apology and the Crito are Socrates speaking plainly to a jury and to a friend. The Enchiridion of Epictetus is a Stoic handbook in fifty numbered paragraphs. The Communist Manifesto is forty pages written to be read aloud in a workers' hall.

Approachable is not the same as slight. Pride and Prejudice is on this shelf and is one of the greatest novels in English; Candide is here and is one of the sharpest; the Declaration of Independence is two pages and changed the world. Animal Farm, Tartuffe, the Gospel of Luke, Bacon's New Atlantis, Lincoln's speeches, the Enchiridion, Walden: what they share is that the surface is clear, and whatever depth they have is reached by rereading rather than by struggle.

Start here if you are new to the lists, or if you are reading alone without a seminar. The shelf pairs well with the short books, which overlap it, and it is the best place to find something for a friend who has asked where to begin.
