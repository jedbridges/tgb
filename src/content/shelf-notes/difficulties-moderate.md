This is the largest difficulty shelf, two hundred and thirty-one works, and the level most of the programs pitch their first year at. The Prince, the Discourse on Inequality, Antigone, Oedipus, the Symposium, Locke's Second Treatise, the Constitution: each of these can be read in a sitting or two, and each rewards a second reading in which the argument turns out to be more than it seemed.

What makes a book moderate rather than approachable is usually one of three things. It assumes a story you may not know, as the tragedies assume the myths and Plutarch assumes Roman history. It makes an argument that has to be held in mind, as the Prince does when its advice contradicts itself on purpose. Or it comes from a world whose assumptions are not ours, as the Discourse does when it asks you to imagine humans before society. None of these is a barrier; each is a reason to read with a pencil.

Most of the novels are here, along with Herodotus, Plutarch, the Gospels, Descartes's Discourse, Mill, Freud's shorter works, and a great deal of the poetry. If the approachable shelf is where to begin, this one is where to spend the first year.
