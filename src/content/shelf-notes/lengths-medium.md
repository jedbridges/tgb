A hundred and sixty-two works, and the shelf on which the programs place their central texts: the Confessions, the Ethics, Lucretius, Pascal's Pensées, the Oresteia, Aristotle's Politics and Physics, Hamlet, Lear, Bacon's Novum Organum, Freud's Introductory Lectures, To the Lighthouse. This is the length at which a book can develop an argument or a world fully and still be read in a single week of evenings, which is the pace most seminars set.

The shelf mixes every form. The medium-length treatises, Aristotle above all, are the ones a student is expected to have read whole. The longer plays are here, along with the novellas and the shorter novels, Candide and Death in Venice and Heart of Darkness. Marcus Aurelius, Cicero's On Duties, the Song of Roland and Plutarch's individual lives are the ancient entries; Woolf, Du Bois and Camus the modern ones.

Plan on a week each, and on two for Aristotle and Augustine, whose last books are the densest. If the short shelf is where to begin, this one is where the great books live: long enough to build something, short enough to hold in mind at once.
