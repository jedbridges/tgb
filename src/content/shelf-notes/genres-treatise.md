The treatise is the form philosophy takes when it stops being a conversation and starts being a system, and Aristotle invented it: the surviving works are lecture notes, organised by subject, each one dividing its topic and working through the divisions. The Ethics, the Politics, the Physics, the Metaphysics and the Poetics are all on the shelf, and the Organon is the logic they run on.

The form ran through Aquinas, whose Summa is a treatise built from thousands of small debates, and into the moderns. Descartes, Hobbes, Spinoza, Locke, Hume and Kant each wrote the treatise that defined their philosophy, and the shelf holds them all. Machiavelli's Prince is a treatise on power, Smith's Wealth of Nations a treatise on markets, Clausewitz's On War a treatise on war, Darwin's Origin a treatise on life. Newton's Principia is the treatise as mathematics.

This is the largest form on the site, with two hundred works, and the hardest to read well, because a treatise has to be read as an argument and not as a story. The trick is to find the question the book is answering, usually in the first chapter, and hold it through every section. Start with Aristotle's Ethics or Locke's Second Treatise, which are the two most approachable.
